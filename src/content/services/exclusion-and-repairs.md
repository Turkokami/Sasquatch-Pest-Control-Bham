## The argument for doing this at all

Nearly every page on this site ends up pointing here, and it is worth saying why
plainly, even though it is the opposite of how pest control is usually sold.

A treatment manages a population. It reduces what is present, it has to be
repeated, and it is billed on a schedule. Exclusion removes the access. It is
bought once, it keeps working while it stays intact, and it does not care whether
the population outside your walls goes up or down.

For most of what we get called about in Whatcom County — rodents above all, but
also wasps in wall voids, spiders, and the overwintering insects that pile onto
south-facing walls every October — the entry point is the actual problem and the
insects are the symptom. Closing a foundation vent that has been open for
fifteen years does more for a rodent problem than any number of visits.

We say this knowing it argues against the recurring revenue that most of this
industry is built on. If you are choosing between a quarterly plan and sealing
your crawlspace, seal the crawlspace.

## A quarter of an inch

That is the number worth remembering. A house mouse gets through roughly a
quarter inch — about a pencil. Both species can also work a gap considerably
smaller than the body looks, because the skull is the limiting dimension and it
is flatter than people expect.

Rats are the part of this that gets muddled, so it is worth setting out
carefully. Every extension source we work from gives rats a larger opening than
mice: University of Nebraska Extension and eXtension put it at slightly more
than half an inch, WSU Extension gives the same half inch, and UC IPM agrees.
None of them offers a smaller adult figure, and any page that quotes one for a
"young" rat is inventing a distinction the literature does not draw.

What makes the quarter inch correct for rats anyway is a different claim,
and Washington's Department of Fish and Wildlife states it directly: a rat needs
only a quarter-inch hole to chew larger and squeeze through. That reframes the
whole question. The number that decides whether a building holds is not the
dimension the animal passes through unaided — it is the dimension at which it
can get its incisors around an edge and start removing material. Rodent incisors
grow continuously and are worn down by gnawing, so an edge is an opportunity.
Seal to half an inch and you have built to the animal's body. Seal to a quarter
and you have built to its teeth, which is the one that lasts.

This is not folklore. Bellingham Municipal Code chapter 6.15 requires rental
properties to pass a certified inspection, and the checklist explicitly flags
**openings in the foundation larger than a quarter inch** as an infestation
risk. The city wrote the number into its own rental standard.

If a code official is looking for it on a rental, it is a reasonable standard for
a house you live in.

## Where they actually get in

On Whatcom County housing stock the same short list accounts for most of what we
find. This is roughly the order we check.

**Foundation vents.** The single most common entry on older Bellingham homes.
Original screens rust through, get pushed in by weather or animals, or were
replaced at some point with mesh too coarse to matter. In the prewar
neighborhoods this is frequently the whole answer.

**Post-and-pier gaps.** A great deal of pre-1940 housing here sits on
post-and-pier, and eighty-odd years of settlement opens gaps between the
foundation and the framing above. Invisible from outside, obvious from inside the
crawlspace.

**Utility penetrations.** Water, gas, electrical, cable, and every line added
since the house was built. Almost nobody seals these, and the contractor who ran
a new line in 2019 had no reason to think about a rat. The gap around the pipe is
the issue, not the pipe.

**Garage door corners.** The bottom corners of a sectional door essentially never
seal, and the garage is usually connected to the rest of the structure.

**Soffit and roofline.** Where a roof meets a wall at a change of pitch there is
often a genuine hole. Combine that with tree or fence contact and you get animals
in the attic rather than the crawlspace.

**Dryer and bath vents.** Plastic louvered covers break, and the duct behind them
is a sheltered highway straight into the building.

**Crawlspace access doors.** Warped, propped open, or missing entirely.

**Siding at grade.** Where siding meets or disappears below soil and bark mulch,
which is both an entry point and a moisture problem feeding
[carpenter ants and dampwood termites](/services/termite-control/).

**Deck ledgers and rim joists.** The junction is frequently unsealed, and on
older decks the flashing is either failing or was never there.

## Materials, which is where most exclusion fails

The difference between exclusion that lasts a decade and exclusion that lasts a
season is almost entirely material choice. This is the part homeowners and a fair
number of contractors get wrong.

**Hardware cloth**, at the right gauge and mesh, is the workhorse. Quarter-inch
mesh for rodents, finer where insects matter. Cut oversize, fastened properly to
sound substrate, not stapled loosely over a hole.

**Sheet metal** where something will be chewed at directly — corners, edges,
door bottoms, anywhere a rodent gets purchase.

**Mortar and appropriate patching compound** for masonry and concrete gaps.

**Copper mesh** as a packing material behind a sealant, because it does not rust
out the way steel wool does in this climate.

Mesh size is a real trade-off rather than a detail. Quarter-inch hardware cloth
stops rodents and passes airflow, but it is an open door to a wasp queen or a
cluster fly, so on a vent that has to do both jobs we back the rodent mesh with a
finer insect screen and accept the slight loss of ventilation.

What does not work on its own:

**Expanding foam.** A rat chews through cured foam in an evening. It has a
legitimate role as an air seal behind a physical barrier, and no role as the
barrier.

**Steel wool alone.** Rusts out, then leaves both a hole and a stain.

**Caulk.** Fine for insects at a tight joint, irrelevant to anything with teeth.

**Screen door mesh or window screen** over a rodent-sized opening. It is an
insect material being asked to do a rodent job.

The other half of durability is fastening. Correct material fixed badly is a flap
rather than a barrier, and this is where most well-intentioned DIY work comes
apart — usually at the top edge, usually within a year.

## The crawlspace

On the housing stock in this county, the crawlspace is where most of this work
happens and where most of the value is.

**Vapor barrier.** A functioning ground barrier stops soil moisture evaporating
into the framing above. Its absence is the root cause behind
[moisture ants](/services/ant-control/), dampwood termites and anobiid beetles,
and it is one of the cheapest interventions available.

**Vent screening.** Two problems solved at once — airflow that actually works,
and a closed door to rodents.

**Access door.** A door that seals, latches and can still be opened by someone
who needs to get in.

**Drainage and standing water.** Sometimes ours to address, sometimes a
groundwater or downspout problem that belongs to another trade. We will say
which.

**Insulation and debris.** Contaminated or fallen insulation holds moisture
against joists and hosts secondary pests. Where that has gone far enough, the job
becomes [crawlspace cleanout and restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/).

## One job, several problems solved

This is the part that makes exclusion good value rather than merely effective.

The same work reduces **rodents**, because it closes their entries. It reduces
**wasps in wall voids**, because it closes the siding gaps a queen uses in spring
— and it does so safely, because sealing happens after a colony is dead rather
than while it is alive. It reduces **spiders**, by cutting both their access and
the insect prey coming through the same gaps. It reduces **overwintering
insects** — cluster flies, boxelder bugs, conifer seed bugs — which are looking
for exactly the small gaps in south-facing walls that exclusion closes. And it
reduces **moisture-driven insects** by improving the conditions rather than
treating the symptom.

It also does things that are not pest control at all. A sealed, screened,
barriered crawlspace is a drier crawlspace, which means less decay, better
insulation performance and lower heating loss.

## What we do not do

We are not general contractors and we will not pretend otherwise to keep a job.

We do pest-related repair: vent screening and replacement, crawlspace access
doors, sealing penetrations, damaged soffit and trim where it is the entry point,
vapor barrier, insulation removal and replacement, and the exclusion materials
described above.

We do not do roofing, siding replacement, structural framing repair, plumbing or
drainage excavation. When the honest fix is one of those, we say so, describe it
clearly enough that you can get a sensible quote, and where we can we will tell
you who does that work well locally.

That distinction matters most when there is decay. Wood that has lost structural
section needs replacing, and screening over it is cosmetic. We would rather lose
the sealing work than seal a rotten sill plate and call it done.

## Rentals and property management

Because Bellingham's rental standard is explicit about the quarter-inch
threshold, exclusion is a compliance item here rather than just good practice.

For an owner or manager, the sensible pattern is exclusion at turnover, when the
unit is empty and access is easy. It is cheaper then than during occupancy, it
avoids scheduling around a tenant, and it puts the work in before the next
tenancy rather than during a complaint.

For multifamily buildings, shared walls and utility chases mean the building is
the unit of work, not the apartment. Sealing one unit's penetrations while the
chase above it stays open moves the problem rather than solving it.

Washington's landlord-tenant law, RCW 59.18.060, generally places responsibility
for pest control on the landlord in multi-unit buildings, with a carve-out for
single-family residences and for infestations caused by the tenant. Where the
answer depends on which side of that you are on, we will say so plainly rather
than billing whoever called.

## Timing

**August and early September is the window.** Rodents move indoors as the ground
saturates through late September and October, so sealing before the push means
sealing an empty structure. Sealing in November means sealing animals in, which
is why at that point exclusion has to follow trapping rather than lead it.

**Spring is second best.** Overwintering insects have left, wasp queens have not
yet established, and there is time before summer.

**After any trade has been on the property.** A new cable run, a replaced dryer
vent, a plumbing repair or a solar install reopens the envelope. It is worth a
look after work of that kind, because a carefully sealed house can be reopened by
one afternoon of somebody else's drilling.

## Around the county

**Pre-1940 city stock.** Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York and Roosevelt.
Post-and-pier, failing vent screens, unsealed penetrations and siding close to
grade. The highest-yield exclusion work in the county, and often the whole
solution to a rodent problem.

**The newer hillside developments.** Foundations up here are generally sound, so
the work moves upward — soffit returns, the junctions where a roofline meets a
wall at a change of pitch, and wherever landscaping has grown into contact with
the siding. Different job, same principle.

**Around Lake Whatcom** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor. Cedar
construction, decks and ledger boards against a forest edge. Exclusion here is
usually combined with moisture correction.

**Rural and agricultural** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Outbuildings,
equipment sheds and open structures, where the work is closing bays and feed
storage as much as houses.

**Waterfront** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Salt air degrades
fasteners and flashing faster, and seasonal homes stand empty through the wet
months with nobody to notice a new gap.

## What a job actually looks like

**The walk.** Someone goes around the outside of the building slowly, then gets
into the crawlspace and the attic. That last part is not optional — the entries
and the evidence are inside those spaces, and a quote produced from a walk around
the outside is a guess. We come out of it with a mapped list of openings, their
sizes, what is behind each one, and the condition of the substrate around them.

**The conversation.** You get that list before any work happens, including the
items that are not ours. If a downspout is dumping against the foundation or the
siding has gone soft at grade, that goes on the list too, marked as somebody
else's trade.

**Sequencing.** If there is an active population inside, exclusion does not go
first. Sealing a building with rodents in it produces animals that cannot get out
and eventually die somewhere inaccessible. The order is trap, confirm quiet, then
seal. On an empty structure — a vacant rental at turnover, a seasonal home before
the wet months — we can seal straight away.

**The work.** Materials cut to fit and fastened to sound substrate, working from
the ground up. Most of the labor is access rather than installation: getting to
a rim joist behind stored belongings, or working an eighteen-inch crawlspace on
your back.

**The walk-through afterward.** We show you what was closed and with what. On a
crawlspace job that usually means photographs, because you are not going to crawl
under there to check and you should not have to take it on faith.

## Why exclusion is undersold in this industry

Worth naming, because it explains why you may not have been offered it before.

Exclusion is hard work. It happens in crawlspaces and on ladders, it takes hours
rather than minutes, it requires materials and tools rather than a tank, and it
cannot be done by a technician moving through eight stops in a day. A recurring
treatment route is a far more comfortable business to run — predictable revenue,
short visits, no confined spaces.

It also does something a treatment plan does not: it reduces the customer's need
for future visits. A company optimizing for recurring revenue has a structural
reason not to lead with it.

We would rather sell the thing that works and be the company you call for the
next house. That is the whole argument, and it is why this page exists on a pest
control site at all.

## Vacant and seasonal properties

A specific case worth calling out, because it is common around Birch Bay, Blaine,
Semiahmoo and the lake.

A house standing empty through a Whatcom County winter has nobody to notice the
first sign of anything. Nobody hears the scratching, nobody sees the droppings
accumulate, nobody spots the vent screen that came loose in a storm in November.
By the time an owner arrives in April, a small entry has been a large problem for
four months, and it frequently involves contaminated insulation rather than just
animals.

For those properties the sensible pattern is to seal the envelope in early autumn
before the property closes up for the season, rather than reacting in spring.
It is dramatically cheaper, and it means arriving to a house rather than to a
cleanup.

Quoted from the walk-through, because the range is genuinely wide and depends on
access more than anything else.

A handful of foundation vents on a single-story house with a workable crawlspace
is a contained job. A prewar house with forty feet of failed screening, an

The honest framing is this: exclusion is usually a larger single number than a

rodent problem it is almost always the better purchase. We will tell you where
