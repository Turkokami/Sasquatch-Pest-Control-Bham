## Verification first, and why we charge for it

Most pest companies will book a bed bug treatment off a phone call. We will not,
and the reason is that a meaningful share of the calls we get about bed bugs are
not bed bugs.

The $150 verification visit exists to answer one question before you spend
anything on treatment: is this actually what you think it is? A technician
inspects the sleeping areas and the harborage points properly — seams, box
spring, headboard, frame joints, baseboard, outlet plates, adjacent furniture —
and finds either live insects, cast skins, eggs, or fecal spotting, or does not.

**The $150 is credited toward treatment if you go ahead.** So if we find bed
bugs and you decide to treat, the visit costs you nothing — you have simply
front-loaded part of the treatment price in exchange for certainty. If we do not
find them, you have paid $150 to avoid paying for a treatment you did not need,
and you have a straight answer about what to look at instead.

That is also why bed bugs are the one service where we do not offer a free
visit. Everything else we do starts with a free inspection. A bed bug search is
a different piece of work — it takes real time, it involves dismantling a bed,
and a meaningful share of them find nothing at all. Charging for it and crediting
it back is what lets us do it properly rather than quickly.

## Why bites are the worst evidence

This is the single most useful thing on this page, so it goes near the top.

Bite reactions are unreliable in both directions. The figure worth knowing comes
from the University of Kentucky's ENTFACT-636 on bed bugs, which reports that
about thirty percent of people show no reaction even when they are bitten
repeatedly, and that the proportion of non-reactors is higher again among older
adults. An absence of bites therefore proves nothing — plenty of infestations
are found by someone who has been sleeping in the room for months without a
mark, and in a care setting the resident least likely to complain of bites can
be the one with the worst harborage under the bed. And in the other direction, a
great many things produce small itchy welts in a row: fleas, mites, an allergic
reaction, a skin condition, or simply a mosquito that got in.

Reaction timing varies from immediate to more than a week, which also makes
"where was I when I got bitten" close to useless as a diagnostic.

So we do not diagnose from bites. We diagnose from the insects, their eggs,
their cast skins and their fecal spotting, because those are physical evidence
that either exists or does not.

## What to actually look for

**The insects.** Adults are about the size and shape of an apple seed, flat,
reddish-brown, and noticeably rounder and darker after feeding. Immatures are
smaller and much paler — nearly translucent when unfed, which is why people miss
them.

**Fecal spotting.** Small dark spots that bleed into fabric like ink on blotting
paper, because it is digested blood rather than a solid. Concentrated where the
insects rest, so look at the seams and folds rather than the open surface of a
sheet.

**Cast skins.** Bed bugs molt five times, leaving translucent shells behind.
Finding exuviae means a population has been breeding there, not that one hitched
a ride.

**Eggs.** About a millimeter, pearly white, cemented into cracks and seams and
genuinely difficult to see. They are also the reason a single treatment is rarely
enough.

**Where to look, in order.** Mattress seams and tufts. The box spring, including
underneath the dust cover. The bed frame joints, especially where wood meets
wood. The headboard, particularly if it is fixed to the wall. Then outward — the
nightstand, the baseboard behind the bed, outlet and switch plates, the edge of
carpet, and any upholstered furniture someone sleeps in.

Bed bugs stay close to where people sleep. An infestation is usually a bedroom
problem, not a house problem, and that is good news for the cost.

## What gets mistaken for them

**Carpet beetle larvae** are the most common false alarm — small, fuzzy, found
in bedding, and they cause skin irritation in some people that looks like bites.

**Bat bugs** are a genuine possibility in older Bellingham housing with attic bat
activity, and they are close relatives that look extremely similar. The
distinction matters, because the real treatment is getting the colony out, which is a specialist's job and not ours. Treating the bedroom while a colony sits above the ceiling solves nothing.

**Spider beetles**, **booklice** and **fleas** round out the list. Fleas in
particular get confused with bed bugs regularly, and the treatment is completely
different.

This is what the verification visit is for.

## How they get in

Bed bugs are a **travel and furniture** problem. They do not come up from the
yard, they are not attracted by food waste, and they have nothing to do with
housekeeping. They arrive in luggage, in a backpack, on secondhand furniture, on
a guest, or through a shared wall.

We say this plainly because the shame around this pest is genuinely costly.
People wait weeks before calling because they think an infestation says something
about them, and in those weeks a small localized problem becomes a large
dispersed one. Bed bugs turn up in immaculate homes and in good hotels
constantly. It is a logistics problem, not a moral one.

## The Bellingham pattern

Two things about this market are worth knowing.

**The student rental belt.** Sehome, Happy Valley, the Western campus edge and
the multifamily stock around them run on an academic calendar, which means an
enormous amount of furniture and belongings move between units every June and
September. That turnover is exactly how bed bugs travel, and it produces a
reliable seasonal pattern that a landlord or property manager can plan around.
Secondhand furniture moving between student houses is the most common vector we
see in this county.

**The January spike.** Holiday travel returns people to Whatcom County with
luggage that has been in hotels, airports and other people's houses, and we see
the resulting calls through January and into February. If you have traveled over
the holidays, the luggage protocol at the bottom of this page is worth ten
minutes.

Beyond those two, the ordinary sources apply — short-term rentals, hospitality
work, healthcare and senior living facilities, and any household with regular
overnight guests.

## Preparation is most of the job

This is where bed bug treatment succeeds or fails, and it is the part
homeowners are least warned about.

A treatment applied to an unprepared room is close to wasted. Clutter gives the
insects harborage we cannot reach. Bedding that has not been laundered and dried
on high heat re-seeds the room. Furniture pushed tight against walls hides the
exact junction we most need access to. Belongings piled on the floor turn a
two-hour job into an impossible one.

So we give you a written preparation list before the visit, and — this is the
part that matters — **if the room is not ready, we reschedule rather than
treat.** That occasionally frustrates people, and it is the right call every
time. Treating an unprepared room means taking money for work that will not
hold, and then having the same conversation in three weeks.

Our guarantee reflects this: the customer cooperation clause on our
[guarantee page](/our-guarantee/) exists precisely because re-service depends on
preparation having been done.

## What not to do

**Do not throw out the mattress or furniture.** It is the most expensive
instinct people have. Dragging an infested mattress down a hallway and a
stairwell distributes the problem across the building, and you will have bought a
replacement into a room that is still infested. Encasements after treatment cost
a fraction of new furniture and let you keep what you have.

**Do not use a fogger or bug bomb.** Total-release aerosols do not penetrate the
cracks bed bugs actually live in, and the repellent effect drives them further
into wall voids and into adjacent rooms — or in an apartment, adjacent units.
Foggers reliably make the job bigger and more expensive.

**Do not move to the couch or the spare room.** It is the most natural response
and it is how a bedroom problem becomes a whole-house problem. Bed bugs follow
carbon dioxide and body heat. Where you sleep is where they go.

**Do not start treating with store-bought sprays before we inspect.** Same
reasoning as elsewhere in this trade — scattered insects are harder to find, and
the evidence we need to confirm the infestation gets disturbed.

## Apartments, HOAs and rentals

In multifamily buildings the unit boundary is a legal concept, not a physical
one. Bed bugs move between apartments through wall voids, shared plumbing runs
and electrical chases, and the single most common reason a multifamily bed bug
job fails is treating the complaining unit while the ones on either side and
above and below go untouched.

For a serious multifamily infestation the honest scope includes inspecting the
adjacent units, and sometimes treating them. That is a larger number up front and
a much smaller one over the following year.

**On who pays:** Washington's landlord-tenant law, RCW 59.18.060, places
responsibility for pest control on the landlord, with a carve-out where the
dwelling is a single-family residence or where the infestation was caused by the
tenant. In a multi-unit building the obligation generally sits with the owner.
In a rented house it frequently does not. We work with tenants, owners and
property managers, and where the answer depends on which of those you are, we
will say so.

## Treatment, and what to expect afterward

We match the method to the situation rather than selling one approach for every
job — the room, the furniture, the extent and the building type all change what
makes sense. What is consistent is that treatment is targeted at harborage
rather than broadcast across surfaces, and that follow-up is planned from the
start.

**Expect more than one visit.** Eggs are well protected and do not respond the
way active insects do, so a second visit timed to hatching is part of the plan.
Seeing activity in the days after a treatment is normal and is not a sign of
failure — disturbed insects move more.

**Expect it to take a few weeks to go quiet.** Confirming success means several
checks with nothing found, not one quiet night.

## Are they dangerous?

Worth answering directly, because the anxiety around this pest is often worse
than the infestation.

Bed bugs are not known to transmit disease to people. What they cause is
localized skin reaction, and in some people a stronger allergic response.
Scratched bites can become secondarily infected, which is the most common actual
medical complication and an ordinary one.

The larger harm is usually sleep and stress. People who know there are insects
in their bed stop sleeping properly, and that has real consequences over weeks.
It is a legitimate reason to treat this as urgent even though nothing about it
is medically dangerous — and it is another reason we would rather verify quickly
than leave someone wondering for a fortnight.

If someone in the household is reacting badly to bites, that is a conversation
for a physician rather than for us. We handle the insects.

## Hospitality, short-term rentals and senior living

Commercial bed bug work runs on a different logic from residential, because the
cost of a missed unit is a review, a complaint or a regulatory conversation
rather than an inconvenience.

For hotels, motels and short-term rentals, the useful program is proactive
inspection on a cycle rather than reactive treatment on a complaint. Finding one
unit early is a room out of service for a couple of days; finding it after three
guests have complained is a reputation problem and frequently several adjoining
rooms. Housekeeping staff are the most effective detection system a property has
and they are rarely trained on what to look for, which is a gap worth closing.

Senior living and healthcare facilities add two constraints. Residents often
cannot prepare a room themselves, so preparation has to be built into the
service rather than handed to the occupant. And reduced sensation means bites
frequently go unnoticed, so infestations run longer before anyone reports them —
which makes scheduled inspection more valuable than in almost any other setting.

For all of these we document what was inspected, what was found and what was
done, because in those industries the record is part of the deliverable.

There is a lot of marketing in this trade built around a single technique, and

Heat, targeted application, encasement, monitoring and structural work each do
something the others do not, and the right combination depends on the room, the
furniture, the extent of the infestation and the building type. A single infested
bedroom in a detached house and a third-floor apartment with activity in two

What we commit to is telling you which approach we are proposing and why, before

method rather than the inspection is selling equipment utilization rather than a
result.

## If you travel

Ten minutes of habit prevents most of what we treat in January.

In the room, put luggage on the rack away from the bed rather than on the bed or
floor. Pull back the sheet at the head of the mattress and look at the seam and
the headboard edge for spotting before unpacking. On returning, unpack directly
into the laundry and run everything through a dryer on high heat — the dryer
does the work, not the wash. Keep the suitcase out of the bedroom, in a garage or
closet rather than under the bed.

And treat secondhand furniture as suspect until inspected, particularly
upholstered pieces and anything with a frame. Free furniture on a curb in a
student neighborhood in June is free for a reason more often than people think.

If you host guests regularly, the same logic applies in reverse and it is worth
knowing without being rude about it. Guest bedding washed and dried hot after a
stay, and a quick look at the seams of the guest bed a few days later, catches
almost everything that would otherwise establish quietly in a room nobody sleeps
in for weeks at a time. Infrequently used rooms are where infestations get the
longest head start.
