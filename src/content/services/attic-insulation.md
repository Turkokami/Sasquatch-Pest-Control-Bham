## Why this ends up being a pest page

Attic insulation replacement sits on a pest control site for a simple reason:
almost every one we do follows an animal.

Rodents tunnel through blown-in insulation and nest in it, and they are incontinent as they travel, so a run becomes a contaminated channel rather than a single soiled patch. Over a season that channel spreads across a surprising share of an attic, and compressed, soiled material has lost most of the R-value you are paying to heat against.

What all three leave behind is the same: material that has lost most of its
function, holds contamination and odor, and is sitting directly above the ceiling
of the rooms you live in.

## Contaminated, or just thin?

Two genuinely different jobs, and the honest distinction matters because one of
them costs several times the other.

**Contaminated insulation needs removing.** Rodent runs and nesting, urine
saturation, or material that has fallen off the framing and been lying
on the ceiling drywall. Adding new insulation over it seals the contamination in,
does nothing about the smell, and wastes good material on a bad substrate.

**Thin but clean insulation can be topped up.** If what is there is dry, intact,
uncontaminated and simply insufficient — which describes a great many older
Bellingham attics that have never been upgraded — adding to it is legitimate and
substantially cheaper.

We will tell you which one you have, with photographs. You are not going into the
attic to check and you should not have to take anyone's word for it.

## The order, again

Same rule as the [crawlspace](/services/crawlspace-restoration/), and it is worth
repeating because getting it wrong is expensive.

**Exclusion and trapping first**, confirmed quiet over several visits. **Then**
removal, treatment, air-sealing and re-insulation.

New insulation installed into an attic that still has an active population is
nesting material you have paid to provide. Any company willing to re-insulate
before the animals are confirmed gone is arranging to do the job twice.

Where the animal was not a rodent, that removal is somebody else's job before ours. We do not do bird or wildlife work, so the sequence there is their specialist first, our restoration second, and we will say so plainly rather than starting work over an unresolved problem.

## What the work involves

**Removal by vacuum.** Blown-in material is vacuumed into sealed bags rather than
handled loose, with the hose run out through a window, hatch or gable vent.
Batting is bagged in place. Access routes through the house are protected.

**Contamination and debris removal.** Nesting material, droppings, guano and
anything an animal has brought up there.

**Surface treatment.** Appropriate disinfection of the joists, ceiling deck and
framing where contamination was heaviest.

**Air-sealing.** Described below, and the step that most changes the outcome.

**Ventilation check.** Soffit vents clear rather than stuffed with old
insulation, baffles installed so new material cannot block airflow at the eaves,
and ridge or gable venting confirmed working.

**Exclusion confirmation.** Every roofline entry closed, because there is no
sense insulating a space that is still open. That is
[exclusion work](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) and it belongs in the same
project.

**Re-insulation** to current recommended levels, evenly distributed, with the
depth marked so it can be verified later.

## Air-sealing, and why it is skipped

This is the part worth understanding, because it separates a real job from a
quick one.

Insulation slows heat conduction. It does not stop air moving. Warm, moist air
from the living space rises and leaks into the attic through every penetration in
the ceiling — recessed light fixtures, bath fan housings, plumbing stacks, wiring
holes, the attic hatch, and the top plates of interior walls, which are usually
the single largest leak in the building and are invisible once insulation is
down.

Two consequences. The heat you paid for leaves. And the moisture it carries
condenses on cold roof sheathing, which over winters produces damp sheathing,
degraded insulation and eventually the moisture conditions that bring in the
[wood-destroying insects](/services/termite-control/) discussed elsewhere on this
site.

Blowing new insulation over unsealed penetrations buries the problem under
material that will then underperform for the life of the house. Sealing them
first costs a fraction of the insulation itself.

Most insulation contractors do not do it, because the attic is already open and
the incentive is to blow and go. It is the single highest-value hour in this job.

## What you get for it

**Comfort, quickly.** Most heat loss goes up, so upstairs rooms hold temperature
noticeably better. People report it within a week of cold weather.

**Lower heating cost**, for the same reason. On an older Bellingham home going
from badly under-insulated to current levels, this is real money annually rather
than a rounding error — though anyone quoting you an exact payback figure is
guessing at your habits.

**The odor stops.** Immediate, and usually the first thing customers mention
after a contaminated removal.

**Cleaner air below.** Air moves from the attic into the living space through the
same penetrations it leaves by. A contaminated attic is contributing to what you
breathe.

**Drier sheathing**, which is a structural maintenance saving over a decade
rather than a comfort one.

**Fewer secondary pests.** Rodent nesting hosts mites and dermestid
beetles, and when the host animals leave, those move inward.

## How much insulation is right

Whatcom County sits in a marine climate zone where the current recommendation for
attics is roughly **R-49 to R-60**.

A great many houses here are well short of that. Homes from the mid-century era
frequently have a third of it. Even homes that were adequate when built are often
underperforming now, because insulation that has been compressed, walked on,
disturbed by animals or partially fallen is delivering well below its nominal
rating.

Depth matters more than people assume, and it is worth having marked at
completion so anyone can verify it later without guessing.

## Around the county

**The century-old city housing.** Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York, Roosevelt and South Hill. Original or minimal insulation, decades of rodent access through
soffit and roofline gaps, and knob-and-tube era wiring that needs identifying
before anything is blown in. The largest efficiency gains in the county are in
these attics.

**Around Lake Whatcom** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor. 1970s construction against a forest edge, heavy rodent pressure off the treeline, and roofline and soffit gaps that have been open for years.

**Newer hillside** — Alabama Hill, Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain. Generally
adequate insulation, so the work here is usually contamination-driven rather than
performance-driven, and often follows a specific soffit entry.

**Rural** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Larger footprints, deer mice rather
than house mice, and outbuildings that may need including.

**Waterfront and seasonal** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Homes empty
through the wet months where an entry point can go unnoticed for a whole season,
so contamination is frequently more advanced than the owner expects.

## Timing

Any time of year works, with two preferences.

**Late summer and early autumn** is best, because it pairs with the
[exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) that should precede it and gets
ahead of the rodent push that comes with the September and October rain.

**Before winter** is the practical deadline if the driver is comfort or heating
cost, since the benefit only shows up when the weather does.

The exception is a job beyond our size, which we sub to a local insulation contractor rather than stretch to cover. If a bat colony is involved,
the insulation work follows the exclusion window rather than your calendar or
ours.

## What we find up there

The attic is the least-visited part of most houses, and what turns up is
consistent enough to list.

**Rodent runs through blown-in material.** Channels compressed to the ceiling
deck, usually following the top plates of interior walls because that is the
easiest travel route. The insulation either side may look fine while the runs
themselves are contaminated through.

**Insulation off the framing.** Batts that have slipped or fallen and are lying
on the ceiling drywall, where they hold moisture rather than resisting heat.

**Blocked soffit vents.** The single most common ventilation fault. Insulation
pushed into the eaves during the original install, or by an animal, closing off
the intake that the whole attic depends on. Baffles fix it and are cheap.

**Bath fans venting into the attic.** Genuinely common in older homes, and it
means the moisture from every shower is being deposited directly onto the roof
sheathing. It needs ducting to the exterior, and it is worth finding.

**Recessed lights and open penetrations.** The air leaks described above, plus
older fixtures that cannot be safely covered with insulation and need
identifying before anything is blown.

**Knob-and-tube wiring.** In prewar Bellingham housing this is a real
possibility, and it changes the job — it needs an electrician's assessment before
insulation goes over it, and that is not a corner to cut.

**Stored belongings.** Boxes, decorations and the occasional forgotten item,
frequently sitting directly on insulation and compressing it. Where a household
needs attic storage, a raised platform preserves the insulation underneath and is
worth building into the job.

**Damp or stained sheathing.** Which is a roofing and ventilation question before
it is an insulation one, and the reason we look up as well as down.

You get photographs of all of it. It is the only part of the house where the
owner genuinely has no idea what the condition is, and that information is worth
as much as the work.

## Blown-in or batts?

A reasonable question and the answer is mostly about the attic rather than the
material.

**Blown-in** — usually fiberglass or cellulose — is what most attic replacements
use here, because it fills irregular spaces, works around framing and wiring
without gaps, and reaches the eaves properly where batts frequently do not. It is
also faster to install to a consistent depth. The disadvantage is that it is
harder to move if someone needs to work up there later.

**Batts** make sense in a small, regular, easily accessible attic, or where a
household genuinely needs to use the space. They are much less forgiving of poor
installation — a batt with a gap at the edge or compressed around a wire is
underperforming in a way that is easy to see and easy to ignore.

The honest position is that installation quality matters more than the choice
between them. Blown-in at an even depth with clear soffit intake beats
meticulously specified batts with gaps at every joist end, and the reverse is
also true.

What we will not do is recommend a material because it prices better on the
estimate. The attic decides.

## For landlords and property managers

Two reasons this comes up outside owner-occupied homes.

**Turnover is the cheap moment.** An empty unit means no scheduling around a
tenant, unrestricted access, and no disruption to anyone. Insulation work at
turnover costs less in practice than the same job during occupancy.

**It shows up in condition and cost.** A contaminated attic produces odor
complaints that get attributed to everything but the ceiling, and a badly
insulated one produces heating complaints and higher tenant costs. Where heating
is included in rent, the arithmetic is direct.

a point in time, which is useful at both ends of a tenancy.

## Ventilation, which decides whether any of it works

Insulation and ventilation are frequently treated as separate trades, and in an
attic they are the same conversation.

A vented attic is supposed to move outside air in at the eaves and out at the
ridge or gable, keeping the sheathing near outdoor temperature and carrying away
whatever moisture gets past the ceiling. When that airflow is working, a cold
attic is a dry attic. When it is blocked, the attic becomes a condensation
chamber every winter.

**Soffit intake is where it usually fails.** Insulation pushed into the eaves —
during the original install, by an animal, or by whoever last blew material in
without baffles — closes the intake completely. From below, everything looks
fine. The symptom shows up as damp sheathing, frost on nails in cold weather, and
insulation that never performs to its rating.

**Baffles are the fix**, and they are inexpensive. Fitted at each rafter bay
before insulation goes down, they hold a clear channel from the soffit up past
the insulation line. Any attic job that blows material to depth without them is
storing up the problem it was meant to solve.

**Exhaust matters too.** Ridge venting, gable venting or roof vents need to be
open and not obstructed. Mixing vent types can short-circuit the airflow so that
air enters and leaves at the top without ever passing the eaves.

**And bath fans have to go outside.** Venting into the attic is common in older
Bellingham homes and it deposits the moisture from every shower directly onto the
roof deck. Ducting it to the exterior is a small job with a large effect.

We check all of this as part of the work rather than as an add-on, because
insulating an attic with blocked intake is spending money to make it damper.

## After the work

Two habits keep an attic in the condition you just paid for.

**Look after any trade has been up there.** An electrician, a plumber, an
antenna installer or a solar crew will move insulation and baffles to get where
they are going, and very few put them back. A ten-minute look afterward catches
it while it is still trivial.

**Watch for the symptoms rather than inspecting.** A room that stops holding
temperature, frost on nail points in cold weather, a musty smell returning, or
sounds overhead are all the attic telling you something changed. Those are worth
a call; routine inspection is not necessary.
