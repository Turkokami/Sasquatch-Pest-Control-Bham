## Three ants, and only one of them eats your house

More money is wasted on ant treatment in this county than on any other pest,
and almost all of it comes down to one mistake: treating the ant you can see
instead of identifying which ant it is.

Roughly, and from our own call volume rather than from a textbook:

**Odorous house ants lead, and it is not close.** This is the ant most homes
here actually have. It does no structural damage at all, which is exactly why
people treat it themselves — and it is the one species where doing that makes
things worse rather than merely failing. Repellent sprays cause the colony to
**bud**: it fragments and relocates instead of dying, and one nest becomes three
or four. A great many of the ant jobs we are called to are jobs somebody has
already made harder.

**Carpenter ants are about three calls in ten**, and they are the species that
matters structurally. They excavate galleries, maintain satellite colonies
inside buildings, and the work is finding the nest rather than killing the
trail.

**Moisture ants are fewer again** — a small share of the year — and they are not
really a pest problem at all. They are an indicator. They only nest in wood that
is already wet, so finding them inside a wall means water has been getting into
that wall long enough to soften it. Correct the water and they leave on their
own, because the thing that made the site habitable is gone.

Those three want different work, and one of them wants the opposite of what the
bottle under the sink does. That is why we will not quote ant work over the
phone from a description of "small black ants."

## Odorous house ants

**Odorous house ant** (*Tapinoma sessile*). Small, dark brown, and unmistakable
once you know it — crushed workers smell strongly of coconut or blue cheese.
Nests indoors and out, often in wall voids, under floors, or beneath objects on
the ground. The important thing about this species is the budding described
above: it is the ant that most often gets worse after somebody treats it from a
hardware-store bottle. It requires baiting and patience, and patience is the
part people are not warned about — a bait working properly still looks like
nothing is happening for the first week.

## Carpenter ants

**What they are.** *Camponotus modoc* is the common one here — large, matte
black, workers ranging from about a quarter to over half an inch in the same
colony, because carpenter ants are polymorphic. That size variation is a useful
field mark. A single evenly-sized column of ants is probably not carpenter ants.

**They do not eat wood.** This is the most common misconception and it changes
the strategy. Carpenter ants chew wood to excavate galleries and push the
shavings out as waste. They feed on other insects, honeydew and sugars, which is
exactly why baiting works — a worker will carry a sugar bait back and share it
through the colony, which a wood-eating insect would never do.

**Parent and satellite colonies.** This is the part that defeats most
do-it-yourself treatment and a fair amount of professional treatment too. A
carpenter ant colony is not one nest. There is a parent colony containing the
queen and the brood, which needs consistent moisture and is very often outdoors —
a stump, a fallen log, a standing dead tree, a woodpile, a landscape timber.
Radiating from it are satellite colonies containing workers and older larvae,
which tolerate drier conditions and are quite happy inside your wall void, your
attic, your door frame, or the void behind a bathtub.

Kill a satellite and the parent replaces it. Spray a trail and you have removed
foragers the colony can afford to lose. The only treatment that ends it is one
that gets back to the queen, which is why we use non-repellent products and
baits designed to be carried rather than contact killers designed to knock down
what you can see.

Foraging distance is part of why this is hard: a hundred yards between the parent
colony and the building is unremarkable, and workers cover it along fence lines,
utility wires and branch contact rather than across open lawn. The stump that
matters is sometimes on the neighboring lot.

**The winter tell.** Foraging carpenter ants outdoors in summer are normal and
frequently not worth treating. Large ants walking across your kitchen floor in
January are a different signal entirely — they are not commuting from a stump in
midwinter. Winter activity indoors means a satellite colony is already in the
structure, and that is worth acting on.

**Frass.** Carpenter ants push their excavation waste out of slit-like openings,
producing small piles of what looks like coarse sawdust. Real frass is fibrous
and shredded rather than powdery, and it usually contains insect body parts —
legs, wing fragments, bits of the colony's prey. That last detail is the
reliable way to tell it from drill dust left by a contractor. If you find it,
leave it where it is. It marks the gallery, and sweeping it up removes the best
evidence we have about where to open the wall.

**Swarmers.** Winged reproductives appear in spring here, generally March
through May. Finding them *inside* the house is significant — it means a mature
colony, at least a couple of years established, is inside the structure rather
than outside it. Winged ants at a window in April are among the clearest signals
in this business.

## Moisture ants

**What they are.** Several *Lasius* species, smaller than carpenter ants, and
usually yellowish to light brown rather than black. Some produce a distinctly
citrus or lemony smell when crushed.

**What they mean.** Moisture ants excavate only wood that is already
water-damaged and beginning to decay. They cannot establish in sound dry lumber.
So their presence inside a structure is diagnostic: there is, or has recently
been, a persistent water source in that location.

In practice, when we find moisture ants in a Whatcom County house, the real
finding is almost always one of a short list. A failed or overflowing gutter
running down a wall. A downspout discharging against the foundation. A crawlspace
with no functioning vapor barrier and standing groundwater. Bathroom or kitchen
plumbing leaking slowly inside a wall. A deck ledger board flashed badly or not
flashed at all. Siding that has been in contact with soil or bark mulch for
years. Window flashing that failed a decade ago.

**Why this is good news, mostly.** Moisture ants themselves do very little
structural damage — the wood they occupy was already compromised. The bad news
is what they are pointing at, because a wall wet enough to support them has
usually also been supporting decay fungus, and that is a repair conversation
rather than a pest conversation.

We will tell you when the person you actually need is a contractor. That
sometimes costs us the treatment, and it is the right call.

## The others you will meet here

**Pavement ant.** Small, dark, characteristically nesting under slabs, driveways,
walkways and against foundations, throwing up small soil craters at cracks and
expansion joints. Mostly an outdoor nuisance that comes inside to forage.

**Velvety tree ant.** Sometimes confused with carpenter ants — dark head, reddish
mid-section, black abdomen, and a strong odor when crushed. Nests in trees and in
damp structural wood, and can be aggressive.

**Thatching and field ants.** Common outdoors on rural and forest-edge property.
Rarely a structural concern, occasionally a yard one.

## Why the hardware-store approach usually fails

Three reasons, and they are worth understanding because they explain most of
what people have already tried before calling us.

**Repellent products scatter the colony.** Most consumer sprays are contact
killers and repellents. They kill the ants they touch and warn everything else
away from the treated surface. Against a budding species like the odorous house
ant that is actively counterproductive.

**Killing foragers does nothing.** The ants you see are a small percentage of the
colony and the most expendable part of it. A colony that loses its foragers
sends more.

**The trail is destroyed.** This is the practical one. That line of ants across
your counter is a chemical trail leading somewhere, and following it is how we
find a nest without opening walls at random. Spraying it removes our best
diagnostic tool for a day of temporary relief.

So the single most useful thing you can do before we arrive is leave the trail
alone. Clean up the food source, by all means. Do not treat the ants.

## How we work an ant job

**Identify.** Under magnification where it matters. Everything downstream
depends on this, and it takes minutes.

**Find the nest.** Trail following, moisture readings, sounding suspect wood,
and inspecting the places these species actually nest — sill plates, rim joists,
crawlspaces, wall voids around windows and doors, subfloor beneath bathrooms and
kitchens, attic sheathing, and outdoors, the stumps, woodpiles and landscape
timbers within foraging distance of the house.

**Treat to reach the colony.** Non-repellent products the ants walk through and
carry, plus baits matched to the species and the season, because these species
shift between sugar and protein preference through the year. Applied where the
colony travels rather than broadcast across surfaces.

**Correct the conditions.** Where moisture ants are involved this is the actual
treatment. Where carpenter ants are involved it is what stops the next colony —
removing the stump, moving the woodpile off the foundation, cutting the branch
that bridges to the roof.

**Exclude.** Sealing the entry routes, which on Whatcom County housing tends to
mean around utility penetrations, window and door framing, and where siding
meets foundation.

**Follow up.** Decline runs in weeks rather than days, and activity often
*increases* briefly first as the bait circulates. We do not publish a fixed
number of weeks for carpenter ants, because no extension source we can point to
gives one, and colony size, satellite count, nest location and how much material
actually reaches the brood all move the answer on a given house. Odorous house
ants are the species where the two-to-three-week figure is well attested, and
that is a different insect with a different nest structure. So we check rather
than assume, and we would rather revisit a property than quote you a schedule
we cannot stand behind.

## What the damage looks like over time

Carpenter ants work slowly. A satellite colony is not going to bring a wall down
in a season, and anyone telling you otherwise is selling urgency. What they do
over years is hollow structural members along the grain — sill plates, headers,
studs around windows, joists near a chronically damp area — and because they
prefer wood that is already slightly softened, they compound an existing decay
problem rather than starting a new one.

The practical consequence is that a colony left for five or six years in a
chronically wet wall turns a pest bill into a carpentry bill. That is the
argument for dealing with it, and it is an honest one.

## The ant year in Whatcom County

Ant activity here follows a predictable arc, and knowing where you are in it
tells you a lot about what you are looking at.

**February and March.** Colonies come out of winter dormancy as soil
temperatures rise. First indoor sightings of the year, usually foragers testing
routes. Early activity in a house that had ants last year is worth acting on
rather than waiting to see.

**March through May.** Carpenter ant swarming season. Winged reproductives
appear, and this is the diagnostic window — swarmers found indoors mean a
mature colony inside the structure, not a nest in the yard. It is also when
moisture ant activity shows up most clearly, because the wet season has just
finished doing its work on any wall that leaks.

**June through August.** Peak foraging. This is when most people call, and it is
when trails are easiest to follow, which makes it a good time to treat. Colonies
shift toward protein as brood production peaks, so bait selection changes with
the season.

**September and October.** Activity concentrates as the rain returns. Ants that
have been living comfortably outdoors start using structures more heavily, and
satellite colonies that were established in summer become obvious.

**November through January.** Outdoor activity effectively stops. Anything you
see indoors now is living indoors — which is exactly why winter sightings carry
more diagnostic weight than summer ones.

## Commercial and multifamily ant work

In apartments, condominiums and HOA-managed buildings, ants raise the same
structural problem bed bugs do: a colony does not respect unit boundaries. Wall
voids, utility chases and shared plumbing runs connect units, and treating one
apartment while the adjacent one goes untouched simply moves the nest.

Odorous house ants are the species that most often turn a single complaint into
a building-wide program, precisely because repellent treatment causes budding.
A well-meaning maintenance response with a consumer spray can convert one nest
into several across a floor, and by the time we are called the problem is
genuinely larger than it was.

For food handling, healthcare and school accounts the constraint is different
again — bait placement has to be secure and documented, application in occupied
school buildings carries notification requirements under Washington law, and the
record matters as much as the result.

## Keeping them out

Fix the water first — gutters, downspouts discharging away from the foundation,
crawlspace drainage and vapor barrier, and any plumbing you already know about.
Keep bark mulch and soil below the siding line. Move woodpiles off the ground and
away from the house. Take out stumps within reach of the property where you
reasonably can. Cut branches that touch the roof or walls, because they are a
bridge. Seal around utility penetrations and window framing. Deal with fallen
fruit and keep sugars sealed indoors.

For carpenter ants specifically, the yard matters more than the kitchen. The
parent colony is usually outside, and removing its habitat does more than any
amount of interior treatment.

For moisture ants, there is only one prevention step that matters and it is not
a pest control step at all: keep the wood dry. Every other measure on this list
is secondary to that one. A wall that stays dry cannot host them, and a wall
that stays wet will host them again no matter how thoroughly it was treated.
