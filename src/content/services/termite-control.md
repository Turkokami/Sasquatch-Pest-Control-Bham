## What we actually have here

Nearly everything written about termites online is about a different insect in a
different climate. Search the topic and you will get material about eastern
subterranean termites, Formosan termites, mud tubes, soil barriers and bait
station perimeters — an enormous industry built around species that are
genuinely destructive across the South and East.

That is not our situation, and pretending otherwise is how people in Whatcom
County end up paying for the wrong thing.

**Pacific dampwood termite.** This is the one we find. It is large — swarmers
run around an inch including wings, which makes them startling — and it is
defined by a single requirement: it needs wood with high moisture content.
Unlike subterranean species it does not need soil contact and does not build
shelter tubes, because it lives directly in the wet wood it is consuming. It is
common in the Pacific Northwest precisely because our climate keeps wood damp.

**Western subterranean termite.** Present in Washington, but where in Washington
is the part that decides how this page is written. WSU Cooperative Extension
bulletin EB0787 states that the subterranean termite occurs only as far north as
Seattle in western Washington. Whatcom County sits roughly ninety miles beyond
that limit, which puts this species outside its documented range here rather
than merely uncommon in it. Distributions do move and timber travels on trucks,
so it is not an impossibility — but the default assumption on a Whatcom County
termite call is dampwood, and anything that treats subterranean as the starting
point has imported a Southern frame into a place it does not fit.

The insect itself is smaller than the dampwood termite and soil-dependent, and
it does build the mud shelter tubes people associate with termites. Genuine
tubes running up a stem wall would change both the species question and the
method, so they are worth thirty seconds of anyone's attention even where the
odds are firmly against them.

**What we do not have.** Formosan termites are not established here. Drywood
termites of the kind that drive fumigation in California and the Southwest are
not a Whatcom County problem. If someone has proposed tenting your house in
Bellingham, get a second opinion before you agree to anything.

## The moisture point, which is the whole point

A dampwood termite colony cannot establish in sound, dry lumber. It is not
capable of it. So when we find one in a structure, the finding is not really
"you have termites." The finding is **this wood has been wet for a long time.**

In practice the source is almost always on a short list. A gutter that has been
overflowing down a wall. A downspout discharging against the foundation. A
crawlspace with no working vapor barrier and standing groundwater. Plumbing
leaking slowly inside a wall. A deck ledger board flashed badly or not at all.
Siding in contact with soil or bark mulch. Window or door flashing that failed
years ago. A roof leak nobody traced because it only shows in heavy weather.

That is the same list as our [moisture ant](/services/ant-control/) page,
because it is the same underlying condition producing two different insects.

It also means the wood was already decaying before the termites arrived. Fungal
decay does not need an insect, and by the time a colony is established the
structural loss is usually a combination of both. Which is why treating the
insects and stopping there gets you a quiet year and the same conversation
later.

We will tell you when the person you actually need is a contractor or a roofer.
It costs us work regularly and it is the correct answer.

## Telling termites from carpenter ants

The two get confused constantly, especially during spring and fall swarms, and
the distinction changes the entire approach.

**Waist.** Termites are broad through the middle with no obvious narrowing.
Carpenter ants have a sharply pinched waist. This is the easiest field mark.

**Wings.** A termite's four wings are all roughly the same length and extend
well past the body. An ant's front pair is noticeably longer than the back pair.
Shed termite wings found on a windowsill are often all the same size in a small
pile.

**Antennae.** Termite antennae are straight and look beaded. Ant antennae have a
distinct elbow.

**Frass.** Carpenter ants excavate and push shavings out, so you find fibrous
frass containing insect body parts below a gallery. Dampwood termites consume
the wood and produce hard fecal pellets rather than shavings.

**Behavior.** Carpenter ants forage in the open — you see workers walking. You
will rarely see a termite worker, because they stay inside the wood and avoid
light.

Get this wrong in either direction and the treatment is wasted, which is why we
identify before we quote.

## Swarming season

**Dampwood termites swarm in late summer and fall**, roughly August through
October, on warm evenings. They are strongly drawn to lights, which is why the
classic report is a large winged insect at a porch light or a window in
September.

Swarmers found **outdoors** mean there is a colony somewhere in the vicinity —
possibly a stump, a fallen log, a landscape timber, a woodpile, or someone
else's structure. It does not by itself mean your house is infested.

Swarmers found **indoors**, or a pile of shed wings on an interior windowsill,
is a much stronger signal. Reproductives emerging inside a structure usually
means the colony is inside the structure.

Western subterranean termites swarm on the opposite schedule — spring, generally
during the day, often after rain.

## The insect nobody warns you about

If you own an older home in this county with a damp crawlspace, the wood-boring
insect you are statistically most likely to encounter is not a termite at all.

**Anobiid powderpost beetles** are genuinely common in Pacific Northwest
crawlspaces, and they are frequently mistaken for termite damage or dismissed as
old damage. The signs are small round exit holes — roughly the diameter of a
pencil lead — in joists, subfloor and sill plates, with fine powdery frass below
them. Unlike some wood borers, anobiids will re-infest the same timber
generation after generation as long as the moisture content stays high enough.

The reason they matter here is the reason everything on this page matters: they
need damp wood. A crawlspace with a functioning vapor barrier, decent drainage
and airflow does not support them. One with standing water and no barrier does,
indefinitely.

Fine powder below small round holes in a crawlspace is worth a look. It is
treatable, and the underlying fix is the same moisture correction.

## How we work a wood-destroying pest job

**Identify the insect.** Termite, carpenter ant, or wood-boring beetle. Everything
after this depends on it.

**Find the moisture.** Meter readings on suspect timber, and tracing the water
back to its source rather than noting that the wood is wet. This is the part that
determines whether the work holds.

**Map the extent.** Sounding and probing to establish how far the affected
timber runs, because the visible damage is routinely the smallest part.

**Treat.** Targeted application to the affected members and the colony, matched
to the species. Dampwood termites, having no soil dependency, do not call for the
soil-barrier approach used against subterranean species — treating them that way
is expense without benefit.

**Report the moisture finding clearly.** In writing, with where it is and what we
believe is causing it, so you can act on it with whichever trade is appropriate.

**Recommend the structural work.** Sometimes that is us — vapor barrier,
crawlspace drainage, ventilation, exclusion. Sometimes it is a roofer, a
plumber, a gutter contractor or a carpenter. We will say which.

## What the damage looks like over time

Dampwood termites work slowly compared to the subterranean species that generate
most of the industry's marketing, and anyone selling you urgency about an
imminent collapse is overselling.

What actually happens over years is that structural members in a chronically wet
area lose section — sill plates, rim joists, subfloor, the framing around a
window that has been leaking. Because the wood was already decaying, the two
processes compound, and the eventual repair is a carpentry bill rather than a
pest bill.

The argument for dealing with it is not that your house is about to fall down.
It is that a moisture problem left running for five more years costs
substantially more to fix than one addressed now, and the insects are the most
visible evidence you will get that it is running.

## Prevention, which is just moisture control

Keep gutters clear and downspouts discharging away from the foundation. Maintain
a working crawlspace vapor barrier and address standing water. Keep soil and bark
mulch below the siding line. Store firewood off the ground and away from the
structure. Remove stumps and buried wood where you reasonably can. Check deck
ledger flashing. Keep irrigation off the siding. Trace and fix small plumbing
leaks rather than living with them.

Every item on that list is a building maintenance task rather than a pest control
one, which is the honest summary of this whole page: in Whatcom County, termite
prevention is water management.

So the order of operations in a Whatcom County sale is straightforward. An

If your inspection report lists a wood-destroying organism finding and the

What we can tell you generally is what buyers in this county should be looking

anything else on the list.

If a seller's paperwork mentions dampwood termites or anobiid beetles, the useful
question is not "were they treated" but "was the water fixed." Treatment without
correction is a temporary result on a permanent condition.

## What we will not sell you

Soil-barrier treatment for a dampwood infestation. The species has no soil
dependency, so treating the perimeter of a house for an insect living in a wet
window header is money spent on the wrong problem.

Tenting or fumigation. It is not a Whatcom County treatment for a Whatcom County
species, and if it has been proposed to you here it is worth a second opinion
before you commit.

An annual renewing termite plan on a structure with no findings and no moisture
problem. Recurring programs make sense where pressure is recurring. A dry, sound,
well-drained house does not need one, and we would rather tell you that than
collect for it.

Treatment of an old, inactive infestation without evidence of current activity.
Damage persists long after the insects are gone, and the presence of galleries
in a joist is not by itself a reason to treat.

## What we find in a crawlspace, in order of frequency

Most of this work happens under floors, so it is worth describing what the space
actually tells us.

**Standing water or saturated soil.** The most common finding in older Bellingham
housing and the root of nearly everything else. Groundwater, a downspout
discharging under the house, or a plumbing leak that has been running quietly.

**Missing, shredded or unsecured vapor barrier.** Ground moisture evaporates
straight into the crawlspace and into the framing above it. A functioning barrier
is one of the cheapest interventions available and one of the most frequently
absent.

**Blocked or missing vent screens.** Two problems at once — moisture that cannot
escape, and an open door for rodents. It is why wood-destroying pest work and
[rodent work](/services/rodent-control/) so often turn out to be the same visit.

**Wood in contact with soil.** Posts, forms left in place after construction,
stored lumber, an old stack of offcuts nobody removed. Every one of those is a
colony site within a few feet of the structure.

**Elevated moisture readings in the framing.** Meter readings on sill plates,
rim joists and subfloor tell us whether a problem is active or historical, which
determines whether treatment is warranted at all.

**Insulation on the ground.** Usually a sign of a rodent history, and it holds
moisture against the joists once it falls.

**Small round exit holes with powder below.** Anobiid beetle activity, and worth
distinguishing carefully from old damage that is no longer live.

A crawlspace that is dry, sealed, screened and clear of debris does not support
dampwood termites, moisture ants or wood-boring beetles. That is not a treatment
claim — it is a statement about what those insects require, and it is why the
most valuable thing we do on many of these jobs is fix the space rather than
spray it.

## Timing

Unlike wasps or rodents, this work has no urgent season — the damage accrues over
years rather than weeks, so there is no month where acting is dramatically better
than another.

What does have a season is the evidence. Swarmers appear in late summer and fall,
which is when most people first suspect they have something. Crawlspace moisture
is most obvious in the wet months, roughly November through March, when a
drainage problem is actually running rather than sitting dry and invisible in
August. If you want the clearest picture of what a crawlspace is doing, look at
it in February.

The practical advice is simply not to sit on it. A moisture source left running
for another two winters costs more to remedy and takes more wood with it, and the
insects are the most legible warning you are going to get.
