## What this service actually is

A repeating treatment of the outdoor space on a property through the warm
months, aimed at the mosquitoes that live and rest there. The mosquito part of
it is a botanical product applied to the shaded, humid places adult mosquitoes
spend the daylight hours — under decks, inside dense shrubs, along fence lines,
in the leaf litter nobody rakes. A bifenthrin granule goes down on the same
visit, and that one is the tick and flea half of the work: its label names
those pests and says nothing about mosquitoes. Two products, each used for
what it is labeled for.

It is not a single heroic spray and it is not a fogging service. It is a
program, because mosquito pressure is a seasonal flow rather than an event, and
because the botanical product's label calls for reapplication roughly every
thirty days. Treating a mosquito problem once is like mowing a lawn once.

This is a newer service line for us, and it is worth saying so plainly rather
than implying otherwise. Our own job records for 2023 through 2025 contain one
mosquito job in three years. What we bring to it is not a decade of mosquito
work in this county — it is the products, the licensing, the method, and the
same refusal to oversell that the rest of this site runs on. If you want a
company that will tell you mosquito treatment does less than you hope, you have
found one.

## Why this is a comfort service here, and not a disease one

Most mosquito marketing in the United States is built on West Nile virus. In
Whatcom County that case is weak, and we would rather lose the sale than make
it dishonestly.

The [Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2026-06/420-720-WNVSurveillanceReport-2021-2025.pdf)
publishes a five-year West Nile surveillance report covering 2021 through 2025.
Across those five years the state recorded **16 human West Nile cases**,
between one and four a year. Of those sixteen, **five were acquired in
Washington** — the rest were infections picked up elsewhere and diagnosed here.
And all five of the locally acquired cases were exposed in Eastern Washington:
two in Yakima County, one in Benton, one in Walla Walla, and one in either
Benton or Franklin.

Not one locally acquired human case west of the Cascades, in five years.

The single detail that touches this county is instructive rather than alarming.
Of the sixteen West Nile-positive horses confirmed in that period, most were in
Eastern Washington, with Yakima alone accounting for seven. **One was reported
from Whatcom County** — and the report is careful to add that the animal had
been to Yakima County before it fell ill. That is not local transmission.
It is a Whatcom County address on an Eastern Washington infection, and the
difference is the whole point.

So what are we actually selling? The use of your own garden in August. That is
a real thing to want and a reasonable thing to pay for. It is simply not a
medical emergency, and we are not going to dress it up as one.

Whatcom County Health and Community Services is one of the partners listed in
the state's mosquito surveillance network, so the testing that would detect a
local change is being done by people whose job it is. If that picture changes,
this page changes with it.

## The mosquitoes you actually meet here

[WSU Extension](https://wpcdn.web.wsu.edu/wp-puyallup/uploads/sites/415/2014/12/PNW_PPMosquito_Spring2014.pdf)
counts over fifty mosquito species across Washington and Oregon, with the
genera *Culex* and *Aedes* dominating regional populations. Three names do most
of the work in any honest conversation about a Whatcom County back garden.

**Aedes vexans** is, in WSU's words, one of the most abundant mosquito species
in Oregon and Washington. It is the one that ruins an evening: WSU notes it
bites most often at dusk and through the evening on warm summer nights. It lays
eggs in moist soil in places that flood, that get irrigated, or that hold
standing water, and those eggs are resistant to freezing and drying — which is
why a low corner of a lawn can produce mosquitoes year after year without
anyone ever seeing standing water sitting in it. It has a reputation as a
floodwater breeder, from irrigated ground to flooded streams, but WSU records
it as very common in urban areas too.

It is also the reason this page is careful about what it promises. WSU reports
that adult *Aedes vexans* females **may disperse up to 29 miles**. No yard
treatment reaches 29 miles.

**Culex pipiens** is the urban one. WSU describes the larvae of this group as
fairly tolerant of polluted or high-organic water, and names *C. pipiens* as
the most pollution-tolerant of them, tending to dominate in urban areas. In
plain terms: it is perfectly happy in the water in a forgotten bucket, a
clogged gutter, a storm drain, a tin can, the drip tray under a potted plant.

**Culex tarsalis** matters more east of the mountains than it does here, but it
travels alongside *pipiens* in the literature because the two of them are, per
WSU, the most abundant West Nile vectors during peak mosquito season — which
WSU places at **July through September**. Washington DOH's surveillance report
names the same two species as the state's primary vectors, and adds that adult
females of both can overwinter.

The practical reading of all that: the mosquitoes bothering you in Bellingham
in August are mostly a dusk-feeding floodwater species and a container-breeding
urban species. One of them is produced by the shape of your ground and the
other by the things sitting on it. Those two facts set the ceiling on what any
treatment can do.

## What we apply, and where you can read the label

Two products, both published, and each used for exactly what its own label
names. That distinction is not pedantry — under federal pesticide law a product
has to be used and described consistently with its labeling, so which pest is
printed on which label decides what we are allowed to tell you each one does.

**EcoVia MT** is the mosquito product. It is a botanical material whose label
names mosquitoes and ticks, applied to the places adults rest rather than
broadcast across a lawn, and its label calls for reapplication about every
thirty days — which is what makes this a monthly rhythm rather than an
arbitrary one.

**The bifenthrin granule** is the tick and flea product. Ours is Bifen LP
Insecticide Granules, **EPA Reg. 53883-124**, whose single active ingredient is
bifenthrin. Its label reads: *for use to control Ants (including Imported Fire
Ants), Chinch Bugs, Fleas, Mole Crickets, Ticks and other insect pests on lawns
in landscaped areas and perimeters.* The use areas it lists are the ones that
matter here — lawns, flower beds, mulch beds, along fence lines, around decks
and decking, and the foundation perimeter.

Mosquitoes are not on that granule's label anywhere. So we do not describe it as
a mosquito treatment, and if anyone tells you a granule broadcast on a lawn is
how mosquitoes get handled, they are describing a product in terms its label
does not support. The granule earns its place on this visit because the ground
it covers is tick ground.

You can read the labels yourself. Every pesticide product this company applies
is listed on [what we use](/what-we-use/) with its EPA registration number, its
active ingredients, and its label and safety data sheet hosted here as PDFs.
Almost nobody in this trade does that. Washington requires a licensed applicator
to tell you what was applied if you ask; it does not require anyone to publish
it in advance, which is exactly why publishing it is worth something.

One honest gap, recorded rather than hidden: EcoVia MT's own label and safety
data sheet are not yet in that register. Every entry there was built by reading
the product's label and corroborating it against its safety data sheet — two
documents agreeing before anything got written down — and we are not going to
break that rule for one product by copying a supplier's catalog description.
Its label joins the register when we have it in hand.

What you will not get from us is the word "safe". Under federal pesticide law it
is unlawful to describe a product in terms that differ from its accepted
labeling, and "safe", "harmless" and "non-toxic" are the specific words that
draw enforcement. A pest control company that uses them about a pesticide is
telling you something about itself. We will tell you what went down, where it
went, and what the label requires afterwards, and we will hand you the label.

## The part that matters more than anything we spray

Here is the thing a mosquito company is not supposed to tell you: the
highest-leverage action on your property is yours, not ours.

WSU Extension states it flatly — preventing mosquito breeding habitat and
managing populations at the larval stage is the most effective approach to
reducing mosquitoes. Not adult treatment. Larval source reduction.

The numbers are the argument. WSU reports that as many as **500 larvae** can be
found in just two and a half cups of water. A mosquito can go from egg to adult
in **as little as seven days**, depending on species and temperature. And eggs
get laid in habitats ranging from storm sewers and mud puddles to the water left
in a tin can or the drip tray beneath a potted plant.

So a saucer under a flowerpot, refilled by a sprinkler every morning and never
emptied, is a mosquito factory running a one-week production cycle. No adult
treatment outcompetes emptying it.

When we walk a property we will show you the water. Sometimes it is obvious — a
tarp over a woodpile, a wheelbarrow left upright, a birdbath nobody has changed
since June. Sometimes it is structural: a downspout discharging onto a flat
spot, a French drain that stopped draining, a low seam in a lawn that holds
water for four days after rain and then dries just enough that you never notice
it. In a county with our rainfall the structural ones are the ones that matter,
and they are also the ones a treatment cannot fix.

None of that is a reason to skip the treatment. It is a reason to do both, and
to know which half is doing the heavy lifting.

## What a mosquito treatment cannot do, stated plainly

Four limits, so that nobody is surprised in August.

**It is not a barrier.** Mosquitoes are not held out by a treated perimeter the
way a mouse is held out by sealed penetrations. They fly in. Given WSU's
29-mile dispersal figure for *Aedes vexans*, your neighbor's standing water,
the ditch down the road and a flooded pasture a mile away are all upstream of
your garden.

**It does not reach what it cannot touch.** A product applied to resting sites
works where it lands. Mosquitoes resting in a blackberry thicket on the far side
of a property line are not treated, and the treatment does not travel to them.

**It does not last the season.** Roughly thirty days for the botanical product,
by its own label. Anybody quoting you one visit for a summer is quoting you one
month of a summer.

**It cannot out-produce a breeding site you keep.** If the sprinkler keeps
filling the low corner, the low corner keeps making mosquitoes, and we will keep
treating the adults it produces.

What it does do is reduce the resident population on a property and pull the
pressure down from unbearable to tolerable across the months you actually want
to be outside. For most properties in Bellingham, Ferndale, Lynden and the
valley, that is the honest offer.

## When in the year this is worth doing

Start before the peak rather than during it.

WSU places peak mosquito season in this region at **July through September**.
Treating in July, once a yard has already become unusable, means working against
a population that has had the whole spring to build. Beginning while the ground
is still producing its first generations means treating a smaller problem at
every subsequent visit.

The exact start and end months of a program are set per property, because
properties differ more than calendars do. A dry, sunny lot on a hill in
Bellingham and a shaded acre backing onto a wetland in Everson do not have the
same season, and a program that pretends otherwise is being sold by the month
rather than by the mosquito. We will tell you from the visit when yours is worth
starting — and if the answer is that your property does not need a program at
all, we will tell you that too. It is the same position we take on
[bees](/services/bee-removal/), where most calls need a beekeeper or nothing.

## Mosquitoes and ticks are one visit

We sell these as two services because people search for them as two problems,
but on the ground it is one application. EcoVia MT is labeled for mosquitoes and
ticks together, and the habitat overlaps almost exactly: shade, leaf litter, and
the humid margin where a mown lawn meets taller growth.

If you are buying mosquito treatment, tick suppression comes with it. If you
arrived worried about ticks specifically, read
[tick control](/services/tick-control/) first — it is honest about how little
tick pressure this county has, and about the fact that
[the western black-legged tick](/pest-library/western-black-legged-tick/) is the
only Washington species that carries Lyme disease. Our guide to
[ticks in Washington](/guides/ticks-in-washington/) has the full picture, built
on a statewide tick survey rather than on national averages.

## What it costs, and how we quote it

From a visit, in writing, and never over the phone.

The reason is in everything above: the work on a property is decided by its
water and its shade. Two addresses the same size on the same street can need
different programs, because one has a flat back lawn that holds water and a
cedar hedge, and the other has slope and sun. A phone price for mosquito work is
a guess dressed as a quote, and the guess is usually wrong in whichever
direction favours the person who gave it.

What we will tell you on the phone is whether it sounds like a mosquito problem
at all. A surprising share of midge, crane fly and fungus gnat complaints arrive
as mosquito calls, and identification is free. If you have been bitten in a
pattern that does not fit mosquitoes, the honest answer may be
[bites but no bugs found](/services/bed-bug-control/bites-but-no-bugs-found/)
rather than a yard program.
