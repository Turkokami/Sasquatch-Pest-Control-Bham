## The Whatcom County rodent year

Ask most people why rats come indoors and they will say cold. Here that answer
is wrong, and getting it wrong leads to the wrong prevention.

We do not get a sustained hard freeze. What we get is roughly thirty-five inches
of rain, and the rodent calendar in this county runs on water rather than
temperature. Through the dry part of summer, Norway rats live outdoors
perfectly well — in blackberry, under decking, in woodpiles, along creek
corridors and greenbelts, in the field margins out toward Lynden and Everson.
Then the rain sets in around late September, the ground saturates, burrows
flood, and every one of those animals starts looking for somewhere dry.

Your crawlspace is dry. That is the whole story.

The consequence is that the useful window for rodent work is August and early
September, before the push. By November we are working a structure that already
has a population inside it, which is a longer and more expensive job than
closing the same gaps eight weeks earlier. It is the single piece of timing
advice worth acting on in this county.

## What you actually have

Correct identification changes the work, so it is worth being specific rather
than saying "rodents."

**Norway rat.** The dominant rat here by a wide margin. Heavy-bodied, blunt
nose, tail shorter than the body, and a burrower — which is why they turn up
under slabs, decking, sheds and in crawlspaces rather than in attics. Droppings
are large and blunt-ended, around three-quarters of an inch. If you have rats in
Bellingham, this is almost certainly what you have.

**Roof rat.** Present but far less common in this county. Slighter, pointed
nose, tail longer than the body, and a climber. When we do find them it is
usually in attics and upper structure rather than crawlspaces, and often on
properties with heavy tree contact to the roofline.

**House mouse.** Small, gray-brown, droppings the size of a rice grain with
pointed ends. Mice need very little — a quarter inch of gap and a few grams of
food a day — and they do not need to travel far, which is why a mouse problem
is often confined to one part of a house while a rat problem involves the whole
structure.

**Deer mouse.** White belly, distinctly bicolored tail, larger eyes and ears
than a house mouse. Common in rural and forest-edge properties: Sudden Valley,
the Lake Whatcom shoreline, Edgemoor, and the farm country north and east of the
city. Deer mice matter for one specific reason, covered below under health.

Telling the difference is not academic. Rats and mice respond to different trap
placements, different spacing and different bait. A trap line set for rats will
sit untouched in a house that has mice.

## How they are getting in

Every rodent job is really an exclusion job, and exclusion starts with knowing
where to look. On Whatcom County housing stock, the same short list accounts for
most of what we find.

**Foundation vents.** The single most common entry on older Bellingham homes.
Original screens rust out, get pushed in, or were replaced at some point with
hardware cloth too coarse to matter. In the prewar neighborhoods — Lettered
Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York, parts of Fairhaven and South Hill — this is
the first thing we check and frequently the whole answer.

**Post-and-pier and settled foundations.** A lot of pre-1940 housing here sits
on post-and-pier, and eighty-odd years of settlement opens gaps between the
foundation and the framing above it. These are hard to see from outside and
obvious from inside the crawlspace.

**Utility penetrations.** Water, gas, electrical, cable, and every line added
since. Almost nobody seals these properly, and a contractor running a new line
in 2019 had no reason to think about a rat. Look for the gap around the pipe,
not the pipe.

**Garage door corners.** The bottom corners of a sectional door almost never
seal, and a garage is usually connected to the rest of the structure.

**Soffit and roofline.** Where the roof meets a wall at a change of pitch there
is often a genuine hole. On newer hillside construction — Alabama Hill, Barkley,
Cordata, King Mountain — combined with tree or fence contact, this is how you
get animals in the attic rather than the crawlspace.

**Dryer and bath vents.** Plastic louvered covers break, and the duct behind
them is a sheltered highway.

**Crawlspace access doors.** Frequently warped, frequently propped, occasionally
missing.

The number to remember is a quarter inch. That is the figure in Bellingham's own
rental inspection checklist, which flags foundation openings larger than a
quarter inch as an infestation risk. If a code inspector is looking for it on a
rental, it is worth looking for on a house you own.

It is also the right number for rats, which surprises people, because every
extension source gives a larger figure for the animal itself. University of
Nebraska Extension, eXtension, UC IPM and WSU Extension all put rats at slightly
more than half an inch to pass through, and mice at slightly more than a
quarter. What reconciles the two is the framing Washington's Department of Fish
and Wildlife uses: a rat requires a quarter-inch hole to chew larger and squeeze
through. The exclusion threshold is not the dimension the animal fits through.
It is the dimension at which the animal can get its incisors around an edge and
begin removing material, and with rodent incisors growing continuously the
removal gets done. Half-inch mesh over a crawl vent stops a rat and passes a
mouse; quarter-inch mesh stops both and denies the rat the edge it needs. That
is why one figure covers all three species we work on here.

## Signs, and what each one tells you

**Droppings.** The most informative evidence on the property. Size tells you
species. Location tells you travel routes and where to set. Condition tells you
timing — fresh droppings are dark and pliable, old ones are gray, dry and
crumble under pressure. A crawlspace with only old droppings is a different
situation from one with fresh, and it changes what we recommend.

**Rub marks.** Rodents follow the same routes and carry oil and dirt in their
coats, leaving dark smudging along joists, pipe runs and wall bases. Rub marks
are how we find the highway rather than guessing at it.

**Gnawing.** Incisors grow continuously, so rodents gnaw constantly. Fresh
gnawing on wood is pale; older gnawing has weathered. Gnawed wiring is the one
that matters most, and it is the reason we take attic and crawlspace activity
seriously rather than treating it as a nuisance.

**Sound.** Scratching or scurrying overhead at night, usually just after dark
and again before dawn. Rats in a crawlspace are often heard as thumps rather
than scratching.

**Disturbed insulation.** Tunneling and flattened runs through batts, and in a
long-running infestation, insulation compressed and contaminated to the point
where it has lost most of its R-value.

**Pets.** A cat or dog fixating on one spot on a wall or ceiling is worth
believing. They are frequently right, and they are frequently the first sign.

## How we work a rodent job

**Inspection.** We get into the crawlspace and the attic. This is not optional
and it is not a walk around the outside of the house — the entries and the
evidence are inside those spaces, and a company quoting rodent work without
going into them is quoting a guess. We map entry points, activity, contamination
extent and the condition of the insulation.

**Exclusion.** Sealing every entry we found, using materials rodents cannot get
through: hardware cloth of the right gauge, sheet metal, mortar where
appropriate. Not expanding foam alone, which a rat will chew through in an
evening, and not steel wool by itself, which rusts out. This is the part of the
job that ends the problem, and it is where the money should go.

**Trapping.** Snap traps and multi-catch devices set to the mapped routes, in
the numbers the activity justifies. We return to check and reset, because
populations come out over days rather than at once. Trapping after exclusion —
not before — so we are not removing animals while the door is still open.

**Monitoring.** We keep the line running until it goes quiet, and quiet means
several visits with nothing, not one.

**Cleanup and restoration.** Droppings and contaminated material removed,
surfaces treated, and where insulation is compromised, removal and replacement.
This gets scheduled after trapping is complete, so we are not restoring a space
that is still occupied.

## How we use rodenticide, and what we choose

We run rodenticide programs where they are the right tool, and we are
deliberate about which products go into them. This is worth explaining, because
"do you use poison" is one of the first questions people ask and the honest
answer has more in it than yes or no.

**Secondary poisoning is the concern that drives the product choice.** The
classic problem with rodent bait is not the rodent — it is what eats the rodent
afterward. Older anticoagulant chemistries kill slowly, so a baited animal keeps
moving for days and a share of them die in the open, where they are taken by
barn owls, red-tailed hawks, coyotes and cats. Barn owls in particular are among
the most effective rodent control any rural Whatcom property has, and poisoning
them to kill their prey is a bad trade on a working farm.

So we select rodenticides formulated to limit or prevent that secondary
exposure, rather than defaulting to whatever is cheapest per bait block. That
choice costs slightly more per station and it protects the predators already
working your property for free.

**Placement is as important as product.** Every bait placement goes in a
tamper-resistant station, positioned along mapped runs rather than scattered,
and recorded so anyone auditing the site — or you, a year later — can see
exactly where devices sit and why.

**Interior work is trapping.** Bait belongs on the exterior and in stations. We
trap inside, because a rodent that dies in a wall void or under a floor becomes a
decomposition and blowfly problem in a place you cannot reach. Trapping puts the
animal somewhere we can remove it.

**And none of it substitutes for exclusion.** A bait program manages pressure on
a property; it does not stop animals entering a building. The quarter-inch gap is
still the thing that decides whether you have rodents indoors, which is why the
sequence on every job is exclusion first, trapping for what is already inside,
and bait where exterior or commercial pressure genuinely warrants it.

## The damage, and why waiting costs more

**Wiring.** Rodents gnaw insulation off conductors. This is the risk that
justifies acting quickly, and it is not theoretical.

**Insulation.** Beyond the tunneling, urine and droppings saturate batts and
destroy R-value. Attic insulation replacement is routinely the largest single
line on a rodent job, and it scales directly with how long the animals were
there.

**Contamination.** Rodents are incontinent as they travel, so a route is a
contaminated surface, and it is often above your ceiling or below your floor.

**Structure.** Long-running crawlspace infestations damage vapor barrier and
ductwork, and a torn duct in a contaminated crawlspace moves that air into the
house.

**Secondary pests.** Rodent nests support mites and dermestid beetles, and when
the rodents are removed or die, those move inward looking for somewhere else to
be.

## Health, stated plainly

Rodent droppings and urine carry pathogens, and the sensible response is
straightforward handling precautions rather than alarm.

The one worth naming specifically is hantavirus, which in Washington is carried
by the deer mouse. It is tempting to file that as a country problem, and the
state does not let you. The Washington State Department of Health states that
infected deer mice live throughout the state and that people are at risk for
hantavirus pulmonary syndrome in any part of Washington. What concentrates the
risk is not a map, it is a situation: a closed-up space holding rodent droppings
and nesting material that somebody then disturbs. Cabins, sheds, barns, garages,
stored vehicles and crawlspaces are the settings the guidance names, and a
detached garage behind a house in town qualifies as readily as a cabin at the
end of a gravel road.

Rarity is the other half of that picture and belongs in the same breath rather
than in a separate one. The department reports typically one to five cases a
year statewide, with about one in three of the people diagnosed having died. Its
surveillance guideline records 58 Washington cases in total through 2022, 20 of
them fatal, and approximately 70 percent reporting exposure in eastern
Washington. Severe consequence, low probability — which is exactly the
combination where following a method beats either panic or dismissal.

Transmission is by inhaling aerosolized material from dried droppings, urine and
nesting material, so the practical rule follows directly: **do not dry-sweep or
vacuum rodent droppings.** Ventilate the space first, wet the material down with
a disinfectant, use gloves and appropriate respiratory protection, and bag it.
The state's full procedure, down to soaking times and glove materials, is set
out on the [deer mouse](/pest-library/deer-mouse/) profile.

Leptospirosis is the other one worth knowing about, spread through contaminated
water and of most relevance where standing water and rodent activity overlap.

If any of that sounds like more than you want to take on, that is exactly why
cleanup is part of the job rather than something we hand back to you.

## Renting, or managing a rental

Rodents are the pest that most often turns into an argument about who pays, and
in Bellingham that argument has a legal shape worth knowing before you have it.

Washington's landlord-tenant law, RCW 59.18.060, puts responsibility for pest
control on the landlord — with a specific carve-out where the dwelling is a
single-family residence, or where the infestation was caused by the tenant. In a
multi-unit building the obligation generally sits with the owner. In a rented
house it frequently does not, which surprises tenants regularly.

Bellingham adds a second layer. Municipal code chapter 6.15 requires rental
properties to pass a certified inspection, and the checklist explicitly names
foundation openings larger than a quarter inch as an infestation risk. That
turns exclusion from good practice into a compliance item, and it means a
landlord who seals the crawlspace is buying two things at once.

We work with tenants, owners and property managers, and where the answer
depends on which of those you are, we will say so rather than quietly billing
whoever called.

## Commercial rodent work

Commercial accounts differ in what gets documented rather than what gets done.
Device placement is mapped so an auditor or inspector can see the layout without
taking anyone's word for it, every visit produces a record of findings and
actions, and the recommendations that sit on your side — a dock door that does
not seal, waste stored too close to the building, drainage keeping a slab damp —
go in writing rather than being mentioned in passing.

The pressure points are consistent across the county: loading docks and roll-up
doors, dumpster enclosures, utility chases between units, feed and grain storage
on agricultural sites, and gear storage around the marinas. In multifamily
buildings the shared wall and utility chase is the whole problem, because
treating one unit while the adjacent one is untouched simply relocates the
population.

## What it costs

We quote from the inspection rather than a price list, because the range is
genuinely wide. A mouse problem confined to one part of a house with two entry
points is a small job. A 1920s farmhouse with rats through the crawlspace,
forty feet of failed vent screening, and insulation that needs removing and
replacing is not.

What moves the number is access and extent rather than the animal: crawlspace
clearance, how much of the perimeter needs sealing, whether the siding is sound
enough to seal against, and how far the contamination has spread. You get the
figure in writing before anything starts, and the estimate is free.

## Keeping them out

Trim vegetation and tree limbs back from the roof and walls. Keep bark mulch
and soil below the siding line. Store feed, seed and pet food in sealed
containers rather than bags. Deal with fallen fruit. Keep firewood off the
ground and away from the house. Manage standing water and get downspouts
discharging away from the foundation. Check vent screens once a year — spring is
a good habit — and seal every new penetration the moment a contractor makes one.

None of that substitutes for exclusion on a house that already has a gap. It is
what keeps a sealed house sealed.
