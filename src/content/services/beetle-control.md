## Two problems on one page

The service covers two groups of insects that have nothing in common except that
both are source problems and both get treated wrong.

**Fabric pests** — carpet beetles and their dermestid relatives. The larvae feed on
keratin and other animal proteins: wool, silk, fur, feathers, leather, and
crucially, dead insects and animal remains.

**Pantry pests** — sawtoothed grain beetles, flour beetles, drugstore and cigarette
beetles, and the [Indianmeal moth](/services/moth-control/), which is a moth
rather than a beetle but behaves identically and turns up in the same cupboard.

In both cases the insects you notice are adults, the damage is done by larvae you
have not seen, and the entire job is finding where the larvae are eating.

## Carpet beetles: the larvae are the problem

**Adults** are small, rounded, and often patterned in mottled white, brown and
black — the varied carpet beetle is the common one here. They feed on pollen and
nectar outdoors, they are harmless, and they are attracted to light. That is why
you find them on windowsills and in light fittings.

**Larvae** are the damage. Small, tapered, densely covered in bristles, and often
called woolly bears. They avoid light and move away from disturbance, so they feed
in the places nobody looks.

They also **shed their skins repeatedly as they grow**, and those cast skins are
frequently the first real evidence — they look like tiny hollow versions of the
larva and accumulate wherever feeding has been happening. If you find cast skins,
you have found the site.

**What they eat** is animal protein. Wool carpets and rugs, wool clothing, felt,
silk, fur, feathers, leather, taxidermy, natural-bristle brushes, and pet hair
accumulations. They do not eat cotton, linen or synthetics — unless those are
soiled with sweat, food or body oils, which supplies what they need.

**Where they feed.** The edges of wool rugs, particularly under furniture that
never moves. Carpet under and behind beds and sofas. The back of closets. Stored
wool in boxes. Under baseboards where lint and pet hair accumulate. Inside heating
ducts. And in wall voids and soffits — covered next, because that is the one
people miss.

## The source nobody finds

If there is a carpet beetle problem and no obvious wool, the source is almost
always something dead.

Dermestid beetles are scavengers by trade. In nature they clean carcasses and
nests. In a building, the richest food available is frequently:

**A bird nest.** In a soffit, gable vent, dryer vent or eave. Nests contain
feathers, dropped food, insect remains and often dead nestlings, and a single
abandoned nest supports a carpet beetle population for years — feeding new adults
into the house every spring. This is why nest removal matters more than treatment. That work includes removing old nests rather than just closing the entry.

**A rodent nest or a dead rodent** in a wall void, under a floor or in an attic.
Same principle. See [rodent control](/services/rodent-control/).

**An accumulation of dead insects** in a wall cavity — which is exactly why we
refuse to treat overwintering
[stink bugs and cluster flies](/services/stink-bug-control/) inside wall voids. A
few hundred dead insects in a cavity is a dermestid buffet, and the beetles that
develop there move inward into the living space.

**A dead animal** anywhere in the structure, including a chimney.

So when a household has beetles and no wool, the useful question is not "what
fabric is being eaten" but "what died, and where." That reframing resolves a large
share of these calls.

## Carpet beetle or clothes moth?

The damage looks similar and the treatments differ, so the distinction matters.

**Carpet beetles** graze irregular patches, often thinning the pile rather than
making clean holes, and they will feed in relatively open and lit areas as well as
hidden ones. They leave cast skins and fecal pellets roughly the size of a grain
of salt. No webbing.

**Clothes moths** make clean holes, prefer genuinely dark and undisturbed places,
and leave **silk webbing** — tubes, mats, or small cases the larvae carry around.
Webbing is the give-away, and it is the single most reliable difference.

Both eat keratin. If you find webbing, see
[moth control](/services/moth-control/). If you find bristly larvae and cast
skins, you are on the right page.

## The rash that is not bites

Worth its own note, because it causes real distress and gets misdiagnosed
routinely.

Carpet beetle larvae carry fine bristles, and some people develop an itchy,
raised, bumpy rash from contact with them or with shed skins in bedding, carpet or
upholstery. It looks enough like insect bites that households conclude they have
bed bugs.

The distinction matters because the treatments are entirely different and a bed
bug job is far more disruptive and expensive. If you have "bites" with no bed bug
evidence — no live insects, no cast skins in seams, no fecal spotting on the
mattress — and you do have small beetles at windows or bristly larvae in carpet,
this is a likely explanation.

That is one reason we verify before treating for
[bed bugs](/services/bed-bug-control/) rather than booking off a description.

## Pantry beetles

Different insects, same logic: find the package.

**What they are.** Sawtoothed grain beetles, flour beetles, drugstore beetles and
cigarette beetles are the common ones. All small, brown, and capable of getting
into packaging that looks sealed — several can chew through paper, cellophane and
thin plastic, and they exploit the folded seams of a bag or box without needing
to chew at all.

**What they infest.** Flour, cereal, grains, rice, pasta, dried fruit, nuts,
spices, chocolate, tea, and dry pet food. Drugstore and cigarette beetles have
notoriously broad tastes — dried botanicals, potpourri, decorative seed items,
book bindings, and stored tobacco.

**Where the source hides.** The four we find most often, in order:

Pet food, in a large bag stored in a garage or utility room and rarely emptied to
the bottom.

Birdseed, same problem, and frequently the single largest reservoir on a property.

Decorative items — dried flower arrangements, seed pods, ornamental corn, potpourri
— which nobody thinks of as food and which are exactly that.

Spilled material in a cupboard seam or under a shelf liner, where a tablespoon of
flour supports a population indefinitely.

**Where it usually did not start.** Your housekeeping. Most pantry infestations
arrive already inside a package, having been introduced at a processing or storage
stage well before you bought it. That is worth saying, because people are
embarrassed about these calls and they should not be.

## Why we do not spray a pantry

Because the answer is removal, and product does not belong on food surfaces
anyway.

**Find the source.** Inspect everything, including the packages that look fine.
Look for small holes, webbing, live insects, and material that looks slightly
clumped or dusty. Anything infested goes out of the house, not into the kitchen
bin.

**Empty and clean.** Shelves out where possible, vacuum the seams, corners and
shelf-support holes — those small drilled holes in cabinet sides are a classic
harborage — then wipe down.

**Re-store properly.** Airtight glass, metal or rigid plastic containers rather
than original packaging. This alone prevents most recurrences, because it stops a
new introduction from spreading beyond its own package.

**Freeze new bulk goods.** Anything bought in bulk, particularly grains, nuts and
dried fruit, held in a freezer for a few days on arrival kills anything present.

**Then treat, narrowly.** Targeted crack and crevice application in the cabinet
structure — not on surfaces where food sits. In commercial settings, pheromone
monitors tell us whether the population is actually falling.

If someone proposes spraying your food storage, that is worth declining.

## How we work a carpet beetle job

**Confirm what it is.** Bristly larvae and cast skins, or webbing. The difference
sends the job in two directions.

**Find the feeding site, not the adults.** Windowsill adults are the symptom.
We work outward from where larvae and cast skins actually accumulate — rug edges
under furniture, closet floors, under and behind fixed furniture, baseboard lines,
heating vents.

**Look up if there is no fabric.** Soffits, eaves, attic spaces, wall voids and
chimneys, for the nest or the accumulation that is really feeding the population.
This is the step that resolves the cases nobody else has managed to.

**Clean thoroughly.** Vacuuming is a substantial part of the treatment, because it
physically removes larvae, cast skins, eggs and the lint and hair that feed them.
Emptied outside afterwards.

**Treat harborage.** Carpet edges, under fixed furniture, baseboard voids, closet
structure. Not on textiles, and not broadcast across floors people live on.

**Deal with the structural source.** Nest removal and
[exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) where a bird or rodent nest is
involved, because closing the entry without removing the nest leaves the food in
place.

**Follow up.** Dermestid life cycles are long — larvae can develop over many months
depending on conditions — so a single clean visit is not proof. We check rather
than assume.

## Preventing both

**For fabric pests:** clean wool and natural fibers before storing, keep stored
textiles in sealed containers rather than cardboard, vacuum under and behind
furniture that rarely moves, and deal with pet hair and lint accumulation along
baseboards and in vents. Where a wool rug sits under a heavy piece of furniture,
lifting and vacuuming it once a year is worth more than any product.

**For pantry pests:** airtight containers as standard rather than original
packaging, first-in first-out rotation, freezing bulk purchases on arrival, and
periodic inspection of the things nobody inspects — pet food, birdseed, decorative
botanicals, and the back of the top shelf.

**And for both:** keep birdseed and bulk pet food out of the house entirely if you
can, in a sealed bin in an outbuilding. It is the single most effective change
available on most properties, and it addresses both problems at once.

## Wood-boring beetles are a different page

Worth separating clearly, because "beetle in the house" covers two unrelated
situations and only one of them is on this page.

If you are finding **small round exit holes in timber** — joists, subfloor, sill
plates, furniture — with fine powder beneath them, that is a wood-boring beetle
rather than a carpet or pantry beetle. In Whatcom County the common one is the
anobiid powderpost beetle, and it is genuinely common in damp crawlspaces here.

The distinction matters because the driver is moisture rather than food storage
or fabric, and the fix is drying the space rather than cleaning a cupboard. See
[termite and wood-destroying pest control](/services/termite-control/) and
[crawlspace restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/).

**Ground beetles, click beetles and June beetles** turning up indoors are a third
category again — outdoor insects that wandered in, breed nowhere in the house, and
generally need nothing beyond a door sweep and a vacuum.

## Timing and life cycle

Both groups have long, temperature-dependent development, and that shapes what to
expect.

**Carpet beetles** can take many months to develop from egg to adult, and longer
in a cool space. Adults emerge and become obvious in **spring and early summer**,
which is when most of these calls arrive — people see the adults at windows and
assume the problem is new, when the larvae have been feeding since the previous
year. Because the cycle is long, confirming a clear result takes months rather
than weeks.

**Pantry beetles** move faster in a warm kitchen — weeks rather than months — which
is why an infestation in a warm cupboard escalates quickly and why removing the
source produces a fast improvement. Activity is year-round indoors, with a
noticeable rise in warm weather.

The practical implication for both: judge success by monitoring and by absence of
new damage over a season, not by whether anyone has seen an insect this week.

## Commercial and institutional

Both groups turn up in specific commercial settings, and the answer is almost
always monitoring plus stock and storage discipline rather than application.

**Food storage, groceries and bakeries.** Pantry beetles arrive in deliveries.
First-in first-out rotation, inspection at receiving, and pheromone monitoring in
storage areas do more than any treatment. Where a persistent population exists,
the source is usually a spill behind a rack or a forgotten pallet rather than the
visible stock.

**Restaurants.** Dry storage and the cabinet structure around it, plus the same
receiving discipline.

**Museums, collections and historic buildings.** Dermestids are the classic
collections pest, and here the tolerance is close to zero — a beetle population in
a textile or natural history collection is a conservation emergency rather than a
nuisance. The approach is environmental control, sealed storage and monitoring,
with treatment kept away from the objects.

**Hotels and senior living.** Carpet beetles in carpet edges and stored linens,
where the population is frequently fed by lint accumulation rather than by
anything visible.

For all of these the deliverable is the record as much as the result. See
[commercial programs](/commercial/).

## Before you call

**Keep a specimen.** A beetle or larva in a small sealed bag, or a clear
photograph next to something for scale, settles the identification immediately.

**Note where the larvae are**, not where the adults are. Cast skins in a rug edge
tell us far more than beetles on a windowsill.
