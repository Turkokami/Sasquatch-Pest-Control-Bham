## What this service actually is

Two things, in this order: working out what the habitat is doing, and then
trapping what is already there.

That order is not modesty, and it is not an attempt to talk you out of a job.
It is what the evidence supports. [UC IPM](https://ipm.ucanr.edu/PMG/PESTNOTES/pn7439.html)
and [WSU Extension](https://pubs.extension.wsu.edu/product/vole-management-in-home-backyards-and-gardens-home-garden-series/)
both put the effective measures against voles in cultural and physical control —
mowing, mulch depth, weed control, clear circles around trunks, physical guards —
and not one of those is a pesticide application.

So a vole visit from us is a walk round the property with somebody who can tell
a vole runway from a mole run, a list of the things keeping the population fed
and hidden, and a trapping program on the runways and the margins where that is
worth doing. If the habitat work alone would settle it, you will hear that
instead of a quote.

This reverses what this site used to say. Until November 2026
[our vole page](/pest-library/vole/) ran to nearly four thousand words
explaining that vole damage was a horticultural problem rather than a structural
pest one and that we did not take it on. The correction is recorded there too,
and the reason is worth knowing: the job records disagreed with the page.
Twelve vole jobs across 2023 to 2025, eleven of them in 2025, and a live vole
line on the service list. The work was happening while the website declined it.

## Vole or mole, because the answer decides everything

We take on vole work. We do not do mole work. So this is the first question and
it is not a technicality.

**Voles eat plants.** They leave narrow surface runways an inch or two wide
under the grass, worn to bare or matted soil, connecting small open holes. In the
runways you will find cut grass clippings left lying, and greenish droppings
about three-sixteenths of an inch long. The damage is to the plants: bark gnawed
in short crossed grooves at the base of shrubs and young trees, bulbs that never
come up, hostas grazed to the crown.

**Moles eat worms.** Oregon State Extension is explicit that moles hunt worms,
grubs and insects rather than eating plants. The sign is soil: rounded,
volcano-shaped mounds thrown up from below, and raised ridges where a tunnel
runs shallow. The plants are usually untouched and merely disturbed.

The short version: **a lawn full of soil heaps with healthy plants is moles. A
lawn with runways and chewed plants is voles.**

Both can be on the same property at once, and voles will happily use a mole run
as a highway, which is exactly how each gets blamed for the other's work. If you
are not sure, send a photograph. Identification costs nothing and it is usually
settled in a couple of minutes, and it saves you paying somebody to solve the
wrong animal.

## What does most of the work, and it is not us

Four things, roughly in order of how much they matter.

**Mow the margin, and keep mowing it.** Nearly every residential lot in this
county has reservoir habitat within a few yards — a field edge, a greenbelt
boundary, a drainage ditch, a creek corridor, the untended strip along a fence
line. That is where voles live in numbers, and a mown lawn beside unmown cover
is a lawn that keeps being restocked. Cutting that margin and keeping it cut
does more over a season than any treatment.

**Pull the mulch back off the trunks.** A deep mulch ring against a trunk is
cover, food access and concealment in one. A clear circle of bare soil around the
base of every young tree and shrub removes the thing that makes trunk gnawing
safe for them.

**Guard the young trees before winter, not after.** Trunk guards on young and
thin-barked trees, set slightly into the soil and tall enough to cover the snow
line. This is the one that has to be done in advance, because by the time you can
see the girdling the tree has already taken it.

**Take away the overhead cover.** Long grass, dense ground cover and weed mats
give voles continuous protection from hawks and owls. Thinning it changes the
arithmetic of living there.

None of that requires us. We publish it because a customer who does the habitat
work and then buys trapping gets a result that lasts, and a customer who buys
trapping alone is buying it again next year.

## Why the damage appears all at once, and why the calls do not

A genuine oddity in this work, and our own records show both halves of it.

The damage is done through winter. Voles feed at the base of trunks under matted
grass, heavy mulch and whatever snow we get, and UC IPM notes that snow cover
lets gnawing extend a foot or more up a trunk. They are working in a covered
corridor where nothing can see them, for months.

Then the grass lifts in spring and it all appears in a week. The lawn looks
scored and thinned across a wide area, the tulips do not come up, and a shrub
that was fine in October is girdled all the way round. Spring reveals the
damage; it does not cause it.

But the phone rings later. In our own job records for 2023 through 2025, vole
calls run **June through December** — a couple in June, July and August, the
most in September, then trailing away through the autumn — and **none at all
between January and May**. People call when they are out in the garden using it,
not when the damage was being done, and not even in the month they first saw it.

Two practical consequences. The guards and the clear circles need to go on in
autumn, before the winter they are meant to survive. And if you are reading this
in February with a lawn full of runways, that is the best possible month to deal
with the habitat, because the population is about to breed into a season of
cover you could remove now.

That job figure is twelve over three years, which is a small number and we are
not going to dress it up as a trend. It is below the floor we use for charting
anything on the [Whatcom County pest calendar](/guides/whatcom-county-pest-calendar/),
so it appears there in prose rather than as a curve. It is reported here because
it is first-party and because the pattern was consistent, not because twelve
jobs make a statistic.

## Why this county suits them

Townsend's vole is the species west of the Cascades, and the conditions here are
close to ideal for it.

Mild wet winters mean the grass never really stops, so the food supply and the
cover persist through the months that would thin a population further east.
There is no long continuous snowpack here, which caps the worst of the
above-trunk girdling you see in colder regions, but it also means no hard reset.
And the landscape is a patchwork of pasture margins, ditches, hedgerows, berry
ground and greenbelt — reservoir habitat laced through residential development
at close range.

Vole populations also cycle, which is why some years are much worse than others
and why a quiet decade is no guarantee. A year when the numbers are high in the
reservoir habitat is a year when the margin of your lawn matters more than
usual.

## What we will not sell you

**A broadcast lawn treatment.** There is no product application across a lawn
that constitutes vole control, and if you have been quoted one, ask what is being
applied and what its label says about voles specifically. Under federal pesticide
law a product has to be used and described consistently with its labeling, and
"it will help with the voles too" is not a label.

**Mole work, bundled in.** It is a different animal doing a different thing and
it is outside what we do. If you have both, we will say so and handle our half.

**A program for an orchard or a nursery block we are not the right outfit for.**
At that scale this becomes a management question — mowing regimes between rows,
weed control, guards on everything young — and a landscape contractor or an
agricultural adviser will often serve you better. We will tell you that at the
visit rather than take the work and under-deliver on it.

**Repeat trapping with the habitat untouched.** We would rather lose the
recurring revenue than put somebody on a schedule that exists because nobody
mowed the margin.
