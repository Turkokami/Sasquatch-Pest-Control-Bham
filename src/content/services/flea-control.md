## The pupal stage explains everything

Almost every frustrating flea story comes down to one biological detail, and once
you know it the whole job makes sense.

A flea goes through egg, larva, pupa and adult. The adults you see are a small
fraction of what is in the house — most of the population is eggs and larvae down
in carpet fibers, floor cracks, pet bedding and upholstery.

The **pupa** is the problem. It spins a sticky cocoon that picks up dust and
debris, camouflaging it and — crucially — making it resistant to most of what can
be applied. Inside, a fully formed adult can wait for weeks until something tells
it a host has arrived: warmth, carbon dioxide, and physical vibration.

So here is what happens on almost every job. We treat. Adults and larvae die. For
about a week the house seems clear. Then the pupae that survived begin hatching,
and the household concludes the treatment failed.

It did not. That emergence is the expected next act, and it is why **a follow-up
visit is part of the plan from the start** rather than a rescue. Any company that
treats once and tells you that is the end of it either does not know this or is
not telling you.

## Why the vacuum matters more than you would think

This is the part homeowners can do that genuinely changes the outcome.

Vacuuming physically removes eggs, larvae and the dried blood residue that larvae
feed on. But the more useful effect is that the **vibration triggers pupae to
emerge early**. An adult that hatches into a treated environment is dealt with. An
adult that waits three more weeks in its cocoon is not.

So the instruction is: vacuum daily for at least two weeks after treatment, and
focus on where pets rest, along baseboards, under furniture and in the seams of
upholstery — not the middle of the floor, where fleas do not develop. Empty the
canister or remove the bag after each session and take it out of the house.

Wash pet bedding in hot water and dry it hot. The dryer does the work.

## What you actually have

**Cat flea.** Effectively all of it. Despite the name, the cat flea is the species
found on dogs, cats and wildlife alike across this region, and it is what we are
dealing with in nearly every residential job.

Dog fleas and human fleas exist and are rare enough here that if someone tells you
that is what you have, it is worth a second look.

The other insects that get confused with fleas are springtails — tiny, jumping,
and associated with damp, appearing in bathrooms and around houseplants — and
carpet beetle larvae, which cause skin irritation that gets read as bites. Both
are common and neither needs flea treatment.

## Where they come from

**Pets are the obvious route**, and the pattern is that a pet picks up fleas
outdoors, the adults feed and lay, and the eggs fall off wherever the animal
spends time. Eggs are not sticky; they drop into carpet, bedding and floor
cracks. The pet is the delivery mechanism, the house is the nursery.

**Wildlife is the one people miss.** A flea problem in a house with no pets is
almost always an animal problem in the structure. Raccoons or opossums denning
under a deck or in a crawlspace, rodents in a wall void or attic, or a feral cat
using the space under a porch. When the animal leaves — or dies — its fleas need a
new host, and they come inward.

That case is not really a flea job. It is an animal-removal job with a flea treatment attached — [rodent control](/services/rodent-control/) where it is a rodent, and a wildlife specialist where it is not — and
treating the carpet while an animal is still denning under the floor guarantees a
repeat.

**The vacant property.** Buying or renting a house that has been empty is the
other classic. Pupae wait, and the warmth, carbon dioxide and footfall of people
moving in triggers mass emergence within days. It feels like the house was
infested and hidden; what actually happened is that a dormant population got the
signal it was waiting for.

**Visiting animals** account for a smaller share — a friend's dog, a pet sitter,
or a cat that comes and goes.

## The pet side, which is not ours

We treat structures. We do not treat animals, and we would be wrong to.

Flea products for pets are a veterinary matter. The active ingredients, the
dosing by weight, the species restrictions and the interactions are your vet's
call, not a pest control company's. That is not caution for its own sake:
**several products that are safe for dogs are seriously harmful to cats**, and
misapplied over-the-counter treatments cause real veterinary emergencies every
year.

What matters practically is that both halves have to happen. Treating the house
while the pet stays untreated means the pet re-seeds the house continuously.
Treating the pet while the house stays untreated means the pet gets re-infested
from the carpet. Either alone fails, and the two should happen close together.

So the honest sequence is: talk to your vet, book us, and try to get the pet
treatment and the house treatment within a few days of each other.

## How we work a flea job

**Confirm it is fleas.** Springtails and carpet beetle larvae are the common
false alarms, and neither responds to this treatment.

**Find the source.** If there is no pet, we are looking for an animal — crawlspace,
attic, under a deck, in a wall void. That changes the job and it is the first
question rather than the last.

**Identify the focus areas.** Where the pet rests, along baseboards, under
furniture, in upholstery seams. Treatment concentrates where the life cycle is
happening rather than across open floor.

**Treat with an adulticide and a growth regulator.** The regulator is the part
that matters over time — it prevents eggs and larvae from developing into
breeding adults, which is what breaks the cycle rather than just reducing the
current generation.

**Exterior where warranted.** Shaded, protected areas where a pet rests outdoors,
and around crawlspace access and deck voids where wildlife has been. Open sunny
lawn does not support fleas and does not need treating.

**Schedule the follow-up.** Timed to the pupal emergence, not to whether anyone
has complained.

**Give you the vacuum schedule**, because the household half of this genuinely
determines the result.

## Preparation

Before treatment, and it makes a real difference.

Vacuum thoroughly and remove the bag or empty the canister outside. Wash all pet
bedding hot and dry it hot. Clear the floor — toys, shoes, clothing, anything
under beds and furniture — so treatment reaches where the larvae are. Pick up pet
food and water bowls. Cover or remove fish tanks and turn off their air pumps.
Plan for people and pets to be out for the period we specify, and to keep off
treated surfaces until they are dry.

Then the vacuuming continues daily for at least two weeks afterward. That is not
housekeeping advice; it is part of the treatment.

## The seasonal pattern here

Our climate keeps flea activity going longer than a colder region would, but
there is still a shape to the year.

Activity builds through **late spring and summer** as temperatures rise, with the
peak in **late summer and early autumn** — which is also when wildlife pressure on
structures is climbing. Most of our flea calls arrive between August and October.

**Autumn** brings the wildlife-source cases, as animals move into crawlspaces and
deck voids ahead of winter and bring fleas with them.

**Winter** is quieter outdoors, but indoor populations continue unaffected —
heated houses do not have a flea season. Pupae from autumn can also carry through
and emerge when activity in the house picks up.

**Spring** is the vacant-property season, as sales and rentals turn over and
dormant populations meet new occupants.

## What does not work

Worth listing, because the flea aisle is full of products that sell well and
perform badly.

**Foggers and bug bombs.** They deposit on horizontal surfaces and do not reach
down into carpet fibers, floor cracks or upholstery seams where the larvae and
pupae actually are. They also do nothing about the pupal stage, which is the part
that determines whether the problem returns.

**Flea collars alone.** Whatever the pet is wearing, the eggs already in the
carpet continue developing. Collars are a veterinary decision anyway.

**Salt and diatomaceous earth on carpet.** Both are widely recommended online.
Both work far more slowly than the population reproduces, and both are difficult
to remove from carpet afterward. If you want to use DE, the sensible places are
crawlspaces and voids rather than living space.

**Ultrasonic repellers.** No.

**Treating the lawn.** Fleas do not develop in open, sunny grass — it is too dry
and too hot at the surface. Treating a lawn is money spent where the insects are
not. Shaded, protected spots where a pet rests, and voids where wildlife dens,
are the outdoor areas that matter.

**One treatment and nothing else.** The most common failure. Without the follow-up
and without the vacuuming, the pupal generation restarts the whole thing.

## If there is no pet in the house

Worth separating out, because it changes the job entirely and it is a case we see
often around the forest edge and in older housing.

Fleas need a warm-blooded host. If there is no pet, something else has been living
in or under the structure — and it is frequently something that has recently left
or died, which is precisely why the fleas came looking indoors.

The sequence in that case is: find the animal, resolve it, close the access, then
treat. Treating first produces a few good weeks and then a repeat, because the
source is still under the floor.

Common sources here are rodents in a wall void or crawlspace, raccoons or
opossums under a deck or porch, a feral cat using a crawlspace, and occasionally
squirrels in an attic. The relevant pages are [rodent control](/services/rodent-control/) and [exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/). Where the animal is wildlife rather than a rodent, removing it is a specialist's job rather than ours.

If you have no pet and a flea problem, tell us that on the phone. It is the single
most useful thing you can say, because it redirects the whole visit.

## The life cycle, briefly

Worth having the shape of it, because every decision on this page follows from it.

**Eggs.** Laid on the host, but not sticky, so they fall off within hours wherever
the animal goes. This is why the pet's resting spots matter far more than the
middle of a room.

**Larvae.** Blind, avoid light, and burrow down into carpet fibers, floor cracks
and upholstery. They feed on organic debris and on adult flea droppings — dried
blood — rather than biting anything. Because they move away from light, surface
treatment that does not penetrate misses them entirely.

**Pupae.** The armored waiting stage described at the top of this page, and the
reason for the follow-up visit.

**Adults.** Emerge in response to a host cue, feed within a day, and begin laying
shortly after. The adults you see are a small share of the total population — most
of what is in the house is in the three stages you cannot see.

The practical consequence is that a treatment aimed only at adults reduces the
visible problem and leaves the reservoir intact. That is why the growth regulator
matters more than the adulticide over the course of the job.

## Fleas and your yard

A short section, because most of the outdoor advice circulating on this subject is
wrong.

Fleas do not develop in open, sunny lawn. Surface temperatures are too high and
conditions are too dry for larvae, which need shade, humidity and organic debris.
Blanket-treating a lawn is money spent where the insects are not.

What does matter outdoors is the specific protected spots: under decks and
porches, in crawlspace access areas, beneath shrubs where a dog lies in summer,
in the shaded strip along a north-facing foundation, and anywhere wildlife has
been denning. Those are shaded, humid, and directly connected to a host.

Clearing leaf litter and keeping vegetation off the foundation helps. Closing deck
and crawlspace voids helps more, because it removes the wildlife that brought the
fleas.

## Landlords, tenants and vacant units

Fleas come up more often in rentals than most pests, and the responsibility
question follows a predictable pattern.

**Between tenancies** is where the vacant-property emergence happens. A unit that
housed a pet, then stood empty for weeks, then received new occupants produces
mass emergence within days of move-in. The new tenant reasonably believes the unit
was infested when they took it. In a sense it was — the population was dormant and
invisible.

**The cheap fix is treatment at turnover**, while the unit is empty and access is
unrestricted. It costs less than treating around an occupant, and it prevents the
complaint entirely.

**On responsibility**, Washington's landlord-tenant law, RCW 59.18.060, generally
places pest control on the landlord in multi-unit buildings, with a carve-out for
single-family residences and for infestations the tenant caused. A dormant
population inherited from a previous tenancy is not usually the new tenant's
doing, and we will say so plainly rather than billing whoever called.

For property managers running several units, treatment at turnover as standard
practice is substantially cheaper over a year than reacting to move-in complaints.
