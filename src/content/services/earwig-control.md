## They live outside, and that is the whole point

Earwigs are outdoor animals that occasionally end up indoors by accident.

They need consistent moisture — enough that a dry heated house kills them within
days. They do not breed indoors, they do not feed on anything in your house, and
they do not establish populations in wall voids the way a genuine indoor pest
does. Every earwig you find in a bathroom came in from outside within the last day
or two.

That single fact reorganizes the whole job. Interior spraying treats insects that
were going to die anyway and does nothing about the population producing them.
What works is changing the conditions in the first few feet around your foundation
and closing the routes in.

The same is true of **sowbugs and pillbugs**, which turn up in the same places for
the same reasons and are covered on this page because the answer is identical.

## What they actually are

**European earwig.** Introduced, long established, and the dominant species in
this region — reddish-brown, flattened, with the distinctive forceps at the rear.
Males have curved pincers, females straighter ones.

They are nocturnal. During the day they wedge themselves into tight, dark, humid
spaces, which is why turning over a board or lifting a pot produces a sudden
scatter. At night they come out to feed.

**The pincers** are used in defense and in interactions with other earwigs.
Against a person, a large one can produce a pinch you would notice and nothing
more. No venom, no sting, no medical relevance.

**The ear thing is folklore.** It has no basis. They are not drawn to ears, they
do not burrow into them, and the name is simply very old.

**Sowbugs and pillbugs** are not insects at all — they are terrestrial crustaceans,
with gill-like breathing structures that require constant humidity. Pillbugs roll
into a ball when disturbed; sowbugs do not. Both eat decaying plant material and
neither damages anything indoors.

One genuinely interesting thing about earwigs: the female guards her eggs and
tends the newly hatched young, which is unusual among insects and has nothing to
do with pest control but is worth knowing.

## Where they live, in the first three feet

Almost every earwig problem is a perimeter problem, and the population is in a
narrow band around the building.

**Bark mulch and compost** held against the foundation. This is the single biggest
factor on most properties — deep mulch right up to the siding is close to ideal
earwig habitat, and it is standard landscaping practice.

**Leaf litter** in beds and against walls.

**Under anything lying on the ground.** Boards, bricks, pavers, stacked pots,
firewood, landscape fabric, children's toys, a tarp that has been there since
spring.

**Dense low ground cover** — ivy, vinca, thick low plantings — which holds humidity
at soil level all day.

**Under siding at grade**, in the gap behind trim, and in the joints of retaining
walls.

**Damp crawlspaces**, which in this county are frequently continuous with the
outdoor habitat through unscreened vents.

Move any of those and you will find them. That is not a sign of infestation; it is
what the outdoors looks like in a wet climate.

## How they get in

They are following moisture and dark, and they are small and flat.

Under exterior doors with worn or missing sweeps. Through sliding door tracks.
Around window frames at ground level. Through unscreened or damaged crawlspace and
foundation vents. Around utility penetrations. Up through gaps at the sill plate.
And directly through open doors on summer evenings, drawn by porch lights.

Indoors they head for the humid rooms — bathrooms, laundry, kitchens, and
basements — because those are the only places that suit them, and it is why the
same two rooms produce all the sightings.

## The garden question

Worth being even-handed about, because earwigs are genuinely both.

**Useful.** They are omnivores and significant predators of aphids, mites, insect
eggs and other soft-bodied pests. A garden with earwigs has fewer aphids, and in
some horticultural systems they are actively valued.

**Damaging.** They also chew seedlings, soft fruit, flower petals and foliage. In
this county the recurring complaints are dahlias, strawberries, lettuce and other
tender greens, and corn silk.

The damage pattern is distinctive: irregular, ragged holes chewed through leaves
and petals, appearing overnight, often with small dark droppings nearby. It gets
attributed to slugs regularly. Slug damage comes with slime trails and earwig
damage does not, which usually settles it.

Where earwigs are damaging a specific crop, the useful response is targeted rather
than broad. Rolled damp newspaper or a length of hose left near the affected
plants overnight gives them somewhere to hide by morning, and can be emptied. A
shallow container of oil sunk to soil level works on the same principle. Both are
better than treating a whole garden for an insect that is also eating your aphids.

## What actually works

**Pull the mulch back.** A clear, dry band of six inches to a foot between mulch
or soil and the siding removes the habitat immediately against the building. This
is the highest-value change on most properties and it costs nothing but an
afternoon.

**Lift what is lying on the ground.** Boards, pots, bricks, tarps, firewood — off
the soil, off the wall, or gone.

**Thin the ground cover** immediately against the foundation. Ivy and dense low
planting against a wall holds moisture all day.

**Fix the water.** Gutters clear, downspouts discharging well away from the
foundation, irrigation not spraying the siding, and grading that moves water away
rather than holding it against the building.

**Close the gaps.** Door sweeps, weatherstripping, window and vent screening, and
sealing at utility penetrations — which is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work and reduces several
other problems at the same time.

**Deal with the crawlspace** if it is damp and open, because it is functionally
part of the outdoor habitat. See
[crawlspace restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/).

**Change the lighting.** White exterior lights near doors draw insects, and where
insects gather so do the things that eat them. Warm-toned bulbs or moving fixtures
away from entries makes a visible difference.

**Then treat, at the perimeter.** A targeted exterior band and harborage
application does help, particularly in a heavy year, and it works far better once
the habitat has been reduced. Interior treatment is rarely warranted.

A word on diatomaceous earth, since it is the thing most people have already
tried by the time they call. It works by abrading the waxy layer on an insect's
cuticle so that the animal dries out, which means it only does anything while it
is itself dry. Put down around a foundation here it is useful for as long as the
weather stays fine, and after the first real rain it cakes and stops working.
In a dry crawlspace or under a deck it keeps earning its place. Broadcast over
an open mulch bed in a Whatcom County October it is dust you paid for.

## Why we do not spray indoors for these

Because the insects indoors are already dying.

An earwig in your bathroom has left the conditions it needs and has a very short
future regardless of what anyone applies. Treating interior baseboards puts
product where people live in order to kill insects that were not going to survive
the week, and it does nothing to the population outside that keeps supplying more.

A vacuum handles the ones you find. The perimeter work handles the reason they
keep appearing.

## The year in earwigs

**Late winter.** Overwintered females are underground in nest chambers tending
eggs. Nothing is visible above ground and nobody calls.

**Early spring.** Eggs hatch, young emerge, and the population begins building.
This is the cheapest point of the year to change the habitat, because clearing
mulch and lifting debris in March removes shelter before numbers develop.

**Late spring and early summer.** Growth. Numbers climb steadily, garden damage
begins on tender growth, and the first indoor sightings appear.

**Midsummer through early autumn.** Peak. Adults are numerous, active every night,
and this is when almost all the indoor calls arrive. Also peak garden damage —
dahlias, strawberries, lettuce and corn silk.

**Autumn.** Numbers fall as conditions cool and adults die off. Survivors move
down into soil and protected spaces.

**Winter.** Dormant underground.

The pattern that matters: the population is set in spring by how much damp shelter
exists around the building, and experienced in August by whoever lives there.
Habitat work in March costs an afternoon; treatment in August is managing a
population that was already decided.

## Sowbugs and pillbugs, specifically

They get their own note because the reassurance is different.

They are crustaceans — closer relatives of crabs than of insects — and they breathe
through structures that must stay moist. That is why they die quickly indoors and
why they are found under exactly the same boards, pots and mulch as earwigs.

They eat decaying plant material and are genuinely useful in a compost heap or a
garden bed, breaking down organic matter. Occasionally they will graze seedlings
or soft fruit lying on wet soil, which is the only garden complaint worth taking
seriously and it is minor.

Indoors they do nothing at all. They do not bite, damage, contaminate or breed.
They are simply in the wrong place and dying.

So the honest answer for a household finding a few pillbugs in a bathroom or
garage is: vacuum them, pull the mulch back from the wall, fit a door sweep, and
nothing else is warranted. If a company proposes a treatment program for pillbugs,
that is worth questioning.

## Why the mulch matters so much

Worth expanding, because it is the single most actionable thing on this page and
because the advice runs against standard landscaping practice.

Bark mulch does exactly what it is meant to: it holds moisture in the soil and
moderates temperature. Held against a building it also creates a permanently damp,
dark, insulated strip at ground level directly against the siding — which is
precisely the habitat earwigs, sowbugs, pillbugs, millipedes and Oriental
cockroaches all require.

Landscapers install it to the wall because it looks finished. Nobody is doing
anything wrong; the consequence is simply not part of the design conversation.

**What to do instead.** Keep a clear band of six inches to a foot between mulch
and siding, using gravel, bare soil or nothing. It looks slightly less tidy for
about a week and then reads as a normal edge. It also keeps siding out of contact
with damp material, which matters for
[carpenter ants, moisture ants and dampwood termites](/services/termite-control/)
far more than it matters for earwigs.

**The same applies to firewood**, which should be off the ground and away from the
house rather than stacked against a wall, and to landscape fabric, which holds a
humid layer beneath it indefinitely.

If a property does one thing from this website, this is a strong candidate. It
reduces six or seven different problems at once and costs an afternoon.

## Commercial and multifamily

Earwigs come up in specific commercial contexts and the answer is the same
perimeter logic at a different scale.

**Apartment and condominium complexes** with extensive landscaped beds against
ground-floor units. Residents on the ground floor get all the complaints, and the
cause is the landscaping specification rather than anything in the units. Treating
unit by unit is expensive and ineffective; adjusting the beds and treating the
perimeter is neither.

**Food service with outdoor seating**, where the planters and the damp beneath
decking supply the population.

**Institutional and municipal buildings** with mature planting held to the
foundation.

**Greenhouses and growing operations**, where the conditions are deliberately
what earwigs want and the management is about exclusion from specific crops rather
than elimination.

For all of these, the useful arrangement is a spring perimeter program timed
before the population builds rather than reactive treatment in August. See
[commercial programs](/commercial/).

## What to do before we come

**Note which rooms.** Bathrooms, laundry and kitchen means humidity is the driver.
A bedroom on an upper floor suggests something different and is worth mentioning.

**Note when.** Overnight appearances are normal for a nocturnal insect. Daytime
activity in numbers suggests a large population immediately outside.

**Look outside at night with a torch**, an hour after dark, at the mulch beds
against the house. If the ground is moving, that is the population and that is
where the work goes.

**Leave the perimeter as it is** so we can see what is there. Clearing everything
the day before makes the assessment less useful.

## Traps, and what they are good for

Worth a short note because the internet is full of earwig trap advice and most of
it is aimed at the wrong problem.

Traps work on earwigs, genuinely. Rolled damp newspaper, a length of old hose, or
a flowerpot stuffed with straw and inverted on a cane all exploit the same
instinct — a tight, dark, humid space to spend the day in. Set them near the
affected plants in the evening and empty them in the morning.

What they are good for is **protecting a specific plant or bed**. What they will
not do is reduce a property-wide population, because you are competing with every
board, pot and inch of mulch already available.

So they belong in a garden, targeted at the dahlias or the strawberry row, rather
than as a control method for a house.
