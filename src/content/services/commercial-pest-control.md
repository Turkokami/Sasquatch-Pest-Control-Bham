## What a commercial program actually is

The distinction worth understanding before comparing quotes: a commercial pest
program is not the residential service performed more frequently. It is a
different product.

**The service is documented rather than performed.** Every visit produces a record
of what was inspected, what was found, what was done, and what needs attention on
your side. That record is the thing an inspector, an auditor or an insurer asks
for, and it is the reason the program exists in the form it does.

**Devices are mapped rather than placed.** Monitors and stations have numbered
positions on a site map, so anyone can see the layout without taking a
technician's word for it, and so trend data means something over time.

**The scope covers what a home program never touches.** Loading docks and roll-up
doors, waste enclosures and the pad beneath them, utility chases between units,
product and feed storage, floor drains, mechanical rooms, and the exterior
perimeter as a system rather than a wall.

**And it runs on your schedule.** For food service, retail and healthcare that
usually means before opening or after close, and it is set at the start rather
than negotiated every visit.

## Integrated pest management, in practice

The term gets used loosely, so here is what it actually means on a site rather
than in a brochure.

**Inspection and identification first.** What is present, where, and in what
numbers. Species drives everything downstream, and a program built on "we spray
the perimeter monthly" is not a program.

**Monitoring with thresholds.** Devices placed to tell us whether pressure is
rising or falling, and an agreed level of activity that triggers action.
Monitoring is what makes the difference between responding to a complaint and
seeing a trend three weeks earlier.

**Non-chemical measures before chemical ones.** Exclusion, sanitation correction,
harborage removal, drain servicing, waste handling, and physical device work.
These do most of the durable work in a commercial setting, and they are where a
program earns its money.

**Targeted application where it is warranted.** Placed where the biology says,
in the quantities the situation justifies, recorded with product, rate, location
and technician.

**Documentation of everything**, including the recommendations on your side.

**Review.** Trend data looked at over time rather than each visit treated as
isolated, so a recurring issue in one area gets structural attention instead of a
repeated treatment.

## The record, which is half the service

Worth spelling out, because "we provide documentation" means very different things
at different companies.

**Service reports** for every visit: date, technician, areas inspected, findings
by location, actions taken, materials used with rates and target sites, devices
serviced, and recommendations with responsibility assigned.

**A device map** showing numbered positions for every monitor and station, updated
when anything moves, so an auditor can walk the site against the map.

**Trend data** — activity by device and by area over time, which is what turns a
stack of visit reports into something you can actually manage from.

**Material records** — labels and safety data sheets available on site, with
application records that satisfy the reporting your sector requires.

**Corrective action log** — the things on your side, written down, dated, and
tracked rather than mentioned in passing to whoever happened to be at the door.

The test we hold ourselves to: if an inspector or auditor asks for the pest
control file and you hand it over without calling us for an explanation, the
documentation is doing its job.

## Where the pressure actually is

Across sectors the same handful of points account for most commercial pest
problems, and most of them are structural rather than about cleanliness.

**Doors.** Dock doors and roll-up doors that do not seal at the bottom corners,
exterior doors with worn sweeps, and doors propped open during deliveries or
prep. This is the single most common entry route on commercial sites.

**Drains.** The organic film inside floor and sink drains is the most consistently
under-serviced pest source in any commercial kitchen, and it is where
[drain and phorid flies](/services/fly-control/) breed. A drain maintenance
routine is usually the highest-value line on a food service program.

**Waste.** Dumpster placement, lid condition, collection frequency, and whether
the pad beneath it is ever washed. A dumpster on an unwashed pad produces flies
indefinitely regardless of what happens indoors.

**Deliveries and cardboard.** Corrugated cardboard is how
[cockroaches](/services/cockroach-control/) and
[pantry pests](/services/beetle-control/) arrive on most sites. De-boxing at the
dock rather than carrying cartons into storage removes the main vector and costs
nothing.

**Storage.** Product held on the floor rather than on racking, no clear inspection
aisle around the perimeter, and stock that is not rotated.

**Equipment voids.** The warm space behind and beneath refrigeration, cooking
equipment and compressors, where grease accumulates and nobody can reach.

**The exterior.** Vegetation against the building, standing water, lighting that
draws insects to entrances, and gaps in the envelope. That is
[exclusion work](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/), and on a commercial building it
usually pays back faster than on a house.

## Regulated settings

Some sectors carry specific requirements, and knowing them is part of the job
rather than an add-on.

**Schools and childcare.** Washington has particular rules around pesticide
application at schools, including notification and posting requirements. We
schedule and document to meet them, and in these settings we lean harder on
exclusion, sanitation and monitoring than on application wherever the situation
allows.

**Food handling.** Whether you are under a health department inspection regime, a
third-party audit scheme, or both, the expectations are broadly consistent:
mapped devices, current records available on site, no unlabeled materials, and a
demonstrable corrective action process.

**Healthcare and senior living.** Occupancy constraints, vulnerable residents,
and residents who may not report bites or activity — which makes scheduled
inspection more valuable here than almost anywhere. Preparation for treatment also
has to be built into the service rather than handed to an occupant.

**Multifamily and HOA.** Shared walls and utility chases mean the building is the
unit of work rather than the apartment, and responsibility between owner, manager
and tenant is set by Washington's landlord-tenant rules. We will say plainly who
we think needs to be in the conversation.

**Agriculture and food processing.** Product protection, rodent and fly pressure at scale, and record-keeping that supports the buyer requirements a producer
already operates under.

## Getting started

**A walk of the site**, and for anything larger than a single storefront it takes
a while, because most of what matters is not where the activity is but where the
access is.

**A written program** covering scope, frequency, what happens on each visit, the
device map, the areas included, and the things on your side that will make or
break the result.

**Then we run it**, and adjust it when the site tells us to rather than when the
calendar does.

There is no long-term contract requirement. The agreement sets out the schedule,
the scope and a short notice period.

## What it costs

Priced from the site rather than from a table, because the variables are real:
square footage, sector, how many exterior doors and docks, the number of devices
the site warrants, service frequency, and how much documentation your sector
requires.

Two honest points about the money.

**Exclusion usually beats frequency.** A site with door sweeps fitted, a dock seal
replaced and a waste pad that gets washed needs less service than one without,
and we would rather set that up than sell more visits to manage an open building.

**The cheapest program is rarely the one that survives an audit.** A monthly
perimeter spray with a signature on a docket is inexpensive and does not produce
the record your sector expects. If you are comparing quotes, compare what the
documentation actually contains.

The site assessment and the written program are free.

## What we ask of you

A commercial program is a shared job, and being explicit about the split saves
argument later.

**Access.** To the areas on the program, at the agreed times, including locked
rooms, mechanical spaces and roof access where devices sit. A visit that cannot
reach half the site produces half a record.

**A point of contact** who receives the report and can action the corrective
items. Reports that arrive at an unmonitored inbox achieve nothing.

**The corrective actions on your side.** Door seals, drain servicing frequency,
waste handling, storage off the floor, and the inspection aisle around the
perimeter. These are the items that decide the result, and they are outside our
control.

**A pest sighting log** that staff actually use. The people in the building every
day see things a technician on a schedule does not, and a log with dates and
locations is genuinely the most valuable single input to a program.

**Notice of changes.** A new supplier, a layout change, construction next door, a
new waste contractor, a renovation — all of these change pest pressure, and knowing
in advance is cheaper than discovering it.

We will tell you plainly when a recurring problem is on your side of that line.
It is not a way of deflecting; it is the only way the problem gets fixed.

## Staff training

Frequently the highest-return part of a commercial program, and the part most
often left out.

The people in your building all day are the detection system. Trained to know what
to look for — droppings and where they indicate, gnaw marks, grease rub marks,
signs in received product, drain conditions, door seal failures — they will find
things weeks before a scheduled visit would.

For food handling and hospitality this is usually a short session covering what to
look for, what to record, what to do immediately, and what not to do — the last
being important, because a well-meaning staff member with a can of consumer
insecticide can turn a contained
[cockroach](/services/cockroach-control/) problem into a building-wide one.

For senior living and healthcare it also covers reporting, since residents may not
report activity themselves.

We build this in rather than charging separately for it, because a site where
staff report early is a cheaper site to service.

## Reporting and communication

The part that determines whether a program is useful to you or just happening
around you.

**After every visit** you get the service report, promptly rather than at
month-end, with findings by location and any corrective items flagged.

**Monthly or quarterly, depending on the site**, a summary that shows the trend
rather than the incident — device activity by area over time, what changed, what is
improving and what is not. This is what turns service into management information.

**Immediately, when something warrants it.** A rodent entry point discovered
during a routine visit, or activity in a sensitive area, should reach you the same
day rather than appearing in a report you read next week.

**Annually**, a review of the whole program — whether the frequency is right,
whether the device layout still matches how the site is used, and whether the
corrective actions have been done. Sites change, and a program that has not been
reviewed in three years is usually servicing a building that no longer exists in
that form.

## What we will tell you that others may not

**When the frequency is too high.** If a site is quiet across every device for a
sustained period, we would rather reduce the schedule than keep billing it. That
is a genuinely uncomfortable conversation in this industry and it is the right
one.

**When the problem is structural.** A recurring issue at the same dock door is a
dock door problem. We will keep saying it in writing until it is fixed or until
you tell us to stop.

**When it is somebody else's trade.** A persistent
[phorid fly](/services/fly-control/) population in a kitchen frequently means a
broken drain line under the slab, and no amount of pest service resolves it. That
is a plumber, and we will say so rather than servicing around it indefinitely.

**When a cheaper program would serve you as well.** Not every site needs the
documentation load that an audited food facility does, and selling that
specification to a small retail unit is overselling.

**And when we are not the right fit.** Some national accounts require a vendor
footprint we do not have. We would rather say that at the quote stage than take
work we cannot service to the standard the account expects.

## Getting a program started quickly

For a site with an active problem rather than a preventive need, the sequence
compresses.

**Same-week assessment** where it is urgent, because an active
[rodent](/services/rodent-control/) or
[cockroach](/services/cockroach-control/) issue in a food business is a trading
risk rather than an inconvenience.

**Immediate stabilization.** Deal with what is happening now — treatment, trapping,
device placement — while the fuller program is being written.

**The structural findings in writing on day one**, so the items on your side can
start in parallel rather than after we finish.

**Then the ongoing program**, set at a frequency that reflects the site rather
than a default.

That order matters when an inspection is imminent or a complaint has already been
made, and it is worth saying that a program started in a crisis is more expensive
than one started in a quiet month — not because we price it differently, but
because the population is larger and the corrective work is more urgent.

## Small businesses, not just large ones

Worth saying plainly, because commercial pest control is often presented as
something for facilities with a compliance department.

A single café, a small retail unit, a two-person office above a shop or a
family-run store does not need the documentation load of an audited food plant,
and quoting them that specification is overselling. What they usually need is a
sensible schedule, an honest look at doors and waste, and a record that satisfies
a health inspection.

That is a modest program and we price it as one. The same principles apply —
inspection first, structure before spray, findings in writing — at a scale that
fits the business.

If you are a small operator who has been quoted a program that felt built for
somebody much larger, it is worth getting a second view.
