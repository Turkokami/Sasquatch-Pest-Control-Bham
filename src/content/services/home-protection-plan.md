## Who this is actually for

Most of this site argues against buying treatment you do not need, so it would be
strange to put up a recurring plan page that pretends everyone needs one. Here is
the honest version.

**A plan makes sense when the pressure is continuous.** A house on a forest edge
in Sudden Valley, a property backing onto a greenbelt in Whatcom Falls, an
address surrounded by farmland outside Lynden — these have populations pushing at
the building constantly, from land you do not control. Nothing you do to the
structure changes what is happening outside it, and managing that is ongoing work
by nature.

**It makes sense when there is history.** A house that has had rodents twice, or
carpenter ants in successive springs, has demonstrated something about its site
that is unlikely to change on its own.

**It makes sense for rentals and second homes**, where nobody is present to
notice the early signs. Scheduled visits substitute for the attention an owner
occupier provides for free.

**And it makes sense when you simply do not want to think about it.** That is a
legitimate reason to buy a service and we are not going to be snobbish about it.

## Who it is not for

**One identifiable problem with a fixable cause.** A wasp nest under an eave. A
gap in the foundation letting rodents in. Ants tracking to one moisture source.
Fix the cause, treat once, and you are done. Selling a year of visits for a
problem a single afternoon resolves is the practice that has given this industry
its reputation.

**Well-sealed newer construction with no history.** A house in Cordata or Barkley
with sound siding, screened vents and no record of anything is not buying much.

**A situation where the money should go to exclusion instead.** This is the
common one. If you have a rodent problem and a limited budget, close the gaps.
[Exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) is bought once and keeps working;
a plan has to keep being paid for. We will sequence it that way even though the
recurring number is better business for us.

If you call us and one of those describes your situation, that is what we will
tell you.

## What the plan actually does

**Exterior perimeter treatment**, applied where it matters — foundation,
entry points, eaves, and the harborage around the structure — rather than
broadcast across surfaces for the sake of appearances.

**Web and nest removal.** Physical clearing of webbing, egg sacs and small
starter wasp nests from eaves, entries and outdoor living areas. Unglamorous,
and the most effective single thing done on most visits.

**Interior treatment only where warranted.** Most of the work belongs outside. We
treat inside when there is an active interior problem, not as a matter of routine.

**Monitoring and reporting.** What we found, what we did, and what is changing on
the property. Rising activity in one area is information worth acting on before
it becomes a call.

**Entry point observation.** Every visit is a chance to notice a new gap — a vent
screen that has failed, a contractor's penetration, siding that has gone soft.
Those go on the report, and where they are small we deal with them there.

**Covered callbacks.** Between scheduled visits, if something covered comes back,
we return at no additional charge under our
[100% Service Guarantee](/our-guarantee/).

## What it does not cover

Plainly, because a plan that claims to cover everything is not a real document.

**Bed bugs** are separate — a paid verification visit credited toward treatment,
then per-room pricing. See [bed bug control](/services/bed-bug-control/).

**Structural exclusion** — sealing vents, penetrations, skirting and the gaps an animal is actually using — is quoted per job, because the scope depends entirely on the building. See [exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/).

**Bird work and nuisance wildlife**, including bats and squirrels, are not services we offer at all. If that is what you have, we will say so and point you to somebody who does it.

**Wood-destroying pest treatment** for termites and boring beetles is a separate
scope, because it involves moisture diagnosis and often other trades.

**Exclusion, crawlspace and insulation work** is project work, not plan work.

**Structural repair** is not ours at all, and we will tell you when it is what
you need.

None of that is upselling by omission. Those are genuinely different jobs, and
bundling them into a monthly figure would either inflate the plan for everyone or
leave us doing them badly.

## Timed to what actually happens here

Most recurring plans in this industry run on even quarters because quarters are
easy to bill. The pest calendar in Whatcom County is not evenly spaced, and the
schedule should follow the pests rather than the invoicing cycle.

**Late winter into spring** is when overwintered wasp queens are founding nests
and carpenter ant colonies are becoming active again. Knocking down small starter
nests now prevents the September problem entirely and carries almost no risk. Ant
work done in early spring reaches colonies before they have built.

**Late spring and early summer** is the productive window for spiders and general
perimeter work — populations are building alongside their insect prey, and
treatment and web removal do the most good here. It is also when most people are
least worried, which is exactly why it gets skipped.

**Late summer** is wasp and hornet peak. Colonies are at maximum size and
defensiveness through late August and September, and this is when nearly every
sting happens.

**Early autumn** is the critical one. As the ground saturates through late
September and October, rodents move indoors — driven by water rather than cold.
This is the visit that prevents a winter rodent problem, and it is the reason the
schedule is not evenly spaced.

**Mid-autumn** brings the overwintering insects — cluster flies, boxelder bugs
and conifer seed bugs piling onto south-facing walls looking for a way in.

**Winter** is quiet outside, which makes it the right time for the structural
conversation: what the crawlspace is doing, where the gaps are, what should be
sealed before spring.

## How it works with the guarantee

The plan and the [guarantee](/our-guarantee/) are the same commitment described
two ways.

While a plan is active, covered pests returning between scheduled visits means we
come back at no additional charge, with no cap on return visits within the plan
period and no argument about whether it has been too soon.

Two conditions apply and they are on the guarantee page rather than hidden.
Re-service needs reasonable access to the treated areas. And where we have
identified a cause on your side — a gutter, a vent screen, a door sweep — the
guarantee is not a substitute for fixing it. We will keep coming back, and so
will the pests.

Coverage pauses on an account that is past due and resumes when it clears. If you
cancel mid-plan, coverage runs to the end of the period you have paid for rather
than stopping the day you call.

## No contract, and why

The long-term contract is standard in this industry and its real function is
retention rather than service quality. A company confident in its work does not
need a termination penalty to keep customers.

So there is no multi-year term, no early termination fee buried in a schedule,
and no requirement to justify canceling. The service agreement states the
schedule, the scope and a short notice period, and that is the whole document.

The trade is that we have to be worth renewing every year. We think that is the
right pressure to be under.

## What it costs

Priced from the property rather than from a table, because the variables are
real: square footage, lot size and how much perimeter there is, what is adjacent
— forest edge, greenbelt, farmland or suburb — the pest history, and how much
exterior structure there is to service.

Two honest notes on the money.

First, if [exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work would reduce what
the plan has to manage, do that first. It sometimes reduces the pressure enough
that a plan stops being worth buying, and we would rather tell you that now than
have you work it out in year three.

Second, compare the plan against what you would actually spend calling ad hoc.
For a property with genuine continuous pressure the plan is usually cheaper and
considerably less annoying. For a property with one fixable problem it is not.
The estimate is free and it will include our honest read on which one you have.

## Around the county

**Forest edge and lakeside** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor, Whatcom
Falls. Continuous pressure from land you do not control, and the clearest case
for ongoing service anywhere in the county.

**Farm-adjacent** — the country around Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas.
Rodents from field margins, cluster flies in autumn, and outbuildings that need
including in the scope.

**Older city neighborhoods** — Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York. The
honest recommendation here is frequently exclusion and crawlspace work first,
then a plan afterward if it is still warranted.

**Waterfront and seasonal** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Scheduled visits
substitute for the owner who is not there to notice anything, which makes the
plan more valuable here than the equivalent occupied house.

## What a visit actually looks like

Worth setting expectations, because "quarterly service" means very different
things at different companies and some of it is a truck in a driveway for six
minutes.

A scheduled visit starts outside and stays there for most of its length. The
technician walks the perimeter, clears webbing and any starter nests from eaves,
entries and outdoor living space, checks the foundation line and the entry points
that were noted last time, and applies targeted treatment where it is warranted
rather than everywhere as a matter of routine.

Anything that has changed on the property goes on the report — a new gap, a
downspout that has come apart, vegetation that has grown into contact with the
siding, a vent screen that has failed. Those are the findings that actually
prevent next year's problem.

Interior treatment happens when there is an active interior issue. If there is
not, we do not treat inside for the sake of appearing thorough, and we will tell
you that rather than spraying a baseboard so the visit feels substantial.

You do not need to be home for a standard exterior visit. You get the report
either way.

## Changing or pausing a plan

Circumstances change and the plan should be able to follow.

**Scaling scope.** If you add an outbuilding, a shop or a substantial deck, tell
us and we will re-price rather than quietly under-servicing it. If a problem area
resolves permanently — usually because exclusion work closed it — we will reduce
the scope rather than keeping the number where it is.

**Pausing.** For a property that is genuinely empty for part of the year, we can
schedule around it. There is no requirement to pay for visits to an unoccupied
house you are not worried about, though for seasonal homes the empty months are
frequently when the plan is most useful.

**Stopping.** Short notice period, no penalty, coverage runs to the end of the
period you have paid for.

**Coming back.** Customers who stop and return later do not go to the back of any
queue and do not pay a re-start fee. If the honest answer at some point is that
you no longer need us, we would rather you stopped and came back when you did
than kept paying out of inertia.

## Commercial and multifamily

The residential plan described above is built for houses. Businesses and
multi-unit buildings need something structurally different, and we run those
separately rather than stretching a home plan to fit.

The differences are real. Device placement gets mapped so an inspector or auditor
can see the layout. Every visit produces a record that stands on its own. Scope
usually includes areas a home plan never touches — dock doors, waste enclosures,
utility chases, feed or product storage. And scheduling has to work around the
operation rather than around us, which for food service and retail usually means
before opening or after close.

For apartments and HOAs the unit of work is the building, not the apartment,
because shared walls and utility chases mean treating one unit while its
neighbors go untouched simply relocates the problem. Those are covered under
[commercial programs](/commercial/).

## What we track between visits

The part of a plan that is easy to overlook is the record, and over a couple of
years it becomes the most useful thing about it.

Each visit logs what was found and where, what was done, and what changed since
last time. That accumulates into a picture of the property: which side of the
house takes the pressure, whether activity in one area is climbing or falling,
which interventions actually held, and which entry point keeps reopening.

That record is what lets us tell you in year two that the problem is not really
pests but a downspout, or that the area we have treated four times needs sealing
rather than a fifth treatment. Without it, a recurring plan is just repetition —
which is exactly what recurring service becomes at companies that do not keep
one.

For rentals, second homes and anywhere the owner is not present, that history is
also the only real visibility into what has been happening at the property.

## The honest comparison

If you are weighing a plan against calling as things come up, the arithmetic is
worth doing rather than guessing at.

**Calling ad hoc** costs nothing until something happens, and then costs a
call-out plus treatment each time. For a property with one problem every couple
of years this is straightforwardly cheaper, and we will say so.

**A plan** costs a predictable amount and includes the return visits. It becomes
the better purchase somewhere around two or three service events a year — which
is common on forest-edge, greenbelt and farm-adjacent properties, and uncommon on
a well-sealed house in a suburban street.

**Exclusion** changes the arithmetic more than either. It is a one-time cost that
reduces how many events happen at all, and on a rodent-driven problem it
frequently moves a property from the first category to the second.

The reason we lay it out this way is that the industry standard is to present the
plan as the default and everything else as an exception. For a good share of
Whatcom County homes that is backwards.

## Getting the sequence right

If you are starting from a live problem rather than from prevention, the order
that works is consistent.

**Deal with the problem.** Whatever is actually happening now — rodents, a wasp
colony, an ant trail — gets treated as its own job.

**Find and fix the cause.** The gap, the moisture source, the vegetation contact.
This is the step most often skipped and the one that determines whether anything
recurs.

**Then decide about a plan**, with the pressure already reduced and a clear view
of what is left. Deciding at this point rather than at the first phone call is
how you avoid paying ongoing money to manage a problem that a repair had already
solved.
