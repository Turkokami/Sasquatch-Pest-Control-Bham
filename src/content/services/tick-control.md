## What this service is, and what changed

Until November 2026 this company did not sell tick treatment, and this site said
so plainly on several pages. That has changed, and the honest thing is to say
what changed and what did not.

What changed is that we now carry and apply products labeled for ticks, and we
will treat a property's margins on request or as part of a seasonal program.

What did not change is the underlying picture. Whatcom County has a low tick
load. Tick-borne disease here is uncommon. The exposure that matters most
happens away from your lawn. None of that was marketing when we wrote it and
none of it becomes false now that there is a service attached. If anything, it
is more important to say on a page that sells the work than on a page that
declined it.

So this is not a page that discovered tick danger the moment there was
something to sell. It is the same assessment with a service bolted onto the part
of it that a treatment can actually address: the margin of a property, in
spring.

It is also a new line for us, and the records show it. Our own job book for 2023
through 2025 contains zero tick jobs. What we bring is the licensing, the
labeled products and the method — not a decade of tick work in this county.

## How much tick pressure this county actually has

The best evidence for Washington is a statewide survey published in the CDC
journal [Emerging Infectious Diseases](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/eid/article/26/4/19-1382),
covering 2011 to 2016. It is worth knowing the shape of it before anybody sells
you anything.

Researchers collected **977 host-seeking ticks from 53 sites across 19
counties** and identified **six species**. The western black-legged tick,
*Ixodes pacificus*, accounted for **438** of them — the single most common, and
the one that matters for Lyme disease.

Of those western black-legged ticks, **4.0% carried *Borrelia burgdorferi*
sensu stricto**, the bacterium that causes Lyme disease.

On the human side, the same work counted **202 tick-borne disease cases in
Washington residents** across those six years, of which **68 were acquired
in-state**. Sixty-eight cases, six years, the entire state. That is on the order
of eleven in-state cases a year across all of Washington.

For comparison with the coverage you have probably read: the lone star tick,
which drives a great deal of American tick anxiety and the alpha-gal meat
allergy stories, **is not established in Washington**.

This is the number that should set your expectations, and it is the reason we
sell this as margin work rather than as a whole-property acaricide program.
Broadcast treatment of an entire yard is a real and reasonable practice in
places with genuine tick density. At this load it is not proportionate, and we
will not propose it.

## Tick season here is spring, and that catches people out

The single most useful operational fact on this page.

In that statewide survey, about **91% of the three primary vector species were
collected between March and May**. Spring. Not the deep summer that most people
associate with bugs, and not the stretch when the phone actually rings about
yard treatment.

Compare that with mosquitoes. [WSU Extension](https://wpcdn.web.wsu.edu/wp-puyallup/uploads/sites/415/2014/12/PNW_PPMosquito_Spring2014.pdf)
puts peak mosquito season in this region at **July through September**. So the
two halves of what gets sold as one combined treatment do not peak together at
all — they are months apart, with ticks first.

The practical consequence: **if you sign up for a combined mosquito and tick
program in July, you have missed that year's tick window.** The treatment will
still go down and the granule will still be on labeled ground, but the ticks it
was aimed at were questing in April.

Nobody selling a summer mosquito-and-tick bundle has an incentive to tell you
that. It follows directly from the state's own surveillance, and it is the
reason we would rather talk about tick work in February than in August.

One caveat worth keeping: that figure describes when ticks were out looking for
a host, in a survey that ran March to October. It is a statement about the
season's shape, not a promise that nothing is questing in September.

## What we apply, and what each label actually says

Two products, and the distinction between them is a legal one rather than a
stylistic one. Under federal pesticide law a product must be used and described
consistently with its accepted labeling, so the pest printed on the label
decides what we may tell you the product does.

**The bifenthrin granule.** Ours is Bifen LP Insecticide Granules, **EPA Reg.
53883-124**. Its label reads: *for use to control Ants (including Imported Fire
Ants), Chinch Bugs, Fleas, Mole Crickets, Ticks and other insect pests on lawns
in landscaped areas and perimeters around residential, institutional, public,
commercial and industrial buildings, parks, recreational areas and athletic
fields.* **Ticks are named on the label.** The use areas it lists read like a
description of tick habitat: lawns, flower beds, mulch beds, along fence lines,
around decks and decking, and the perimeter and foundation of a structure.

**EcoVia MT**, a botanical product whose label names ticks and mosquitoes
together, applied to the same margins and resting areas.

Both are the reason this is one visit rather than two. The ground a tick
treatment needs — the boundary where mown grass meets taller growth, the mulch
bed, the shaded strip behind the shed — is the same ground adult mosquitoes rest
on through the day.

Every pesticide product this company applies is published on
[what we use](/what-we-use/) with its EPA registration number, its active
ingredients, and its label and safety data sheet hosted here as PDFs. One gap
recorded honestly: EcoVia MT's own label and data sheet are not in that register
yet, because every entry there was built from the product's label corroborated
against its safety data sheet, and we are not going to break that standard by
transcribing a supplier's catalog page. It joins the register when the
documents are in hand.

We will not call any of it safe. Federal law does not permit a pesticide to be
described in terms that differ from its labeling, and "safe", "harmless" and
"non-toxic" are precisely the words that draw enforcement. You get the product,
the label, and the precautions the label sets.

## Where the treatment goes, and where it cannot

Ticks do not behave like mosquitoes and the treatment does not look the same.

A tick does not fly. It climbs a stem, waits with its front legs out, and
latches onto what brushes past. That behavior is called questing, and it
happens at the edges — where a lawn stops being mown, along a fence line
overgrown with blackberry, in leaf litter under a hedge, on the tall grass beside
a path.

That is where the application goes. Not scattered evenly across open lawn,
because open sunlit lawn is poor tick habitat and a treatment there mostly
treats nothing.

And here is the limit that no product solves. The tick encounters people in this
county actually have overwhelmingly happen somewhere else: on Galbraith, at
Chuckanut, along a logging road, in a field on a hunting trip, on a camping
weekend east of the mountains. A treated margin at home does not follow you onto
a trail.

So the real protection is behavioral, and it is free. Check yourself the same
day you come back from brush, and keep checking for a couple of days. Check the
dog, particularly around the neck and ears, in the leg folds, and between the
toes. Keep fine-tipped tweezers somewhere you can find them. If you are unsure
what you have removed, the
[Washington State Department of Health](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/pests/ticks)
runs tick identification and is the authority we point to.

A yard treatment is a sensible addition for a property whose used space meets
tick habitat. It is not a substitute for a tick check, and we would rather you
heard that from the company taking the money.

## Who should actually buy this

A short list, because the honest answer for a lot of callers is that they should
not.

**Worth it:** a property where the lawn you use runs straight into tall grass,
brush or woodland edge. A dog that works that margin every day. Children who
play at that boundary. An acreage in Everson, Deming or along the Nooksack where
the mown part is a small island in something taller. A rental or holiday property
backing onto brush where guests will not be doing tick checks.

**Probably not worth it:** a tidy in-town lot in Bellingham with mown grass,
fence to fence, and no brushy margin at all. If there is no questing habitat on
the property, treating it accomplishes very little, and we will tell you that on
the phone rather than after the invoice.

**A different problem entirely:** a dog bringing specks into the house is more
often [fleas](/services/flea-control/) than ticks here. A cabin with rodents in
the walls raises a tick-borne relapsing fever question that is really a
[rodent](/services/rodent-control/) question, and rodents in walls is long-
standing work for us. Identification is free in every one of these cases, and it
is worth doing before anybody treats anything.
