## The correction that most of this industry gets wrong

Start here, because it is the single most useful thing on this page and because
the pest control trade has spent two decades getting it wrong in a direction that
happens to sell treatments.

The hobo spider has a reputation in the Pacific Northwest for causing necrotic
skin lesions. That reputation traces to research published in the 1980s and 90s
which was subsequently not supported by later work. The Washington State
Department of Health now states plainly that hobo spiders are **not considered
dangerous as previously thought**, and that the evidence does not support a link
between hobo spider bites and tissue necrosis.

That is a meaningful change, and it has not filtered through to most of the
websites you will find searching for spider control in this region — because
"harmless spider" is a harder thing to sell than "dangerous spider."

We are putting it at the top of our spider page because we would rather be
trusted than sell an unnecessary treatment. There are good reasons to want fewer
spiders around a house. Fear of a bite that the state health department says is
not what it was claimed to be is not one of them.

## What you actually have

**Giant house spider.** This is the big one. Large-bodied, long-legged, brown,
and the spider that produces the majority of "there is an enormous spider in my
bathtub" calls in Whatcom County. It is a funnel-web weaver, it is harmless to
people, and it is very likely the spider you are looking at right now if you came
to this page in September.

It is also a useful animal to have around. It is territorial, it takes a
continuous volume of insects, and it will take other spiders. What we are not
going to tell you is that it hunts hobo spiders down, which is the version of
this that circulates and which we cannot source. What is sourceable is UC IPM's
observation that in parts of the Pacific Northwest the giant house spider has
competitively displaced the hobo spider. Whatever is happening between the two
of them, the practical shape of it is that where the big harmless one is well
established, the one carrying the reputation is scarcer.

**Hobo spider.** A close relative, similar in appearance, and not separable from
a giant house spider by eye. Reliable identification means examining the
reproductive structures under magnification, on a mature specimen — WSU
Extension's identification factsheet states that without them you cannot
definitively identify a hobo spider, and UC IPM's pest note on the species says
the same thing in different words, that telling funnel weavers apart is work for
a qualified arachnologist.

Both of those go straight to the genitalia, and neither mentions chevrons at
all. That is worth knowing, because the abdominal chevron is the test
circulating most widely online and it is the one we used to repeat on this page.
A character that the two authorities on identifying this spider do not bother to
print is not a character anybody can identify it with across a kitchen. Saying
plainly that reliable separation is a microscope job is both truer and harder to
argue with than offering a key that does not work.

There is one thing worth trying at home, and it needs the animal in a clear
container so you can look at it from below. WSU's key separates the two on the
sternum, the plate on the underside between the leg bases: a pale stripe down
the middle on a hobo spider, three or four pairs of pale spots along the sides
on a giant house spider or a barn funnel weaver. Spots mean it is not a hobo.
That is the only field character that does real work, and on an animal moving at
the speed these move it is still not easy.

The practical implication is uncomfortable for the fear-based version of this
page: if you cannot reliably tell them apart by eye, and the one you cannot rule
out is not dangerous, then the identification largely stops mattering for safety.
It still matters for honesty.

**Cross orbweaver.** The garden spider that builds a classic wheel-shaped web
across your back door in late summer, often with a large, patterned,
alarming-looking body. Completely harmless, and an efficient predator of flying
insects. We remove webs where they are genuinely in the way. We do not recommend
treating for them.

**Cellar spider.** Small body, absurdly long thin legs, untidy webs in corners of
basements, crawlspaces and garages. Harmless, and another spider that preys on
other spiders.

**Yellow sac spider.** Small, pale yellow-green, active hunter rather than a web
sitter, often found in a small silk retreat where a wall meets a ceiling. This is
one of the few local spiders whose bite reliably produces a genuine reaction —
usually localized pain and redness rather than anything severe. Worth knowing
about precisely because it gets less attention than the species that do not
deserve theirs.

**Black widow.** Western black widows are a Washington species, and the state
Department of Health describes them as common to eastern Washington. The range
tracks the drier, warmer country east and south of the Cascades, so on this side
of the mountains they are uncommon rather than routine, and the ones that do
turn up are in woodpiles, meter boxes and undisturbed shed corners rather than
in a house. The marking is distinctive enough that a photograph settles it.

**Brown recluse.** Not established in Washington. Nationally, bites attributed to
brown recluse vastly outnumber the spider's actual distribution, and a great many
"recluse bites" turn out on investigation to be infections or other skin
conditions entirely. If you have been told you have them in Whatcom County, get a
second look before anyone treats.

## Why fall is spider season

Every year, from roughly August through October, people in this county discover
large spiders indoors and conclude the spiders are coming in from the cold.

That is not what is happening. Those are **adult males wandering to find mates**.
They have spent the year growing, they have reached maturity, and they are moving
— across floors, up walls, into bathtubs where the smooth sides trap them. It is
a mating season, not a migration, and it has almost nothing to do with
temperature.

Two things follow. First, the spiders you are seeing largely did not just move
in; many of them have been living in and around the structure all along. Second,
the season ends on its own. A treatment applied in late September gets credit for
something that was going to happen in three weeks regardless.

We will tell you that rather than sell you the September treatment, and we would
rather do the useful version of the work — exterior, exclusion and prey reduction
— in spring and early summer.

## Why spraying does less than you would expect

Worth being straight about, because it is why our approach leans on physical work
rather than product.

Spiders are built differently from insects in ways that matter here. They walk on
the tips of their legs, so they contact far less treated surface than a
cockroach or an ant crossing the same band. They do not groom themselves the way
insects do, so they ingest less of what they do contact. And they are predators
rather than foragers — they are not attracted to bait, and they are not following
a trail you can intercept.

What does work is a combination.

**Remove the webs.** Physically taking down webbing and egg sacs removes the
population's infrastructure and its next generation at the same time. It is
unglamorous and it is the most effective single thing done on a spider job.

**Cut the food supply.** Spiders are where their prey is. A house with heavy
exterior insect pressure will have spiders no matter what is sprayed on the
siding, and reducing the insects reduces the spiders more reliably than treating
the spiders does.

**Fix the lighting.** This is the most underrated intervention. White exterior
lights pull in flying insects all night, and the spiders set up exactly where the
food is. Switching to warm-toned or yellow bulbs, or moving fixtures away from
doors, visibly reduces spider populations around entries within a season.

**Close the gaps.** Door sweeps, weatherstripping, window screens, vent screens,
and gaps around utility penetrations.

**Then treat, in the right places.** Targeted exterior application around entry
points, eaves and foundation, timed for spring and early summer when it does the
most good, rather than blanket interior spraying.

## Around the county

**Older Bellingham** — Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York, South Hill.
Unsealed crawlspaces, original single-glazed windows and a century of settlement
gaps make for a lot of interior harborage. Cellar spiders in the crawlspace,
giant house spiders in the living space.

**Against the treeline.** Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor and the Whatcom Falls corridor.
Heavy exterior insect pressure off the treeline and water, which means high
spider numbers regardless of treatment. Exterior lighting choices matter more
here than anywhere else in the county.

**Newer hillside** — Alabama Hill, Barkley, Cordata. Better sealed, so the
problem is more often webbing on eaves, entries and outdoor living space than
spiders indoors.

**Rural and agricultural** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Outbuildings,
equipment storage and barns carry large populations, and in those structures
spiders are largely doing useful work.

**Waterfront** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Seasonal homes standing empty
through the wet months accumulate webbing undisturbed, so the spring visit is
often mostly cleaning rather than treating.

## The spider year here

**February through April.** Egg sacs laid the previous year hatch and spiderlings
disperse. Almost nobody notices, and it is the cheapest point in the year to
intervene, because removing egg sacs in late winter prevents a population rather
than reducing one.

**May through July.** Growth. Populations build alongside their insect prey, and
webbing starts accumulating on eaves, entries and outdoor furniture. This is the
window where treatment and web removal do the most good and the one most people
skip.

**August through October.** The wandering season. Mature males leave their
webs to find females, and this is when the large spiders appear indoors and when
the calls come. It is also, unhelpfully, the least productive time to treat.

**November through January.** Activity drops sharply. Surviving females shelter
and egg sacs sit through winter waiting for spring. A good time for the cleanup
work — clearing webbing from crawlspaces, garages, sheds and eaves — because
nothing is rebuilding it immediately.

## Bites, and being straight about them

Spider bites are massively over-diagnosed, in Washington and everywhere else.
Studies that follow up on presumed spider bites consistently find that most turn
out to be something else — commonly bacterial skin infections, which can look
strikingly similar and are considerably more common than a spider that bit
someone in their sleep.

Most local spiders cannot meaningfully penetrate human skin, and the ones that
can generally only bite when trapped against the body — rolled onto in bed,
pressed inside clothing, caught in a glove. Unprovoked bites are rare.

If you have a lesion you are worried about, the useful step is a physician
rather than a pest control company, and the useful thing to bring is the spider
if you have it. We can identify what you caught. We cannot diagnose a wound, and
we would be doing you a disservice by guessing.

What we will say is that a spider identification is not a medical opinion, and
any company offering one is going beyond what it knows.

## When it genuinely is worth treating

We have spent most of this page talking you out of unnecessary work, so here is
the other side.

Treatment is worth it when webbing is persistent on entries, eaves, porches and
outdoor living areas and keeps returning after removal. When there is a heavy
population in a garage, shed or crawlspace that people use regularly. When
someone in the household has a genuine phobia — that is a real quality-of-life
issue and we do not dismiss it. When a commercial site has an appearance
standard, which for hospitality, healthcare, food handling and retail is a
legitimate business requirement rather than a preference. And when a spider that
does warrant attention, such as a yellow sac spider population in living space,
is actually present.

What we will not do is tell you that a giant house spider in your bathtub in
September is a danger to your family.

## What to do with the one in your bathtub

The practical question people actually have in September.

A giant house spider in a bathtub is stuck — the sides are too smooth to climb
and it fell in overnight hunting. It is not there to menace anyone. Drop in a
towel or a length of toilet paper and it will climb out on its own, or catch it
under a glass with a card slid underneath and put it outside.

It did not come up the drain, either, which is the other thing people ask. A
working trap holds standing water in the bend below the plughole and a spider is
not swimming up through it. What happened is that one hunting across the floor
at night went over the rim, found the enamel too slick to climb back out, and
was still there in the morning. The bath is somewhere it fell into rather than a
route it used, and why sealing a drain or running the hot tap changes nothing
about how many you find next September.

Killing it is a choice rather than a necessity, and it is worth knowing what
goes with it. This is a territorial predator working through insects nightly
that will take other spiders as well, and it belongs to the species UC IPM
credits with having competitively displaced hobo spiders across parts of this
region. The one you remove was holding ground against something.

If you want it identified, photograph it from above with something for scale
before you release it. We identify for free, and a photo settles most questions
in a minute.

What we would ask you not to do is buy a fogger. Total-release aerosols are close
to useless against spiders — they do not reach the harborage, they leave residue
across surfaces people touch, and they kill the insects that were feeding
something you would rather keep.

## Spiders in crawlspaces, garages and outbuildings

Most of the spider volume on any Whatcom County property is in the spaces people
do not spend time in, and that is where the useful work usually happens.

**Crawlspaces.** Cellar spiders and giant house spiders both do well down there,
because it is undisturbed, humid and full of prey. On older Bellingham housing
with an unsealed crawlspace, this is effectively a reservoir that restocks the
living space above it. Clearing webbing and dealing with the moisture does more
than any interior treatment.

**Garages and sheds.** Stored boxes, stacked material and undisturbed corners are
ideal harborage, and a garage attached to the house is a doorway. This is the
single most common place we find heavy webbing on a residential job.

**Barns and outbuildings.** Rural properties around Lynden, Everson and Nooksack
carry enormous spider populations in outbuildings, and in most cases they are
doing useful work on flies and other insects. We clear what is in the way and
leave the rest, unless the building is one people work in daily.

**Boats and seasonal storage.** Covered moorage and stored equipment around
Blaine, Birch Bay and the harbors accumulate webbing through the off-season with
nobody disturbing it. A spring clean-out is usually the whole service.

The common thread across all four is that clearing and drying the space beats
spraying it, and that the result holds for a season rather than a fortnight.
