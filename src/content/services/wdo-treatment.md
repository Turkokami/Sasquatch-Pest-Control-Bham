## Who this page is for

You are buying or selling a house in Whatcom County. An inspector has been
through it and produced a document listing wood-destroying organisms, or
conditions conducive to them, and now somebody has to deal with it before the
sale closes.

That is the situation this page addresses. It is a narrow, time-pressured, and
frequently stressful lane, and it is one where the pest control industry is
routinely vague about what it is actually licensed to do. So we will be
specific.

## The two roles, and why they are not the same company

There are two distinct pieces of work in a transaction like this, they require
different credentials, and it genuinely matters which one you are asking for.

**Identifying.** A licensed structural pest inspector examines the building and
documents what is present. Their finding is what a lender, a buyer and an
escrow officer rely on. It is a regulated activity in Washington and it carries
its own license.

**Treating.** Somebody then has to actually deal with what was found — treat the
active insects, and address the conditions that let them in. That requires a
pesticide license carrying the right category.

**We do the second one.** We are not licensed structural pest inspectors, we do
not perform that examination, and we do not issue that document. If that is
what you need, you need somebody else, and we will happily say so on the phone
rather than waste your time.

We think that separation is a feature rather than a limitation. When the same
company both identifies the problem and sells you the cure, it has a direct
financial interest in the size of the problem. Nobody in that arrangement is
doing anything improper by default, but the incentive is plainly there, and in a
transaction where a buyer is already nervous and a seller is already
negotiating, removing it is worth something. An independent finding and an
independent quote is simply a cleaner arrangement for everybody.

## What our license actually covers

Our people hold Washington State Department of Agriculture licenses — a
commercial applicator license, which under the state's Pesticide Application
Act attaches to the business of applying pesticides to the land of another,
and commercial operator licenses, which attach to the employees doing the
applying. Every one of them carries the same two categories: **PCO Structural**
and **PCO General**.

PCO Structural is the relevant one here. In the state's own terms it covers the
control of pests that attack structural material, including fungus, termites,
carpenter ants and wood-boring beetles. That is a treatment authority, and it is
what allows us to work on the findings in front of you.

The license numbers are published on our about page next to the names of the
people who hold them. You can put any of them into the WSDA license search and
confirm the status, the expiry and the categories yourself, in about a minute.
We publish them because a credential you cannot check is a claim rather than a
credential, and in a transaction with money and a deadline attached you should
not be taking anybody's word for it — ours included.

## The organisms, and what treatment actually involves

Six things turn up on findings in this county with any regularity. They are
genuinely different problems and they need genuinely different work, which is
why a single flat "termite treatment" price is usually a sign that nobody has
looked properly.

### Pacific dampwood termite

By far the most common termite finding here. It is a large insect, it does not
need soil contact, it builds no mud tubes, and it has one absolute requirement:
wood with high moisture content. It cannot establish in dry, sound lumber.

Treatment is localized to the infested material rather than applied as a soil
barrier around the building, because there is no soil-dwelling colony to
intercept. Where the damaged member is structural, the honest answer is often
that a section needs replacing rather than treating, and that is carpentry.

The critical part is the water. A dampwood finding is really a moisture finding
with insects in it. Treating without correcting the source buys a quiet year.

### Western subterranean termite

Present in Washington, and a different animal entirely — though the range is
narrower than most termite writing implies. The WSU Cooperative Extension
termite bulletin, EB0787, gives its northern limit in western Washington as
Seattle, which is around ninety miles south of Whatcom County. Soil-dependent,
builds the shelter tubes people associate with termites, and where it is
genuinely established the treatment approach is the conventional soil-focused
one. It is listed here because the license category covers it and because an
inspector's report can name it, not because it is a probable finding at this
latitude.

Correct identification matters enormously here, because the treatment for this
species is substantially more involved than for dampwood, and being sold one
when you have the other is a common and expensive error.

### Carpenter ants

Extremely common in this county and frequently documented in a finding. They
excavate galleries to nest rather than eating the wood, they prefer material
that has begun to soften, and a mature colony almost always has a parent nest
outdoors — a stump, a dead limb, a landscape timber, a fence post — with
satellites inside the structure.

Treating the satellite without locating the parent is why so many carpenter ant
jobs come back. Where we can find the parent colony, the result holds. Where it
sits on a neighboring property, we say so and plan accordingly.

### Wood-boring beetles

Usually anobiid beetles in this region, and genuinely common in damp older
crawlspaces. The signature is small round exit holes with fine powdery frass
below them, in joists, subfloor and sill.

The question that decides the treatment is whether the infestation is active or
historic. Damage persists for decades after the insects are gone, and exit holes
in a joist are not by themselves evidence of anything current. We would rather
establish that than treat a museum piece, and if the finding is old and inactive
we will tell you so even though it costs us the job.

### Moisture ants

The most honest insect on the list. They colonize wood that is already wet and
already decaying, which makes finding them useful information: they are the
symptom, and the water is the disease. Treatment without correction produces a
new colony in the same wood.

### Wood decay fungus

Not an insect at all, and frequently the most consequential item on a finding.
Decay fungus is what actually destroys structural capacity in this climate, and
it needs sustained moisture to run. Where it is active, the meaningful work is
drying the assembly and correcting the water, followed by repair or replacement
of what has lost strength.

We treat what falls within our category and we are direct about where the line
sits. Structural repair is a contractor's work and we do not pretend otherwise.

## Conducive conditions, which usually matter more than the insects

Most findings in Whatcom County include a section on conditions conducive to
wood-destroying organisms, and in our experience that section is the one worth
reading twice.

The recurring items are unglamorous and predictable. Standing water in the
crawlspace. A missing, torn or displaced vapor barrier. Blocked, broken or
missing foundation vent screens. Earth-to-wood contact where grade has been
built up against siding. Downspouts discharging at the foundation instead of
away from it. A deck ledger flashed badly or not flashed at all. Bark mulch
piled against the sill. Plumbing weeping slowly under a bathroom. Gutters that
have been overflowing down one wall for years.

None of those are pests. Every one of them is the reason pests arrive. In this
climate — no hard freeze, roughly thirty-five inches of rain a year, wood that
never really dries out — the conducive conditions section is the part that
predicts what the house will be dealing with five years from now.

So when we quote, we tell you which items are ours and which are not. Correcting
grade, replacing a vapor barrier, rehanging a downspout and flashing a ledger
are not pest treatments. Some of them we can do, some belong to a contractor,
and pretending the whole list is a pest problem would be a straightforward way
to overcharge you.

## How the timing works

Transactions run on dates, so here is the realistic sequence.

The finding is produced. You send it to us — a photograph of the relevant pages
is fine to start. We can usually tell you from that alone whether it is a
straightforward treatment or something that needs eyes on it first, and give you
a rough range on the phone.

We come out and look. That visit costs nothing and it is where the quote comes
from. We are looking at the specific items documented, at whether they are
active, and at what is driving them.

You get a written quote separating treatment from anything that is a repair. If
part of the work needs a contractor, you get told that at this point rather than
after we have started, because a contractor lead time is the thing most likely
to threaten a closing date.

Treatment is scheduled. For most findings this is same-week. Where drying or a
repair has to happen first, the sequence matters and we will lay out the order.

You receive documentation of what was treated, what was used and when. That is
the record for your file, your agent's file and the transaction.

Tell us the closing date at the first phone call. It is the single most useful
piece of information you can give us, and the difference between a comfortable
schedule and a scramble is almost always that one detail arriving late.

## For agents, lenders and escrow officers

We work with a number of Whatcom County agents and we know what makes this easy
on your side.

You get a quote that distinguishes pest treatment from building repair, so
nobody is negotiating a credit against a number that quietly includes carpentry.
You get realistic scheduling rather than an optimistic date, because a missed
promise on a closing week is worse than an honest longer estimate. You get
documentation that states what was done. And you get a straight answer when the
finding does not actually require treatment, which happens more often than the
industry likes to admit — historic beetle damage with no active infestation
being the usual example.

We also will not quote against a finding we have not read. If somebody is giving
you a price for wood-destroying organism treatment over the phone without seeing
the document or the structure, that number is a guess, and guesses in a
transaction tend to become disputes.

## What we will not do

**We will not issue the report.** Repeating it because it is the thing people
most often hope we will bend on. We are not licensed for it.

**We will not treat an inactive infestation just because it appears in a
finding.** Old galleries and old exit holes are evidence of history, not of
current activity, and treating them changes nothing.

**We will not sell a soil barrier for a dampwood infestation.** The species has
no soil dependency. Treating the perimeter for an insect living in a wet window
header is spending money on the wrong problem.

**We will not recommend tenting or fumigation here.** It is not a Whatcom County
treatment for Whatcom County species. If it has been proposed to you locally,
get a second opinion before committing to anything.

**We will not attach an ongoing annual plan to a transaction treatment** unless
there is a recurring pressure that genuinely justifies it. A dry, sound,
well-drained house with a corrected water problem does not need a recurring
program, and the sale is a poor moment to sign somebody up for one.

## What drives the cost

We do not publish a flat figure for this work, because the variation is real and
a headline number would be misleading.

The things that move it: how many separate items are on the finding and whether
they are the same organism; whether the infestation is active or historic;
access, because a crawlspace you can sit up in is a different job from an
eighteen-inch one; how much of the affected material is reachable without
demolition; and whether drying or a repair has to be sequenced first.

What does not move it is urgency. We do not price a closing deadline as an
upcharge. If we can do it in the window we quote it the same as any other week,
and if we cannot, we say so instead of taking a deposit and hoping.

## What the treatment visit itself looks like

People are often surprised by how much of the day is spent not treating.

We start in the crawlspace, because that is where most Whatcom County findings
live and because it is where the evidence is. That means going in properly with
a light, on our back or our stomach, following the affected member to see how
far the damage actually runs. A finding says a joist is affected. It does not
usually say whether that continues four feet further into a bay nobody could
reach on the day.

Then the exterior: grade, drainage, the siding-to-foundation transition, vents,
downspout discharge and anything holding water against the structure. The point
is to be able to tell you why this happened, not merely what is present.

Treatment is targeted to the material and the organism. For dampwood termites
and carpenter ants in a specific member that generally means working the
galleries directly. For beetles it depends on whether we have established
current activity. For a decay fungus condition the meaningful intervention is
drying and correcting water, and we will say so rather than applying something
for the look of it.

Afterwards you get a treatment record: which items from the report were
treated, what product was used, where, and on what date. It also states which
items are building repairs rather than pest treatments, so nobody assumes a
carpenter's job was covered by ours. In a transaction that last line is the one
that saves arguments later.

What that record is not, and does not pretend to be, is a document setting out
findings of wood-destroying organism damage. Under Washington's rules that is a
regulated report with its own requirements, and it belongs to the licensed
inspector who wrote yours.

## If you are the buyer, before the finding comes back

There is a short list worth walking yourself, because it tells you what is
likely coming and it costs nothing.

Open the crawlspace hatch and look, even briefly. Standing water, a vapor
barrier that has been shoved aside, insulation hanging down, or a smell of damp
earth are all things you will see without training, and any of them predicts
what a finding is going to say.

Walk the perimeter and look at where the ground meets the building. Soil or bark
mulch piled above the siding line is the single most common conducive condition
in this county and among the cheapest to correct.

Look up at the gutters and follow the downspouts. If one discharges at the
foundation, the wall below it has been getting wet for as long as that has been
true.

Check the deck where it attaches to the house. Ledger flashing is a chronic
defect in this housing stock, it is expensive when it fails, and it produces
exactly the wet framing that dampwood termites and moisture ants require.

None of this replaces a proper examination by somebody licensed to do it. It
does mean you will not be surprised by the document when it arrives, and you
will have a sense of which items are trivial and which are a real negotiation.
