## A humidity problem in an insect costume

Everything about silverfish follows from one fact: they need moisture from the air
to survive.

They do not drink the way most insects do; they absorb humidity, and they lose it
continuously through the exoskeleton. In a genuinely dry space they desiccate.
That is why they concentrate in bathrooms, crawlspaces, basements, laundry rooms
and around plumbing, and why they are close to absent in a dry, well-ventilated
house.

It also means that treating the insects you see, in a space that stays damp,
produces a temporary result. The conditions are the infestation; the insects are
the evidence.

In Whatcom County that puts silverfish alongside
[moisture ants](/services/ant-control/),
[dampwood termites](/services/termite-control/), anobiid beetles and Oriental
cockroaches on the same list — a group of pests that are really telling you about
water. Roughly thirty-five inches of rain a year across a long gray season, and
crawlspaces that are frequently unsealed, is the whole explanation.

## What you are looking at

**Silverfish.** Teardrop-shaped, tapering from head to tail, covered in silvery
scales, with two long antennae in front and three long bristles at the rear. They
move in a distinctive rapid wriggle. They prefer cool and damp.

**Firebrats.** Same body plan, mottled gray and brown rather than silver, and they
prefer heat. Water heaters, furnaces, boiler rooms, around heating ducts, and in
commercial settings, bakeries and mechanical rooms. Same approach, different place
to look.

They are among the most primitive insects — wingless, and unusual in continuing to
molt throughout adult life rather than stopping at maturity. An adult sheds its
skin repeatedly over a life measured in years, which is why the first evidence in
a bookcase or a bathroom cabinet is frequently a scatter of translucent cast
skins rather than a live insect.

One naming note, because the two versions sit side by side in the reference
literature and look like somebody's typing error. The common silverfish is
*Lepisma saccharinum*. The genus was reassigned to neuter gender, and under the
rules of zoological nomenclature the ending of the species name changed with it.
A great many extension fact sheets still print *saccharina*. It is the same
animal either way, and our
[silverfish species profile](/pest-library/silverfish/) sets out where the change
came from.

The confusion worth avoiding is **house centipedes**, which are longer-legged,
faster, and predators of silverfish. Finding centipedes frequently means there is
enough prey to support them, which is information rather than a second problem.
See [centipede and millipede control](/services/centipede-control/).

## Slow, and why that matters

Silverfish are unusual among household insects in how slowly everything happens.

They develop gradually — months rather than weeks — and adults live for years
rather than a season. A female produces eggs steadily but in small numbers, so
populations build slowly and are usually well established before anyone notices
them.

Two practical consequences.

**A silverfish problem is older than it looks.** If you are seeing them regularly,
the population has probably been there for a long time and the conditions
supporting it have been in place longer.

**Clearing takes months, not weeks.** With something like a cockroach or a flea
you can measure progress in a fortnight. Here, the population declines as the
existing long-lived adults die off and are not replaced. Anyone promising a fast
resolution is either misinformed or selling repeat visits.

## What they damage

Not the house, and not you. What they eat is starch, and that turns out to include
things people care about.

**Paper and books.** Book bindings and the glue in them, the starch sizing in
paper, and the surface of pages — leaving irregular grazed patches and small
ragged holes rather than clean bores.

**Wallpaper**, specifically the paste behind it, which in older homes with
original wallpaper produces loosening and staining.

**Photographs and documents.** Stored boxes of family papers in a damp basement or
crawlspace is the classic loss, and it is not recoverable.

**Textiles.** Starched or sized natural fibers — cotton, linen, silk, rayon — and
particularly anything stored damp. Not wool, which is more a
[clothes moth and carpet beetle](/services/moth-control/) concern.

**Dry goods.** Flour, cereal, oats and pet food in unsealed packaging.

So for most households this is a nuisance. For a household with stored books,
archives, artwork or textiles in a damp space, it is a genuine and cumulative
loss, and worth acting on properly.

## Where they actually live

The ones you see are a fraction, and they are not living where you find them.

**Crawlspaces** are the reservoir in most Whatcom County homes. Damp soil, no
functioning vapor barrier, cellulose everywhere in the form of framing, debris and
old insulation. If a house has a persistent silverfish problem, this is usually
the source.

**Bathrooms.** Behind and under vanities, around plumbing penetrations, under
flooring at the tub edge, and inside wall voids that stay humid because the
extractor fan is inadequate or vents into the attic instead of outside.

**Basements and laundry rooms**, particularly against exterior walls.

**Attics**, more than people expect, especially where a bath fan discharges into
the space and where stored cardboard and paper provide food.

**Wall voids around plumbing**, which is how they move between floors.

**Bookshelves, storage boxes and stacked paper** in any of the above.

**Behind wallpaper** in older homes, where the paste is both food and shelter.

The bathtub and sink are collection points rather than habitat — they are drawn by
the humidity, fall in, and cannot climb the glazed sides. A tub with several in it
each morning suggests a wall void nearby, not a drain problem.

## How we work a silverfish job

**Confirm the species.** Silverfish or firebrat, because it determines whether we
are looking at damp cool spaces or warm mechanical ones.

**Find the reservoir.** Crawlspace, bathroom voids, attic, basement. Moisture
readings matter more here than insect counts.

**Identify the moisture source.** Missing or torn vapor barrier, blocked or absent
crawlspace venting, a bath fan that vents into the attic or does not run long
enough, plumbing leaking slowly, gutters and downspouts putting water against the
foundation, or simply a basement that has never had a dehumidifier.

**Treat where they harbor**, not where they are seen. Targeted application into
voids, along plumbing runs, and in the crawlspace, plus dusts in dry inaccessible
voids where a dust remains effective for a long time.

**Correct the conditions.** This is the actual fix, and it is often
[crawlspace restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/) or ventilation work
rather than pest control.

**Exclude and reduce harborage.** Sealing gaps, and moving stored paper and
textiles out of damp spaces into sealed containers.

**Follow up on a realistic timeline**, understanding that the decline is gradual.

## The moisture work, which is the real treatment

If you do only one thing, do this.

**Crawlspace.** A properly installed and sealed vapor barrier, clear working vents
screened against rodents, and drainage that keeps groundwater out. On older
Bellingham housing this single project changes the humidity of the whole structure
above it. See
[crawlspace cleanout and restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/).

**Bathroom ventilation.** An extractor fan that is adequately sized, that
discharges to the *exterior* rather than into the attic, and that runs long enough
after a shower to actually clear the moisture — a timer switch is a small
investment with a disproportionate effect.

**Dehumidification** in basements and persistently damp spaces. Frequently the
single most effective intervention available and it requires no pest control at
all.

**The exterior water.** Gutters clear, downspouts discharging away from the
foundation, soil graded away from the building, and bark mulch kept below the
siding line.

**Fix the small leaks.** A slow drip under a sink supports a population
indefinitely.

**Around Lake Whatcom** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor. Sloped lots

**Basement housing** across the older parts of the city, where a finished basement
without dehumidification is an ideal habitat with a stored-paper food supply.

**Waterfront and seasonal** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Closed-up houses
with no ventilation running for months, arriving at spring with high humidity
throughout.

**Newer construction** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain. Much less common,
because the crawlspaces are sealed and the bathrooms vent properly. Where we do
find them there is usually a specific defect, most often a bath fan ducted into
the attic.

## What you can do

**Run the bathroom fan longer** — during the shower and for a good while
afterwards. A timer switch makes this automatic.

**Get a dehumidifier** into any persistently damp basement or utility space and
keep it emptied.

**Move stored paper and textiles** out of crawlspaces, basements and garages, or
into sealed plastic containers rather than cardboard boxes.

**Reduce the cardboard**, which is both food and harborage.

**Fix the drips.**

**Seal gaps** around plumbing penetrations, baseboards and where fixtures meet
walls.

**Do not rely on interior spraying.** It reaches the ones you can see and none of
the ones you cannot, and it does nothing about why they are there.

## Protecting what they damage

Where the loss is books, papers or textiles, storage changes the outcome more than
treatment does.

**Get it out of the damp space.** A box of papers in a crawlspace or an
unconditioned basement is in the reservoir. Moving it to a conditioned room is the
single most effective step.

**Sealed plastic rather than cardboard.** Cardboard is food, harborage and a
humidity sponge. Lidded plastic containers with a proper seal exclude silverfish
entirely.

**Do not store paper directly on concrete.** A concrete floor or wall moves
moisture continuously, and boxes in contact with it stay damp underneath. Shelving
or pallets solve it.

**Watch the wallpaper in older homes.** Where original paper is lifting at seams or
edges in a damp room, the paste behind it is being eaten. That is worth addressing
before the paper is lost.

**Textiles clean and dry, in sealed storage.** Silverfish are drawn to starch and
sizing, so laundered items stored dry are considerably less attractive than items
put away damp or starched.

For genuinely valuable material — archives, artwork, family documents — the answer
is a conditioned space with stable humidity, not a treatment schedule. We would
rather tell you that than sell you an annual visit to protect boxes that should
simply be somewhere else.

## The centipede connection

Worth knowing, because it changes how people react to a second insect appearing.

House centipedes are predators, and silverfish are among their preferred prey. A
house with a silverfish population frequently develops a centipede population,
because there is enough to eat.

People generally find centipedes more alarming than silverfish, so the arrival of
the predator often prompts the call. The useful framing is that the centipedes are
a symptom of the silverfish, which are themselves a symptom of the humidity.

Deal with the moisture and all three resolve in sequence — the silverfish decline,
the centipedes leave for lack of prey, and the conditions stop supporting either.
Treating the centipedes alone leaves the food supply and the damp in place. See
[centipede and millipede control](/services/centipede-control/).

## Commercial and institutional

Silverfish come up in specific commercial settings, almost always for the same
reason.

**Libraries, archives and records storage.** The combination of paper, glue,
starch sizing and a basement or unconditioned store is close to ideal. Damage here
is cumulative and irreversible, and the answer is environmental control — stable
humidity, sealed storage, and no cardboard — far more than treatment.

**Museums and galleries** with textile or works-on-paper collections, where the
same logic applies with higher stakes.

**Older commercial buildings** with basements, service corridors and plumbing
chases that nobody conditions. The population lives in the building services
rather than in the occupied space.

**Bakeries, kitchens and warm mechanical rooms**, where the species is more often
firebrats than silverfish. Around ovens, water heaters, boiler rooms and heating
runs.

**Multifamily buildings**, where a damp crawlspace or basement supplies the whole
structure and treating an individual unit misses the reservoir entirely.

For all of these the useful deliverable is monitoring plus an environmental
assessment, rather than a spray schedule. Sticky monitors placed in service spaces
tell you where the population actually is and whether it is rising or falling,
which is worth more than a technician's impression. See
[commercial programs](/commercial/).

## The seasonal pattern

Less pronounced than most pests here, because the reservoir is indoors and largely
buffered from the weather. But there is a shape to it.

**Autumn and winter.** The wet season raises humidity throughout the structure,
crawlspace moisture peaks, and populations do well. This is when the conditions
are at their most supportive, even though people notice the insects less.

**Late winter and early spring.** The most common time to notice them, as heating
patterns change and activity increases. The bathtub sightings pick up.

**Summer.** Populations concentrate more tightly into the remaining damp spaces as
the rest of the house dries. If you see them in August, the space they are in is
genuinely wet, which makes summer sightings more diagnostically useful than winter
ones.

**And the year-round point:** a heated house does not have a silverfish season the
way it has a wasp season. The population is continuous, which is why the
intervention is environmental rather than seasonal.

The practical upshot is that if you want to know where the moisture problem is,
look in late summer. Whatever is still damp then is the reservoir.

## What a silverfish finding tells us about the house

More than most pests, this one is worth reading as information.

A persistent silverfish population means part of the structure holds high humidity
continuously, and that has consequences well beyond the insects. Sustained damp in
a crawlspace or wall cavity supports fungal decay in the framing, degrades
insulation performance, and creates conditions for the wood-destroying insects
covered elsewhere on this site.

So when we find them, the useful part of the visit is frequently not the treatment
plan but the moisture map — where the readings are high, what is producing it, and
what that is likely doing to the building over time.

That is why a silverfish call sometimes turns into a conversation about a vapor
barrier, a bath fan, or a downspout. It is not an upsell. It is the same finding
described accurately.

## Firebrats, briefly

Worth their own note because the place to look is completely different.

Firebrats want heat rather than damp — they thrive at temperatures that would dry
a silverfish out. So they turn up around water heaters, furnaces and boilers, in
the runs beside heating ducts, in insulation next to a warm flue, and in
commercial settings around ovens and mechanical plant.

The food is the same: starch, paper, glue and dry goods. The damage is the same.
And the treatment approach is the same — find the harborage, treat the voids,
reduce the food, and where possible reduce the humidity, because they still need
some.

What changes is the search. Looking for firebrats in a cool damp crawlspace is
looking in the wrong place, and it is a common reason a job stalls. If your
insects are mottled gray-brown rather than silver, tell us, and we will start at
the water heater rather than under the floor.
