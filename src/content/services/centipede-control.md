## Two very different animals, one message

Both of these turn up for the same underlying reason, and both are worth reading
as information rather than as an infestation.

**Centipedes** are predators. One pair of legs per segment, flattened bodies,
fast, and equipped with modified front legs that deliver venom to prey. They hunt
insects and other invertebrates.

**Millipedes** are detritivores. Two pairs of legs per segment, rounded bodies,
slow, and they curl into a spiral when disturbed. They eat decaying plant
material and are entirely harmless.

Neither damages structures, food, fabric or furnishings. Neither breeds
successfully in a dry heated house. And both need consistent moisture, which is
why finding them means something about your building or your grounds is holding
water.

In this county that puts them on the same list as
[silverfish](/services/silverfish-control/),
[earwigs](/services/earwig-control/), Oriental
[cockroaches](/services/cockroach-control/) and
[moisture ants](/services/ant-control/) — a group of pests whose real message is
about damp.

## House centipedes, which are on your side

The one that generates most of the calls, and the one where the honest advice is
hardest to hear.

**What it looks like.** Grayish-yellow with dark stripes, fifteen pairs of very
long legs, long antennae, a body under two inches but a leg span that makes it
look considerably larger. It moves in fast bursts and stops dead, which is
precisely the behavior people find alarming.

**What it does.** It hunts. Silverfish, small cockroaches, spiders, ants, larvae,
and other soft-bodied insects. It is one of the more effective predators that
lives in buildings, and unlike almost everything else on this website it is
actively reducing your other pest problems.

**Can it bite?** Yes, in principle — the modified front legs can penetrate skin.
In practice it is uncommon, because their first response to anything large is to
run. Where it does happen the result is generally a mild localized sting that
resolves quickly. There is no meaningful medical concern here.

**What its presence means.** This is the useful part. House centipedes are
predators, and predators go where the prey is. A house that develops a centipede
population usually has an insect population worth hunting — very often silverfish,
which need exactly the same humidity.

So the centipede is frequently the messenger rather than the message. Treating the
centipedes while leaving the damp and the prey in place changes nothing, because
the conditions that supported one will support the next.

We understand that "your alarming fast insect is beneficial" is not what most
people want to hear. It is still the accurate answer, and the practical
consequence — deal with the moisture and the prey — is the one that actually
resolves it.

## Millipedes, and the autumn migrations

The other common call, and it usually arrives all at once.

Millipedes live in soil, leaf litter, mulch, compost and rotting wood, feeding on
decaying plant material. They are slow, harmless, and normally invisible.

**Then it rains hard.** When soil becomes saturated — which in Whatcom County means
most of October and November — millipedes move in numbers looking for less
waterlogged ground. Large aggregations travel across lawns, driveways and patios,
meet a foundation, and follow it. Some find a gap.

Indoors they are in trouble immediately, and the reason is the exact opposite of
what people assume. Humidity is what a millipede needs; what it cannot tolerate
is the dryness of a heated house. UC IPM puts it as millipedes dying quickly
indoors from dry conditions and lack of food, and they are generally gone within
a day or two, usually curled into their characteristic spiral. So a household
finding dozens of dead millipedes along a wall after a wet week is seeing the end
of a migration rather than the start of an infestation.

**They do not breed indoors, damage anything or bite.** Crushed, some species
release a defensive fluid that can stain surfaces and irritate skin, so vacuum
rather than sweep.

**And saturation is not the only trigger.** Drought moves them too, and the
sources are explicit about it. UC IPM frames the general case as migration when
the food supply dwindles or the living space becomes either too wet or too dry.
The Alabama extension service records the same mass movement following heavy
rain that lands after a hot dry spell. Locally that is the smaller of the two
events, but a hot August followed by the first real rain will produce a version
of it, and a household that has been told to expect this only after flooding
will be looking for a leak that is not there. The insect is responding to the
moisture in the ground going wrong in either direction.

## Garden and soil centipedes

Worth mentioning because they get lumped in and they are not the same animal.

Soil centipedes are long, thin, pale and slow-moving, found under rocks, boards
and in soil. They are predators of soil invertebrates, they are beneficial in a
garden, and they do not establish indoors. Finding one under a flowerpot is
normal.

## What actually resolves both

The same list, because the driver is the same.

**Dry the crawlspace.** A functioning vapor barrier, working screened vents, and
drainage that keeps groundwater out. On older Bellingham housing this is
frequently the entire reservoir for both animals and for their prey. See
[crawlspace cleanout and restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/).

**Ventilate the wet rooms.** A bathroom extractor that is adequately sized, vents
to the exterior rather than into the attic, and runs long enough to clear the
moisture.

**Dehumidify basements** and persistently damp utility spaces.

**Clear the perimeter.** Pull mulch, leaf litter and compost back from the
foundation, lift boards, pots and firewood off the soil, and thin dense ground
cover directly against the wall. That band immediately around the building is
where the population lives.

**Fix the exterior water.** Gutters, downspouts discharging away from the house,
grading, and irrigation that does not spray the siding.

**Close the gaps.** Door sweeps, weatherstripping, window and vent screening,
utility penetrations, and sill plate gaps. This is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work.

**Reduce the prey**, for centipedes specifically. If there are silverfish or small
cockroaches in the building, the centipedes are there for a reason and dealing
with the food supply is more durable than dealing with the hunters.

**Then treat where it helps.** A targeted exterior perimeter band is genuinely
useful during a millipede migration, and harborage treatment around a crawlspace
has a role. Interior spraying is rarely the answer, because both animals are
transient indoors.

## Why we usually recommend leaving centipedes alone

Stated plainly because it costs us work.

If you have a small number of house centipedes and no other visible problem, the
sensible response is to vacuum the ones you see, address the humidity, and let
them do their job in the meantime. They are consuming exactly the insects you
would otherwise be calling about.

Where we do treat is when the population is large enough to be a genuine
quality-of-life problem, when someone in the household has a real phobia — which
is a legitimate reason and not one we dismiss — or when the underlying prey
population needs dealing with anyway and the centipedes come along with it.

What we will not do is sell a recurring treatment plan for a beneficial predator
that arrived because of a bathroom fan.

## Around the county

**Older housing with damp crawlspaces** — Columbia, Sunnyland, York, Roosevelt and
the Lettered Streets. Post-and-pier foundations, unsealed crawlspaces and original
bathroom ventilation give both animals a continuous reservoir directly beneath the
living space. The highest volume of house centipede calls in the county.

**Shaded lots near the lake and the greenbelts** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach,
Edgemoor, Whatcom Falls. Deep leaf litter, ground that never dries, and heavy
autumn millipede movement against foundations.

**Finished basements** anywhere in the older parts of the city, where a warm damp
room with stored cardboard supports silverfish, which in turn supports centipedes.

**Newly landscaped developments** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain and newer
Ferndale stock. Fresh deep mulch to the siding produces heavy millipede migrations
in the first few autumns after planting.

**Rural properties** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Compost, manure, field
margins and stacked material, with migrations that can be genuinely large.

## What you can do

**Vacuum rather than crush**, particularly millipedes, which can stain.

**Pull the mulch back** from the foundation and lift anything lying on the soil.

**Run the bathroom fan properly**, and check that it actually vents outside rather
than into the attic — a surprisingly common defect in older homes here.

**Get a dehumidifier** into a damp basement.

**Fit door sweeps**, which stop most of what walks in during a migration.

**Deal with the silverfish** if you have them, because that is what the centipedes
are eating.

**And in a migration week, wait.** Millipede movements last days rather than
weeks, and the animals coming inside are dying as they arrive.

## What people try that does not help

**Interior spraying along baseboards.** Both animals are transient indoors. The
ones you treat were dying anyway, and it does nothing to the reservoir.

**Bug bombs.** No reach into the damp voids where either lives, product where
people live, and no effect on the conditions.

**Killing centipedes while leaving silverfish.** The most self-defeating version.
Remove the predator and the prey population it was suppressing does better.

**Sealing the house without drying it.** Exclusion helps, but a damp crawlspace
that is sealed is still a damp crawlspace, and both animals live down there quite
happily without needing to come up.

**Treating during a millipede migration and calling it a success.** The migration
was going to end in a few days regardless. The perimeter work matters for next
autumn, not for this week.

**Diatomaceous earth broadcast indoors.** It works slowly on some insects in dry
conditions, which is precisely the opposite of the conditions here, and it is
difficult to remove from carpet. In a dry crawlspace or void it has a legitimate
narrow use; on a living room floor it does not.

## When it is genuinely worth treating

Having spent this page recommending restraint, here is the other side.

**A large, established population** that persists year-round rather than arriving
seasonally, particularly in a finished basement or a heavily used lower floor.

**Repeated millipede migrations** into a property where the landscaping and grading
cannot easily be changed — a perimeter program timed to autumn genuinely reduces
what gets in.

**Where the prey population is the real job** and the centipedes are incidental.
Treating a silverfish or cockroach problem resolves both, and that is a legitimate
service rather than treating a predator.

**Where someone in the household has a genuine phobia.** House centipedes are
among the most commonly phobia-triggering insects we deal with, and a household
where somebody cannot use a bathroom at night has a real problem regardless of
what the animal does ecologically. We will treat, and we will still tell you what
the underlying driver is.

**Commercial and institutional settings** where appearance standards apply —
hospitality, healthcare, food handling — and the tolerance is legitimately zero.
See [commercial programs](/commercial/).

## Reading the building from the insect

This is worth stating as a general principle, because it applies across most of
this site and centipedes and millipedes are the clearest example.

Some pests tell you about food — cockroaches, pantry beetles, ants at a spill.
Some tell you about access — rodents, wasps in a wall void. And some tell you
almost nothing about themselves and a great deal about the building.

Centipedes, millipedes, silverfish, sowbugs, pillbugs, moisture ants and dampwood
termites are all in that third group. None of them can establish without sustained
moisture, and none of them is doing much harm on its own. What they collectively
indicate is that part of the structure is holding water — and that condition is
doing damage regardless of which insect happens to be reporting it.

A crawlspace wet enough to support millipedes is wet enough to support fungal
decay in the joists above it. A bathroom humid enough for silverfish is depositing
that moisture somewhere. The insects are the visible part of a problem whose
expensive part is invisible.

So when we come out for centipedes and spend most of the visit with a moisture
meter and a flashlight in the crawlspace, that is not scope creep. It is reading
the actual message.

## The seasonal shape

**Winter and early spring.** Both are present but quiet, living in the damp voids
and soil. Occasional centipede sightings in bathrooms as they hunt.

**Late spring.** Activity rises with warmth. Centipede sightings increase because
the animals are moving more, not because numbers changed dramatically.

**Summer.** The most consistent period for house centipede indoor sightings,
particularly at night in bathrooms and basements. Millipedes are largely invisible
unless a dry spell drives them to seek moisture.

**Autumn — and this is the one that matters.** Heavy October and November rain
saturates soil and triggers millipede mass movement. This is when the foundation
lines fill up and the calls arrive in clusters after a wet week.

**And the year-round point:** a damp crawlspace has no season. If the reservoir is
under the floor, the population is continuous and the sightings simply track how
much anyone is looking.

## If you would rather not kill them

A note for the households — and there are plenty in this county — who would prefer
to relocate rather than treat.

Both animals are easy to catch and move. A cup and a piece of card works, and
neither is fast enough to be difficult once cornered, despite the centipede's
reputation. Put them outside under leaf litter or mulch, well away from the
foundation, and they will do better there than they were doing in your bathroom.

For millipedes during a migration, a broom and a dustpan and a walk to the back of
the garden handles a wall's worth in a few minutes, and they are heading somewhere
anyway.

Where we get involved in those households is usually the moisture work rather than
the pest work — a vapor barrier, a bathroom fan, a dehumidifier — which resolves it
without killing anything at all. That is a legitimate way to approach this and we
are not going to talk anyone out of it.

## What to tell us when you call

**Which animal.** Fast with long legs, or slow and curling into a spiral. That
single detail sends the visit in different directions.

**Where and when.** A bathroom at night is a different picture from a garage in
October, and a foundation line after heavy rain is a third thing again.

**Whether it is one or many.** A single house centipede is normal in a Pacific
Northwest home. Several a week suggests a prey population worth finding.

**Whether you have seen silverfish**, which is the most useful single piece of
information for a centipede call and the one people rarely think to mention.

**And what the crawlspace is like**, if you know. Standing water, a torn barrier,
or a musty smell from the floor vents all point at the reservoir before anyone
opens the hatch.

## What it means for an older Bellingham home

A short closing note, because these two animals are so strongly associated with a
specific housing type here.

The prewar city stock — post-and-pier foundations, unsealed crawlspaces, original
bathroom ventilation and a century of settlement — produces most of the centipede
and millipede calls in this county, and it produces them for structural reasons
rather than anything to do with how the house is kept.

That is worth saying to owners of those houses, because the calls often come with
an apology attached. There is nothing to apologize for. A crawlspace built in 1925
was not built with a vapor barrier, and the fix is a project rather than a habit.
