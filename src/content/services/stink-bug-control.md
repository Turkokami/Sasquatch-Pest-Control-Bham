## One behavior, several insects

Four different insects generate almost identical calls in this county every
autumn, and they all want the same thing.

**Brown marmorated stink bug.** Shield-shaped, mottled brown, with distinctive
alternating light and dark bands on the antennae and along the abdominal edge.
Invasive, agriculturally significant, and established in Washington — though at
an earlier stage of that than the mid-Atlantic news coverage suggests. There is
a fuller account of what the state and county records actually say further down.

**Western conifer seed bug.** Long, narrow, brown, with noticeably flared hind
legs. Native, associated with conifers, and a strong flier that lands with an
audible thump. Frequently mistaken for a stink bug and occasionally for something
far more alarming.

**Boxelder bug.** Black with distinct red or orange markings, gathering in large
aggregations on sunny walls. Associated with boxelder and other maples.

**Cluster flies**, which behave identically and get their own treatment on the
[fly control](/services/fly-control/) page.

Different insects, one problem. In autumn they need somewhere sheltered to pass
the winter, and a heated building with gaps in it is the best option available.

## What actually happens

The sequence is the same every year and understanding it tells you when to act.

**Late September and October.** As nights cool, adults seek warmth. They locate it
by temperature, which is why they mass on the elevations that hold afternoon sun —
south and west walls, and particularly large unshaded ones. On a warm October
afternoon a wall can be covered.

**They work inward.** Not by chewing — they have no capacity for that — but through
gaps that already exist: around window and door framing, under siding laps, at
soffit returns, through attic and gable vents, around utility penetrations, and
under loose flashing. The openings they need are small.

**They overwinter in the voids.** Wall cavities, attics, and the spaces above
ceilings, largely inactive. Most of the population is never seen.

**They emerge on warm days.** A mild spell in December, or the first real warmth of
spring, raises the temperature in those voids. Some wake, become active, and move
toward light — which means your windows, your light fixtures, and the inside of
your house rather than the outside.

That last step is why people believe they are breeding indoors. They are not.
Every insect appearing at your window in February entered the building the
previous October.

**And in spring the survivors leave**, heading back outdoors to feed and breed.
Nothing about the indoor phase involves reproduction.

## What they do not do

Worth being explicit, because these calls come with more alarm than they warrant.

They do not bite or sting. They do not carry disease. They do not feed on anything
inside a house — no food, no fabric, no timber, no paper. They do not damage the
structure. They do not reproduce indoors. They do not infest in the sense that
word usually carries.

What they do is arrive in quantity, which is genuinely unpleasant when the
quantity is in the hundreds, and produce odor when disturbed or crushed.

We say this plainly because an aggressive and expensive treatment is easy to sell
against a wall covered in insects, and it is mostly not the right purchase.

## The odor, and why not to squash them

Stink bugs earn the name. Disturbed or crushed, they release defensive compounds
that are strongly unpleasant and persistent, and on some surfaces the fluid
stains.

So the working advice is: **vacuum, do not crush.** And for stink bugs
specifically, use a vacuum you can empty outside immediately, because the odor
transfers into the canister and then into every room you subsequently vacuum. A
stocking or filter insert in the hose, discarded afterward, is the tidy version.

Boxelder bugs stain too — their crushed bodies leave marks on light-colored walls,
curtains and upholstery.

Conifer seed bugs also produce an odor, milder than a stink bug's.

## Why we will not treat inside the walls

This is the most important thing on the page from a service standpoint, and it is
a case where the obvious-sounding solution is the wrong one.

People frequently ask us to treat the wall voids where the insects are
overwintering. We decline, because a treatment that kills a large aggregation
inside a cavity leaves the bodies there.

A substantial accumulation of dead insects in a wall void is food. It attracts
**dermestid beetles** — carpet beetles and their relatives — which develop in the
accumulation and then move inward into the living space, where they damage
textiles, carpets and stored natural fibers. Households that have a wall void
treated for overwintering insects sometimes end up with a carpet beetle problem
the following year, and that one genuinely does damage things.

So the honest answer once they are inside is unsatisfying but correct: vacuum the
ones that appear, tolerate the season, and do the exclusion work in late summer so
next winter is different.

## The treatment that does work, and when

**Exterior application in late summer**, applied to the elevations they gather on
before they arrive, does reduce how many get in. Timing is everything — it has to
be in place before the aggregation starts, which in this county means late August
into September. The same application in November is treating a wall the insects
have already passed through.

One caveat on that exterior application: it is a residual sitting on siding, and
siding here gets rained on. Autumn weather shortens how long it stays effective,
which is another reason the sealing is the half that keeps working after the
product has gone.

**Exclusion is the durable half**, and it is the one that keeps working. The gaps
these insects use are the same ones that admit
[cluster flies](/services/fly-control/), and closing them is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work: sealing around
window and door framing, at siding laps and trim junctions, screening attic and
gable vents with intact material, closing soffit returns, and sealing utility
penetrations.

Because these insects are small and do not chew, sealant and screen are sufficient
here — this is one of the few exclusion jobs where hardware cloth and sheet metal
are not required.

**And it overlaps with the rodent window.** The right month to seal a house
against overwintering insects is the same month to seal it against rodents moving
in with the rain. One project, several problems, which is why we schedule this
work in August and September.

## Brown marmorated stink bug, specifically

Worth separating out because it is the one with a story beyond nuisance.

It is invasive and agriculturally significant. It feeds on a very wide range of
hosts and damages tree fruit, berries, grapes, hazelnuts and vegetable crops by
feeding on developing fruit, causing dimpling and corking that renders produce
unsaleable.

It is also established in Washington, and that word deserves more care than it
usually gets. WSU's tree fruit entomology program, which keeps the state record,
had the species in 30 counties by December 2022, concentrated along the I-5
corridor. Locally the picture is thinner and more recent than that number
implies. The survey published in WSU's Whatcom Ag Monthly turned up fewer than
ten adults and no nymphs at all in Whatcom County in 2017, and its authors
inferred local establishment largely from the confirmed populations across the
line in British Columbia rather than from what they caught on this side of it.

So the accurate description is a species still filling in its range rather than
one that has finished. That is a long way from the mid-Atlantic states, where
covered walls became a routine autumn event within a few years of arrival, and
it is why the count on a Whatcom wall in October is usually tens rather than the
hundreds people have read about.

It is also why a sighting here is worth reporting. WSU builds the Washington
distribution map from what the public sends in: a photograph, a general location
and the address it was found at, to tfrec.reportBMSB@wsu.edu. Whatcom Ag Monthly
adds that a suspect insect can be taken to a local extension office instead. When
the county record rests on a survey that found single figures, one clear
photograph from a kitchen window is a real contribution to a map that growers
use, and it costs a minute.

That matters here because Whatcom County grows things. For a berry or tree fruit
operation this is a production pest rather than a household nuisance, and the
management conversation is entirely different from the one a homeowner in
Bellingham needs.

For a homeowner: it is still just a bug on a wall in October, and the advice above
is the whole advice.

If you are growing commercially and seeing them in numbers, that is worth
discussing with WSU Extension as well as with us, because the monitoring and
management guidance for agricultural settings is theirs and it is better than
anything a pest control company should be improvising.

## Around the county

**Newer construction on open ground** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain, and the
newer stock around Ferndale and Lynden. Large unshaded south and west elevations
are exactly what these insects locate, and modern housing frequently has more
uninterrupted sunlit wall than older stock. The sealing is straightforward because
the detailing is better.

**Older city neighborhoods** — Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York.
Smaller elevations but far more gaps: original window framing, siding that has
moved, unsealed soffits and penetrations added over a century. Fewer insects
landing, more of them getting in.

**The agricultural corridor** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Both the
household version and the production version, and boxelder bugs in quantity where
maples are present.

**Around Lake Whatcom and the forest edge** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach,
Edgemoor. Western conifer seed bugs dominate here for the obvious reason, and they
are the species most often reported as something more alarming than they are.

**Waterfront and seasonal** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Houses closed up
through autumn accumulate populations with nobody to notice, so owners return in
spring to windowsills full of them.

## What you can do

**Vacuum rather than crush**, and empty it outside.

**Seal in late summer.** Windows, doors, siding laps, vents, penetrations. It is
the entire solution and the timing is the whole trick.

**Screen the vents** — attic, gable, soffit and dryer — with intact material.

**Fit or repair door sweeps and weatherstripping.**

**Cut back vegetation** touching south and west walls, which gives them staging
and shelter against the elevation.

**Do not bug bomb.** Total-release foggers do not reach wall voids, and killing
insects in the cavity creates the dermestid problem described above.

**Do not use exterior insecticide in November** hoping to catch them. They are
already in.

## Telling them apart

Four insects, one behavior, and the identification only matters for the odor and
for the agricultural question.

**Shield-shaped, mottled brown, banded antennae** — brown marmorated stink bug.
The alternating light and dark bands on the antennae and along the edge of the
abdomen are the reliable marks, because several native stink bugs look broadly
similar and are not the invasive one.

**Long and narrow, brown, with flared hind legs** — western conifer seed bug. The
flattened, leaf-like expansion on the hind leg is unmistakable once seen. Strong,
noisy flier, and the species most often reported to us as something alarming.

**Black with red or orange lines, gathering in dense groups** — boxelder bug.
Nymphs are bright red and cluster on sunlit surfaces in numbers, which alarms
people more than the adults do.

**Dark, hairy, sluggish, and a fly rather than a bug** — cluster fly. See
[fly control](/services/fly-control/).

None of them bites. None of them damages the structure. The only practical
difference between them indoors is how badly they smell when crushed, and the
answer for all four is to vacuum rather than find out.

## Why the same house every year

Because nothing about the building changed.

These insects find warmth, and the elevation that held sun last October holds it
this October. The gaps they used are still there. And for some species there is
evidence of aggregation cues — chemical signals left by previous gatherings that
make a site more attractive to the next generation, which is part of why a
particular wall becomes reliably the wall.

The practical implication is encouraging rather than depressing: it makes the
problem predictable. If a house had them last winter it will have them this
winter, which means the sealing work can be scheduled in August on the basis of
last year's experience rather than waiting to see.

It also means the improvement is durable. Unlike a treatment that has to be
repeated, a sealed elevation stays sealed, and the year after a proper exclusion
job is usually dramatically different.

## The overwintering calendar

**August.** The window opens. Nothing has gathered yet, the weather is workable,
and this is when exterior treatment and sealing both do their most good. If a
house had a problem last winter, this is the month to act on it.

**September.** Still workable, and the last comfortable point. Gathering begins
late in the month in a warm year.

**October.** Peak aggregation. Walls covered on sunny afternoons, and insects
working inward daily. Treatment now catches some of what is still outside and
none of what is already in the cavity.

**November and December.** They are in. Nothing useful is applied to a wall at
this point. First warm spell produces the first indoor appearances.

**January and February.** The confusing months, when a mild week wakes part of the
population and people find insects at windows in the middle of winter with no
apparent source.

**March and April.** Emergence in earnest as the voids warm, then departure. The
population leaves on its own to feed and breed outdoors.

**May through July.** Absent from buildings entirely. A good window for the
sealing work if late summer is not convenient, though August remains the ideal.

The shape of it is simple: the problem is created in a four-week window in autumn
and experienced across five months of winter. Acting in the window is the entire
strategy.

## What people try that does not work

**Bug bombs and foggers.** They do not reach wall voids, which is where the
population is. What they do reach is the living space, which is where the
population is not. And on the rare occasion they do kill an aggregation in a
cavity, that produces the dermestid problem described above.

**Ultrasonic repellers.** No effect, sold in volume.

**Sealing in November.** Understandable and counterproductive. The insects are
already inside, and closing their exits keeps them there for the whole winter
instead of letting some leave on warm days.

**Treating the interior.** These insects are not feeding indoors and are not
traveling treated surfaces in any consistent way. Interior application achieves
very little and puts product where people live for no benefit.

**Waiting to see if it happens again.** It happens again. The building has not
changed, and a house that gathered them last October will gather them this
October.

**Screening only the obvious vents.** They use gaps far smaller and less obvious
than a vent — a lifted siding lap, the gap where a window frame meets the sheathing,
a soffit return that was never closed. A partial seal produces a partial result.

## If it is a rental

Worth a note, because these calls come from tenants as often as owners and the
answer is usually structural.

The gaps that let overwintering insects in are a building condition, not a
housekeeping one, and no amount of tenant behavior changes them. A tenant cannot
seal siding laps or screen a gable vent, and a tenant treating the interior
achieves nothing for the reason described above.

So the useful conversation is with whoever controls the building envelope. For a
managed property that generally means the owner or the management company, and the
work belongs in the same category as any other exterior maintenance.

The good news for an owner is that it is a one-time job with a durable result, and
it closes the same gaps that admit rodents and cluster flies — so a single late
summer project reduces three sources of tenant complaint at once.
