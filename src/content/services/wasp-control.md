## The late-summer problem

Almost every wasp sting in Whatcom County happens in a six-week window, and
understanding why makes the whole season predictable.

A yellowjacket or hornet colony is annual. It starts in spring with a single
overwintered queen building a nest the size of a golf ball and raising the first
few workers herself. Through summer those workers take over and the colony
compounds. By late August a western yellowjacket nest that was invisible in June
can hold thousands of individuals, and the colony is at maximum size, maximum
defensiveness and maximum food demand all at once.

At the same time, for one group of them, the diet flips. Early in the season
workers hunt insects and spiders to feed protein to developing larvae — which
makes them genuinely useful in a garden and is why we do not treat every nest we
see. Late in the season brood production winds down, the larvae that were
feeding the adults sugar stop, and the workers go looking for carbohydrate
elsewhere. That is why the wasps that ignored you at a June barbecue are in your
drink in September.

That flip is not universal, and knowing which insects it applies to saves people
money every year. UC IPM separates the ground- and cavity-nesting yellowjackets
in the genus *Vespula* from the aerial nesters in *Dolichovespula* — the
bald-faced hornet and the aerial yellowjacket — and only the first group turns
scavenger. The aerial species stay predatory right through to the death of the
colony, hunting flies and caterpillars in September exactly as they did in June.

That distinction pays for itself immediately. The black-and-white insect with the
football-shaped gray nest in the maple is essentially never the one walking
around a plate at a September picnic table, and neither is an open-comb paper
wasp under the eaves. Something is at the table, but it is coming from a hole in
a bank, a rodent burrow or a gap in the siding that nobody has found yet.
Treating the visible aerial nest because it is the one you can point at deals
with the wrong colony, costs money and changes nothing about the table. When
somebody calls us about wasps at an outdoor eating area, the first job is
working out where they are actually flying home to.

Then the first hard frost kills the colony outright. Only newly mated queens
survive, overwintering under bark, in leaf litter or in structures, to start over
in spring.

Two practical consequences follow. First, a nest found in September is a much
bigger and more defensive job than the same nest in June, which is why we say
that if you saw a nest last year in a particular spot, the cheap time to act is
early. Second, an old yellowjacket nest is not a future colony: WSU bulletin
EB0643 records that abandoned nests decompose over the winter and are not used
again. That rule holds without qualification for every yellowjacket and hornet
on this list. It has exactly one local exception, which is covered below.

## What we actually have here

**Bald-faced hornet.** Despite the name this is a yellowjacket, not a true
hornet. Large, black and ivory-white, and it builds the football-shaped gray
paper nest people photograph — hanging from a branch, a soffit, a shrub or
occasionally a wall. Genuinely defensive within several feet of the nest. This
is also the insect most often reported to us as a giant hornet.

**Aerial yellowjacket.** Similar enclosed paper nests in shrubs, trees and under
eaves, smaller and yellow-marked.

**Western yellowjacket.** The ground nester, and the one responsible for most
stings in this county. It nests in abandoned rodent burrows, under root balls, in
retaining walls and in landscape voids — meaning the nest is invisible until
someone runs a mower over it or steps on the entrance. Aggressive when disturbed,
and highly attracted to food and drink in late summer.

**German yellowjacket.** Introduced, and notable because it readily nests inside
structures — wall voids, attics and soffits — rather than in the ground. When we
get a wall-void call, this is often what it is.

**European paper wasp.** Now common across the Pacific Northwest. Slimmer, longer
legs trailing in flight, and easily confused with a yellowjacket by coloring. It
builds the open, umbrella-shaped comb with visible cells rather than an enclosed
paper envelope, and it nests in small cavities constantly — under eaves, in
mailboxes, in gas grills, in pipe ends, in patio furniture tubing. Far less
defensive than a yellowjacket.

This is also the exception to the single-use rule above. Penn State records
queens of this species founding colonies earlier than the natives partly by
taking over the previous year's structure, and WSU PestSense puts it as queens
occasionally reusing old structures although they typically build new ones. The
colony still dies in autumn; the paper is what persists, and a comb left in a
sheltered cavity can give next April's queen a running start. Clearing old combs
out of grills, pipe ends and furniture tubing over winter is the cheapest thing
anybody can do about this wasp, and it involves no treatment at all.

Whether it is pushing our native paper wasps out is genuinely unsettled, and we
would rather say so than pick the more dramatic version. Penn State reports
evidence of an adverse impact on native *Polistes*. Colorado State Extension,
looking at its own state, says there is no evidence of a significant effect on
the natives there. Both are extension entomology sources and they do not agree,
so the honest summary is that the introduced wasp has spread fast and that the
case against it is suggestive rather than settled.

**Native paper wasps.** Same open comb, browner, generally placid. We frequently
leave them alone when the location allows it.

## About the northern giant hornet

This deserves its own section here, because Whatcom County is where it actually
happened and the local memory of it is genuine.

The species now called the northern giant hornet — earlier widely reported as
the "murder hornet" — was first confirmed in Washington State in late 2019, and
the detections were here. Four nests were located and eradicated in Whatcom
County between 2020 and 2021, in the Blaine and Custer area. After three years
with no confirmed detections, state and federal agriculture officials announced
in December 2024 that the species had been eradicated from the United States.

So the honest position is this. It was real, it was here, it was taken seriously,
and it was successfully eliminated. It is not something we expect to find on your
property in 2026.

In practice, essentially every report we receive turns out to be a bald-faced
hornet — large, black and white and startling — or an elm sawfly, which is a
harmless wood wasp that looks alarming and is not a hornet at all.

If you do genuinely believe you have seen one, report it to the Washington State
Department of Agriculture rather than to us. Agencies still want sightings, and a
photograph is worth far more than a description. What you should not do is
poison something you cannot identify on the assumption it might be one.

## Where the nest is decides the job

**Ground nests.** Western yellowjacket, entrance often no bigger than a coin,
usually found by accident. Dangerous specifically because they are invisible and
because mowing or digging provokes a mass response. Treatment goes into the
entrance and the colony traffic does the rest, but it has to be done properly and
at the right time of day.

**Aerial nests.** Bald-faced hornet and aerial yellowjacket. The visible ones.
Height and access set the difficulty, and the risk is largely about what happens
to a person on a ladder who gets stung.

**Open comb.** Paper wasps under eaves, in grills, in furniture tubing.
Straightforward, and frequently there are several small nests around a property
rather than one large one.

**Wall voids and attics.** The one that genuinely needs a professional. A colony
inside a structure enters through a small exterior gap — a gap in siding, a vent,
a utility penetration — and builds in the cavity. Two things make this different.
The nest is not accessible from outside, so treatment has to reach a space you
cannot see. And the failure mode is severe: if the exterior entrance is sealed
while the colony is alive, the wasps chew laterally looking for another way out,
and the path of least resistance is frequently drywall. People seal a hole in
their siding and then have wasps in the bedroom.

Never seal a wall-void nest. Treat it, confirm it is dead, then seal.

## Safety, said plainly

Wasps sting repeatedly — unlike honey bees they do not lose the sting — and a
disturbed colony will pursue. A yellowjacket ground nest hit by a lawnmower can
deliver dozens of stings in seconds.

The risks worth taking seriously are three. **Allergic reaction**, which for
someone with a known sting allergy is a medical emergency and a reason not to go
anywhere near the nest. **Sting volume**, because even without allergy, a large
number of stings is a genuine medical event. And **the ladder**, which is
underrated — a significant share of serious wasp injuries are falls by someone
who got stung at height rather than the stings themselves.

Add to that the fact that yellowjackets are most defensive in exactly the season
when people are most likely to find them, and it is why we would rather you call
than climb.

If someone in the household carries an epinephrine auto-injector for stings, tell
us when you call. It changes how we schedule and how we work the site.

## What about bees?

We do not kill honey bees, and we will tell you when what you have is bees rather
than wasps.

A **honey bee swarm** — a hanging cluster on a branch or fence, often thousands
of bees, usually gone within a day or two — is a colony in transit. It is
generally not aggressive, and local beekeepers will frequently collect one at no
charge. That is the right outcome and we will point you toward it.

An **established honey bee colony inside a structure** is a different matter.
That is comb, brood and honey inside a wall, and resolving it properly means
opening the cavity, removing the colony and the comb, and repairing — because
abandoned comb melts, stains and attracts secondary pests. It is a removal and
repair job rather than a pest treatment, and we will be straight with you about
what it involves.

**Bumble bees** nest in small colonies in ground cavities and insulation, they
are important pollinators, and they are rarely worth treating. Where a nest is in
a genuinely bad location we will discuss it, but the default answer is to leave
them.

The insects that get mistaken for bees most often are the European paper wasp and
various hoverflies, which are harmless mimics.

## How we work a wasp job

**Identify and locate.** Species and nest type change the method entirely, and a
surprising number of calls turn out to involve two nests rather than one. Where
the entrance is a gap in siding we want to know what is behind it before we
treat.

**Time it.** Wasp work is best done when the colony is home and least active —
early morning or dusk — rather than in the middle of a warm afternoon with half
the workers out foraging. Treating at the wrong hour leaves a returning
population with nowhere to go and no colony to return to, which is worse for
everyone.

**Treat the nest, not the wasps.** The objective is the colony, delivered at the
entrance or into the void, not a knockdown of whatever is flying.

**Remove where it makes sense.** Accessible aerial nests come down once the
colony is dead. Wall-void nests sometimes stay in place, because opening a wall
to extract paper is not always worth doing — but the entrance is sealed
afterward, and only afterward.

**Seal, so it does not recur.** The gap that let a colony into your wall will let
another one in next spring. Closing it is the part that lasts, and it usually
belongs with our
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work.

**Come back if it is still active.** A wall-void colony occasionally needs a
second visit, and that is covered under our
[100% Service Guarantee](/our-guarantee/).

Roosevelt. A century of siding, trim and utility penetrations means more cavity
entrances than newer stock, and German yellowjackets in wall voids are common.

**Newer hillside and edge** — Alabama Hill, Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain.
Soffits, deck framing and open eaves. More aerial and paper wasp work.

**Rural and agricultural** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Outbuildings,

around livestock and fruit are a working nuisance rather than an occasional one.

**The lake and forest margins.** In Sudden Valley, Silver Beach and Edgemoor it is ground nesting
in banks and root balls, and property where people are outdoors in exactly the
places the nests are.

**Waterfront** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Decks, sheds and
seasonal homes where a colony can build all summer with nobody there to notice.

## Commercial wasp work

Late-summer wasp pressure is a scheduling problem for commercial sites rather
than an emergency, and it is one of the few pest issues where the calendar is
predictable enough to plan around.

**Food service and outdoor dining.** Yellowjackets at patio tables in late
August are a customer experience problem and occasionally a liability one. The
work that matters happens in spring — removing founding nests before they
establish — plus waste handling and bin placement through the season.

**Schools and childcare.** Ground nests in playing fields and playground
margins, and paper wasp nests in covered walkways and equipment. These sites
carry notification requirements under Washington law for pesticide application,
and they are also the sites where the sting risk lands on children, so we
inspect on a cycle rather than waiting for a report.

**Agriculture.** Yellowjackets around fruit, livestock and equipment through
harvest are a working hazard for the people who have to be there. Volume is
higher and the nests are frequently in stacked material and outbuildings.

**Marinas and waterfront.** Covered moorage, sheds and boat storage give
sheltered nest sites, and the people who encounter them are usually on a dock or
a ladder.

**Property management and HOAs.** Wall-void colonies in multifamily buildings
are the recurring one, because an entrance gap in shared siding serves the
building rather than a unit, and residents report it late.

For all of these the useful arrangement is a spring sweep plus response during
the season, which is part of our [commercial programs](/commercial/).

## Reducing them next year

The highest-value intervention is in **spring**, and almost nobody does it. In
April and May a founding queen is building alone, the nest is tiny, and the whole
colony is one insect. Knocking down small starter nests under eaves and in
sheltered corners at that stage prevents the September problem entirely, and it
carries almost no risk.

Beyond that: keep bins closed and rinsed, deal with fallen fruit, cover food and
drink outdoors in late summer, seal gaps in siding, screen vents, plug open pipe
ends and check the inside of gas grills and patio furniture tubing before the
season. Fill abandoned rodent burrows, which are where western yellowjackets most
often set up.

And a note on traps — commercial lure traps catch foragers, which reduces
nuisance around a specific area, but they do not eliminate a colony. They are a
comfort measure for a deck, not a control method.

## What to do right now, if there is a nest

If it is not in anyone's way, the honest answer is often to leave it. A
bald-faced hornet nest high in a tree at the back of a lot is doing no harm and
will be dead by December. Paper wasps under a shed eave are useful predators.

If it is in the way, keep people away from the immediate area and call. Do not
knock it down, do not spray it with a hose, and do not attempt an aerial nest
from a ladder. Wasps defend a radius around the nest rather than the nest itself,
so the mistake people make is getting close enough to be inside that radius while
holding something that requires both hands.

If someone has been stung multiple times, or has any difficulty breathing,
swelling away from the sting site, or a known sting allergy, that is a medical
call rather than a pest control one. Make it first.

If the nest is in a wall, note where the insects are entering and leave it
completely alone until we get there.
