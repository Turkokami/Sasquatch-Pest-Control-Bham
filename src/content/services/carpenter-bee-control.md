## Two different problems, and hole size separates them

Everything on this page starts with measuring a hole.

**About half an inch, perfectly round, in exposed softwood** — fascia, deck rails,
handrails, eaves, unpainted siding, outbuilding timber. That is a carpenter bee,
and it is a surface-level nuisance with a specific fix.

**About the diameter of a pencil lead, in crawlspace joists, subfloor or sill
plates, with fine powder beneath** — that is an anobiid powderpost beetle. In
Whatcom County this is the one that actually matters, and it is a moisture story.

**Tiny pinholes in hardwood flooring, furniture or trim**, with talcum-fine powder,
suggests lyctid powderpost beetles, which attack hardwood rather than structural
softwood.

Get the identification right and the rest of the job follows. Get it wrong and you
are treating a deck rail for a crawlspace problem.

## Carpenter bees

**What they look like.** Large, roughly bumble bee sized, but with a **shiny,
hairless black abdomen** — that is the distinguishing feature. Bumble bees are
fuzzy all over. Carpenter bees have a densely haired thorax and a bare, glossy
rear.

They are less abundant here than in the eastern and southern states where they
generate most of the online advice, which is worth saying because a great deal of
carpenter bee content is written for a much larger problem than Whatcom County
actually has.

**What they do.** Females excavate a nearly perfect round entrance hole into
unfinished or weathered softwood, then turn and tunnel along the grain, creating a
gallery where eggs are laid in provisioned cells. They do not eat the wood —
they excavate it, like carpenter ants, and the sawdust is pushed out.

**The bluffing male.** The bee that hovers and darts at you when you walk past the
deck is a male defending territory, and males have no stinger. It is entirely
display. Females can sting and essentially never do unless handled or trapped.

**Where they choose.** Unpainted or weathered softwood, preferentially: fascia
boards, deck rails and joists, handrails, eaves and soffit edges, fence rails,
outbuilding timber, and the underside of anything horizontal and unfinished.
Painted and well-finished wood is markedly less attractive, which is the basis of
most of the prevention.

**Why it accumulates.** Galleries get reused and extended year after year, and
new females frequently nest near where they emerged. A rail with one hole this
year may have four next year, and the tunneling behind them can be considerably
more extensive than the exterior suggests.

**The woodpecker problem.** Worth knowing about, because it does more visible
damage than the bees. Woodpeckers hear or detect larvae in galleries and open the
wood up to reach them, converting a neat half-inch hole into a torn strip of
fascia. If you have fresh woodpecker damage on a fascia board, there is usually a
carpenter bee gallery behind it.

## Wood-boring beetles, which are the local one

If you own an older home in this county with a damp crawlspace, this is the
wood-boring insect you are most likely to actually encounter.

**Anobiid powderpost beetles** attack softwood — the structural framing, joists,
subfloor and sill plates in a crawlspace. The signs are small round exit holes
roughly the diameter of a pencil lead, with fine powdery frass accumulating
beneath them or streaking down a joist.

**The moisture requirement is the whole story.** Anobiids need wood with elevated
moisture content, and they will **re-infest the same timber generation after
generation** as long as it stays damp. This is why crawlspace beetle problems
recur endlessly under repeated treatment and stop when the space is dried.

It is exactly the same argument as
[dampwood termites](/services/termite-control/) and
[moisture ants](/services/ant-control/), and it usually turns out to be the same
crawlspace.

**Lyctid powderpost beetles** are a different situation — they attack the sapwood
of hardwoods with high starch content, which means flooring, trim, cabinetry and
furniture rather than framing. The frass is the field test, and it works by
touch: lyctid frass is loose and flour-fine, almost like talc, while anobiid
frass is gritty between the fingers because it is made of compressed fecal
pellets. Lyctid infestations usually arrive in the wood itself rather than
starting in place.

**Old house borer** attacks softwood framing and is notable for the audible
rasping some people report from inside timber. It is worth naming only to rule
it out: it is an eastern species and is not established in western Washington,
so rasping in a local wall is far more likely to be an anobiid, a wood-boring
weevil in decayed timber, or carpenter ants.

## Active or old?

The most useful diagnostic on the page, and one that saves people money.

Exit holes persist indefinitely. A beam with holes made in 1962 looks much the
same in 2026, and a great many "infestations" reported to us are historical damage
in wood that has since dried out.

**The frass test.** Clear away all the powder and debris beneath the suspect area
— brush it off, vacuum it, wipe it down. Note the date. Come back in two to four
weeks. Fresh powder means active. Nothing means the damage is old and no treatment
is warranted.

**Other indicators.** Fresh holes have clean, sharp, pale edges; old ones are
darkened, dusty and weathered. Fresh frass is loose and light-colored; old frass
is compacted and gray. And moisture readings in the surrounding timber tell you
whether the wood could currently support a population at all.

We do this test rather than quoting from the sight of holes, because "you have
wood-boring beetles" is an easy and expensive thing to say about a beam that has
been quietly inert for forty years.

## How we work it

**Identify and measure.** Hole diameter, frass texture, location and timber type.

**Establish whether it is active**, using the frass test where the timing allows,
rather than assuming.

**Take moisture readings.** For anobiids this is the diagnosis rather than a
supporting detail.

**Trace the moisture source.** Crawlspace ground moisture and missing vapor
barrier, blocked or absent venting, plumbing leaks, gutters and downspouts,
grading, deck ledger flashing. This is where the job actually is.

**Treat where treatment is warranted.** Borate products penetrate bare, accessible
wood and are well suited to crawlspace framing, sill plates and subfloor —
unfinished timber that can be reached. They are not effective through paint,
sealer or finish, which is a real limitation and worth saying.

**For carpenter bees**, treat the gallery, confirm activity has stopped, and only
then fill and seal.

**Correct the conditions.** For anobiids this is
[crawlspace restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/) and ventilation work,
and it is the part that decides whether any of the rest holds.

**Assess the structure honestly.** Where a member has lost meaningful section, that
is a carpenter's job, and we will say so rather than treating around it.

## Sealing carpenter bee holes, in the right order

Worth its own note because people reliably get this backwards.

**Do not fill an active gallery.** Sealing the entrance with an insect inside
produces a new hole beside the repair within days — they are perfectly capable of
chewing a fresh exit. It also traps developing larvae in a gallery where they will
die and, in enough numbers, feed
[carpet beetles](/services/beetle-control/).

**The order is:** treat the gallery, wait and confirm no further activity, then
fill with wood filler or a tight-fitting dowel, then paint or seal the surface.

**Timing.** Late summer or autumn, once the current generation has emerged, is the
cleanest window. Filling in spring while a female is provisioning cells is the
wrong moment.

**Then finish the wood.** Paint and solid stain are the genuine deterrent —
carpenter bees strongly prefer bare or weathered timber. A fascia board that gets
painted after repair is substantially less likely to be chosen again. Clear
finishes and semi-transparent stains help less.

## Around the county

**Decks and outbuildings everywhere.** Carpenter bee damage tracks unfinished
softwood rather than neighborhood, so it turns up wherever there are weathered
deck rails, fence posts and barn timber — which in this county is most rural and
lakeside property.

**The old city housing.** Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York and South

hand with the moisture problems described across this site. This is the majority
of our wood-boring beetle work.

**Around Lake Whatcom.** Sudden Valley, Silver Beach and Edgemoor bring 1970s
cedar, extensive decking and shaded lots that never fully dry — good conditions for
both groups at once.

**The farm country.** Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas, where barns,
outbuildings, fence lines and stored lumber give carpenter bees unlimited
unfinished softwood.

**Waterfront properties.** Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo, where salt air is
hard on finishes and weathered exposed timber is the norm.

## What we will not do

**Treat inactive damage.** Old exit holes in dry timber need no product.

**Fumigate.** It is not a Whatcom County treatment for a Whatcom County problem,
and if it has been proposed to you here it is worth a second opinion.

**Sell a recurring plan for wood-boring beetles.** If the wood is dry they cannot
re-establish; if it is wet, the answer is drying it, not visiting annually.

**Fill carpenter bee holes on the first visit** while a gallery is still active.

**Treat around structural decay.** Wood that has lost section needs replacing, and
applying product to it is covering a problem rather than fixing it.

## Telling wood damage apart

Four different insects leave marks in structural timber and they are routinely
confused. The distinctions are practical rather than academic, because they send
the job in four directions.

**Clean round exit holes with powder below, no galleries visible on the surface** —
wood-boring beetle. The tunneling is internal and you only see the exits.

**Rough galleries following the grain, with fibrous shavings pushed out that
contain insect body parts** — [carpenter ants](/services/ant-control/). They
excavate rather than eat, and the frass is shredded rather than powdery.

**Galleries packed with soil-like material, or mud tubes on a foundation wall** —
[subterranean termites](/services/termite-control/), which are uncommon on this
side of the Cascades but present.

**Galleries in wet wood with hard fecal pellets rather than shavings** — Pacific
dampwood termite, the local termite species.

**One perfectly round half-inch hole with a tunnel turning sharply along the
grain** — carpenter bee.

Frass texture is the single most useful clue. Powder means beetle. Shavings with
body parts mean ants. Hard pellets mean termites. Photograph what you find and
leave it in place, because sweeping it up removes the evidence.

## Timing

**Carpenter bees.** Adults emerge in spring and females begin excavating and
provisioning. The nuisance is most visible in late spring and early summer, when
males are hovering. The right time to treat, fill and finish is late summer or
autumn, after the current generation has emerged, which is why we often book this
work months after the call.

**Wood-boring beetles.** Adults emerge and produce new exit holes in spring and
early summer, which is when fresh frass appears and people notice. Development
inside the timber takes a long time — frequently a year or more, longer in cool
damp wood — so the population you are seeing reflects conditions from previous
years.

**And the moisture side has its own season.** A crawlspace tells you the truth in
February, when a drainage problem is actually running, rather than in August when
everything looks dry. If you want to know whether a beetle problem has a future,
look at the space in winter.

## Preventing carpenter bee damage

Almost entirely a finishing question, and it is one of the few pest problems where
ordinary property maintenance is the whole answer.

**Paint or solid stain the vulnerable timber.** Carpenter bees strongly prefer
bare and weathered softwood, and a properly finished surface is markedly less
attractive. Fascia, deck rails, handrails, exposed beam ends and fence rails are
the targets worth finishing. Clear finishes and semi-transparent stains help less
than solid coverage.

**Keep the finish maintained.** A board that was painted twelve years ago and has
weathered back to bare wood is no longer protected. The maintenance cycle is the
protection.

**Cap and cover exposed end grain**, which is where excavation frequently starts,
with trim, flashing or paint.

**Repair damage promptly.** Filled and finished galleries are much less likely to
be reoccupied than open ones, and open galleries attract new females.

**Consider material at replacement.** Where a rail or a fascia is being replaced
anyway, composite or a treated and finished product takes that element out of
the picture — carpenter bees do not excavate it, so the cycle of reoccupation
stops there.

**And do not let woodpecker damage sit.** Open, torn wood is both an invitation
and a moisture entry, and the moisture side matters more for the structure than
the bees do.

## What we find in a crawlspace, and what it means

Because most of our wood-boring beetle work happens under floors, it is worth
describing what an assessment actually looks at.

**Moisture readings across the framing.** Sill plates, rim joists, joists and
subfloor. This is the number that decides whether an infestation has a future,
and it is why we bring a meter rather than a flashlight alone.

**The vapor barrier.** Present, absent, torn, or shifted aside by whoever last ran
a duct. Its condition explains most of the readings above it.

**Standing water and drainage.** Whether groundwater is entering, whether a
downspout discharges under the house, and whether the ground stays saturated.

**Wood on the ground.** Offcuts, form boards, buried lumber and stored material,
each of which is a reservoir within feet of the structure.

**Exit holes and frass, mapped rather than noted.** Where they are concentrated
tells us which part of the structure is wettest, which usually points straight at
the source.

**Ventilation.** Blocked, screened, missing, or painted shut.

What you get in writing from us is a record of the treatment performed — the
product used, the members and locations treated, and the date. We are not
structural pest inspectors and we do not issue a document setting out findings
of wood-destroying-organism damage; that is a separately licensed activity. What
we will do is talk you through what we are seeing while we are standing in it,
because the practical point is usually the same either way: applying borate to
timber that will be wet again next winter is a way of spending money without
changing anything, and the drainage is the job.

## Buying or selling a property

Wood-boring insect damage turns up in property transactions more than any other
pest on this site, and it is worth knowing how to read it.

Most of what gets flagged is **old and inactive**. Exit holes in a beam are a
permanent record of something that may have finished decades ago, and a report
noting their presence is not the same as a finding of active infestation.

The questions that matter are whether there is fresh frass, what the moisture
readings say, and whether any member has lost meaningful section. Those three
answers determine whether this is a repair item, a monitoring item, or nothing at
all.

Ask whoever assessed it which of the three they established, and whether they used
a moisture meter. If the answer is that holes were observed, that is an
observation rather than an assessment.
