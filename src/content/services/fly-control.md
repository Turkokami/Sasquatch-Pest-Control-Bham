## Every fly job is a source-finding job

This is the whole page in one idea, so it goes first.

The flies you can see are adults, and adults are the shortest and least important
part of the life cycle. Somewhere there is a moist, organic breeding site
producing more of them continuously — a drain, a bin, a bag of forgotten
potatoes, a houseplant that gets watered too often, an animal that died in a wall.

Kill the adults and the site keeps producing. Every fly problem that "keeps coming
back after treatment" is a source that was never found.

So the useful skill in fly work is not application, it is diagnosis. And the most
useful diagnostic tool is the species, because **which fly you have tells you where
to look.**

There is one exception, and in Whatcom County it is a big one.

## Cluster flies, the local exception

If you get a large number of slow, dark, slightly hairy flies at upstairs windows
on the first warm day of winter, you have cluster flies, and almost everything
above does not apply.

**They do not breed in your house.** Cluster fly larvae develop outdoors, in soil,
as parasites of earthworms. There is no source indoors to find and no sanitation
problem to fix. Your house is not dirty and there is nothing rotting in the wall.

What happens is this. In autumn, as temperatures drop, adults gather on the warm
sunlit sides of buildings — south and west walls take the worst of it — and work
their way into gaps around window frames, siding, soffits, attic vents and utility
penetrations. They overwinter in wall voids and attics, largely dormant.

Then a warm spell in December or February raises the temperature in those voids,
some of them wake, and they move toward light. Light means your upstairs windows,
which is why they appear indoors in the middle of winter having apparently come
from nowhere.

**The fix is exclusion, and the timing matters.** Sealing gaps around windows,
siding, soffits and vents before October keeps them out. Doing it in January seals
in the ones already there. This is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work, and it is best
scheduled in late summer alongside the rodent-season sealing.

A vacuum is the right tool for the ones already inside. Treatment inside a wall
void kills flies you cannot reach and leaves them there, which on a large
population creates its own secondary problem.

Cluster flies do not bite, do not carry disease of consequence, and do not
contaminate food the way house flies do. They are a nuisance of scale rather than
a risk, which is worth knowing before anyone panics.

## What the other species tell you

**Fruit flies.** Small, often red-eyed, hovering around kitchens and bars. The
source is fermenting material: overripe produce, spillage under an appliance,
recycling that has not been rinsed, a mop bucket, the residue in a drain, or the
bottom of a bin. Almost always within a few feet of where you see them.

**Fungus gnats.** Small, dark, weak fliers drifting around houseplants and windows.
The source is constantly damp potting soil, where larvae feed on fungi and organic
matter in the top layer. This is a watering habit, not an infestation. Let the top
inch or two dry properly between waterings and the cycle breaks.

**Drain flies.** Small, fuzzy, moth-like, sitting motionless on walls near sinks,
showers and floor drains. The source is the gelatinous organic film lining the
pipe. Covered below, because everyone treats these wrong.

**House flies.** The familiar one. The source is garbage, manure or decaying
organic material, usually outdoors and usually within a short distance. Around the
agricultural corridor this is a working reality rather than a housekeeping issue.

**Blow flies and bottle flies.** Large, metallic blue or green, sluggish indoors.
Outdoors they are normal. **Indoors, in numbers, they mean something has died** —
in a wall void, under a floor, in an attic, or in a chimney. If there is also an
odor, that is effectively a diagnosis. This is a carcass removal job, and it links
straight back to [rodent](/services/rodent-control/) and
a wildlife specialist rather than us.

**Phorid flies.** Small, humpbacked, and notable for running in erratic bursts
rather than flying. These matter because of where they breed: decaying organic
material in damp, hidden places — under a slab, around a broken sewer line, in a
long-neglected drain. In commercial kitchens a persistent phorid population is a
recognized signal of a plumbing failure under the floor rather than a sanitation
problem above it.

## Drains, and why bleach does not work

The single most common wasted effort in fly control.

Drain flies breed in the biofilm — the soft organic layer coating the inside of
the pipe above the water line. That film is the food source, the harborage and the
nursery.

Bleach runs through it. It contacts the surface for a second or two, then it is
gone down the drain, and the film that produced the flies is still there. The same
is true of boiling water and most consumer drain products, which are formulated to
clear blockages rather than to remove a biological layer.

**What works** is mechanical plus biological. A stiff drain brush run up and down
the pipe wall physically disrupts the film. Then an enzymatic or bacterial
bio-foam that expands to contact the whole pipe wall and stays there long enough
to digest what remains.

**Finding which drain** is worth doing before treating all of them. Tape a clear
plastic bag or a piece of tape sticky-side-down over a drain overnight; flies
emerging get caught, and you have your answer in a morning rather than treating
six drains on principle.

Floor drains, rarely used guest bathrooms, condensate lines and the overflow in a
sink are the ones people forget.

## How we work a fly job

**Identify the species.** It is the diagnostic, and it usually narrows the search
to one or two possibilities immediately.

**Find the source.** Drains, bins and their surrounds, under and behind
appliances, produce storage, houseplants, recycling, mop and cleaning equipment,
condensate lines, and where blow flies are involved, wall voids and attics.

**Remove or correct it.** This is the actual treatment. Sanitation, drain work,
watering habits, waste handling, or carcass removal depending on what we find.

**Treat where it helps.** Targeted application and light traps have a role,
particularly in commercial settings, but they support the source work rather than
replacing it.

**Exclude.** Door sweeps, window and vent screening, and the gap sealing that
cluster flies specifically require. See
[exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/).

**Follow up on the identification.** If flies persist after a source is removed,
the identification was wrong or there is a second source. Both happen, and both
are worth saying out loud rather than repeating a treatment.

## Commercial fly work

Different in emphasis rather than method, and this is where fly control earns its
keep.

**Drains are the number one source** in food service, and they are the most
consistently under-serviced part of a kitchen. A drain maintenance program is
usually the highest-value item on a restaurant's pest program.

**Grease and organic buildup** behind and beneath equipment, in floor seams and
around drain grates. It accumulates where nobody cleans because nobody can reach.

**Waste handling.** Dumpster placement, lid condition, how often it is emptied,
and whether the pad beneath it is ever washed. A dumpster on an unwashed pad
produces flies indefinitely regardless of what happens indoors.

**Doors and air movement.** Door sweeps, self-closing doors, air curtains and
screening on anything that opens to the outside.

**Phorid flies as a plumbing signal.** Worth repeating: a persistent phorid
population in a commercial kitchen frequently indicates a broken drain line under
the slab. Treating the surface will not resolve it and the useful next call is a
plumber.

For food-handling accounts the documentation is part of the deliverable, and light
traps are placed and serviced on a schedule with the catch recorded. See
[commercial programs](/commercial/).

## Around the county

**The agricultural corridor** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. House flies and
stable flies around livestock, manure and feed, at volumes that are a working
condition rather than an infestation. Management here is about waste handling,
moisture and timing rather than about treating a house.

**Older city neighborhoods** — Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York.
Cluster flies in quantity, because older siding, original window framing and
unsealed soffits give them exactly the gaps they need. Also the most likely place
to find blow flies indicating something dead in a wall.

**Newer construction on open ground** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain, and the
newer stock around Ferndale. Large unshaded south and west elevations are cluster
fly magnets in October, and the sealing work is straightforward because the
detailing is better.

**Food service and retail** — downtown, Fairhaven and the commercial corridors.
Drains, waste and deliveries.

**Waterfront and seasonal** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Houses closed up
through winter that fill with cluster flies unnoticed, so owners arrive in spring
to windowsills full of them.

## What you can do first

**Identify roughly where they are.** Which room, which window, near which
appliance. Flies concentrate near their source, and that alone narrows it a long
way.

**Check the obvious sources.** Produce, the bottom of the bin, under and behind
the refrigerator, recycling, the mop bucket, the dog food bin, and any plant that
gets watered often.

**Tape the drains overnight** if they are small flies near a sink or shower. It
identifies the specific drain without guesswork.

**Let houseplant soil dry** between waterings for two weeks and see whether the
problem goes.

**Note the season.** Sluggish flies at upstairs windows in winter is cluster
flies, and it means calling in August for sealing rather than in January for a
treatment.

**And if there is a smell**, tell us on the phone. It changes what we bring.

## The fly year in Whatcom County

**Late winter and early spring.** Cluster flies waking in wall voids on warm days
and appearing at upstairs windows. Nothing is breeding — this is last autumn's
population emerging on the wrong side of the glass. Vacuum and plan the sealing.

**Spring.** Fungus gnats as houseplants get watered more and greenhouse growing
starts. Drain flies as activity increases in kitchens and bathrooms that were
quiet over winter.

**Early summer.** Fruit flies begin as produce moves faster and warmer
temperatures speed development. House fly pressure builds around agricultural
operations.

**Late summer.** Peak for fruit flies and house flies. Waste handling matters more
than anything else in this window — a bin emptied weekly in February is a
different object in August.

**Early autumn.** The cluster fly gathering. Adults congregate on warm south and
west walls through October and work into the building. **This is the window that
decides your winter**, and it is why the sealing work belongs in late summer.

**Late autumn and winter.** Blow flies indoors become more meaningful, because
rodents and wildlife are moving into structures and some of them die there. A
handful of large sluggish flies in a heated house in December is worth
investigating rather than swatting.

## Fly identification, simply

Without a microscope, size and behavior get you most of the way.

**Tiny, hovering near fruit or drains** — fruit fly. Look for red eyes on the
common one.

**Tiny, drifting weakly around plants and windows** — fungus gnat. Poor fliers,
often seen walking on soil.

**Small, fuzzy, sitting still on a wall near a drain** — drain fly. They do not
fly much; they sit.

**Small, running in erratic bursts rather than flying** — phorid fly. The running
is diagnostic, and it points at something decaying in a hidden damp place.

**Medium, gray, active, in kitchens and around waste** — house fly.

**Large, metallic blue or green, slow indoors** — blow fly. Outdoors normal.
Indoors in numbers, look for a carcass.

**Large, dark, slow, in quantity at winter windows** — cluster fly. The
give-away is the season and the sluggishness.

Photograph it if you are unsure. Identification is free and it decides where we
look.

## Sanitation that actually matters

Not general tidiness — the specific things that produce flies.

**Rinse the recycling.** A beverage container with residue is a fruit fly nursery,
and a bin of them in a warm kitchen produces a population in days.

**Wash the bin, not just empty it.** The film in the bottom is the breeding site,
and emptying leaves it behind. The same goes for the pad under an outdoor
dumpster.

**Look under the appliances.** Spillage that ran under a refrigerator or a stove
months ago is one of the most common fruit fly sources we find, and one of the
least looked at.

**Check the forgotten produce.** A bag of potatoes or onions at the back of a
pantry accounts for an astonishing share of these calls.

**Deal with mop buckets and cleaning water.** Standing dirty water is a breeding
site, particularly in commercial settings where the bucket lives in a warm room.

**Empty and clean drip trays.** Refrigerators, dehumidifiers and air conditioners
all collect water somewhere, and it is rarely cleaned.

**Fallen fruit outdoors**, which in this county means plum and apple trees in late
summer feeding a population that then comes indoors.

None of that is a substitute for finding the source. All of it removes the sources
before they start.

## Light traps, and where they belong

Worth a note because they are frequently sold as a solution and are really a
monitoring tool.

An ultraviolet light trap catches flying insects that are already inside. It does
not address a source, and in a room with a live breeding site it simply fills up.
Where it earns its place is commercial: as a monitor whose catch tells you whether
pressure is rising or falling, and as a supplement in food-handling areas where a
small residual population is unacceptable.

Placement decides whether it works. Away from doorways where it would draw insects
in from outside, out of direct sight of the exterior, at the height flies actually
travel, and never over a food preparation surface. A trap in the wrong place is
worse than no trap.

Residential light traps are rarely worth buying. If flies are a problem in a
house, something is producing them, and the trap is treating the symptom at a
lower rate than the source is generating it.
