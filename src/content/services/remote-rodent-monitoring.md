## What it is

Remote monitoring is a sensor fitted to the rodent devices in a commercial
program. The stations and traps stay exactly where a technician decided they
should go; the sensor is what changes, and what it changes is how quickly
anyone finds out that a device did something.

The equipment is [Skyhawk Trapmate](https://trapmate.ai/). A small unit clips to
a snap trap or to the lid of a tamper-resistant bait station and reports
activity at that device. On a site running dozens of devices across a building
and its yard, that turns a monthly walk into a running record. The
tamper-resistant station is not a house preference, incidentally: the
[EPA's restrictions on rodenticide products](https://www.epa.gov/rodenticides/restrictions-rodenticide-products)
are the reason bait sits inside a protected, secured block rather than loose, and
a sensor on the lid leaves that arrangement exactly as it was.

**It goes on the devices you already have.** That is the part worth knowing
before anyone quotes you: Skyhawk's sensors mount to existing traps and
stations rather than requiring proprietary ones, so adopting monitoring does
not mean replacing the equipment on your site.

**Three ways of sensing, for three kinds of device.** Skyhawk publishes a
vibration sensor that reads the movement of a trap firing, a magnetic reed
switch that reads a trap arm or a door moving, and a passive infrared sensor
that reads the body heat of an animal inside a multi-catch trap. Which one goes
on a device depends on the device — a snap trap, a station and a multi-catch
box each report differently.

**The hub talks over cellular.** The sensors report to a hub, and the hub
reports out over the cellular network rather than over your Wi-Fi, so it does
not need a password, a port, or a conversation with your IT department.
Skyhawk states a range of more than 500 feet between hub and sensors, and
battery life of five years or more on a set of two AA cells in the infrared
sensor. Batteries are replaceable; nothing is hard-wired.

## What you get out of it

**Interaction counts.** How many times each device recorded activity, by
device, rather than an impression of how the site is doing.

**Times of the interactions.** When they happened. A dock station that reports
at three in the morning and a break-room trap that reports mid-shift are two
different problems with two different fixes, and the clock is what separates
them.

**Trend logs in your service reports.** The count from one visit is a
measurement; the same count across eight weeks is information. Trend data is
what shows a program working, and it is what shows a new pressure arriving
before anyone sees an animal.

**A notification when a trap deploys — to us.** That is the part that changes
the response. When a device fires, we are told, and a technician is scheduled
to go and service it rather than the trap sitting until the next routine
visit. On an account where a sprung trap left in place is a finding in its own
right, that gap is what the sensor closes.

## Why the alert comes to us and not to you

This is a deliberate choice and it is worth explaining, because most of the
industry sells it the other way.

An alert is only useful to somebody who can act on it. A notification at 2 a.m.
that station 14 has recorded activity does nothing for a facilities manager who
cannot get into the building and would not service the device if they could.
The same notification routed to us produces a technician.

If your operation wants the raw feed as well — some quality managers do,
particularly where the pest file is part of an audit — say so and we will set
that up. The default is the one that ends with the device actually serviced.

## Where it earns its money

Remote monitoring is worth most where a device being left unattended is
expensive or where the site is too large to read by walking it:

**Food processing and cold storage.** Long runs of exterior stations, interior
devices in areas with restricted access, and an audit that asks what happened
at each one.

**Restaurants and food service.** Small device counts, but a sprung trap behind
a line during service is not something anyone is going to notice until the next
visit.

**Warehouses and distribution.** Dock doors open all day, a perimeter longer
than the building, and enough devices that ranking them by activity is the only
way to prioritize.

**Multifamily and property management.** Shared structures where the question
is always which unit or which run the population is actually using.

**Any account with a documented program.** If your pest file is read by an
inspector, a landlord, or a customer's auditor, device-level history answers
questions that a signature on a service ticket does not.

## What a sensor does not do

This matters more than the feature list, and we would rather say it here than
have you find out later.

A sensor reports that something happened at a device. It does not identify what
tripped it. It does not know whether you have Norway rats moving in from a
greenbelt or house mice breeding in a wall void, and those are opposite jobs.
It does not find the gap under the dock leveler that let them in, and sealing
that gap is still the work that ends the problem rather than managing it.

It also does not fix a program that is wrong. Devices in the wrong places
report from the wrong places, faster. The value of the data is downstream of
the walkthrough that decided where the devices went, which is why this is an
add-on to a program rather than a product sold on its own.

## How it goes on an account

**At the walkthrough.** We look at what you run now, where the devices are,
and where a hub can sit so the far end of the site still reaches it. Skyhawk
puts the hub-to-sensor range past 500 feet, which is generous and is still a
number a long building, a yard fence line or a concrete basement can eat, so it
is better answered by walking it than by assuming it.

**On the scope.** Which devices get sensors and what the monitoring adds to the
program is written into the same scope as the rest of the work, with the count
on it.

**In service.** The devices keep being serviced on the schedule. The
notifications decide which ones we visit between scheduled days, and the
reporting is folded into the record your file already keeps.

**At review.** The trend log is the part worth reading together. Two or three
services in, it usually starts saying something about the building that nobody
had noticed.
