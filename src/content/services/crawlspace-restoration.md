## What is actually under most houses here

Crawlspaces in this county are wetter than almost anywhere else in the
continental United States, and a great many of them have never been properly
finished.

The typical older Bellingham crawlspace has bare soil or a barrier that was
installed decades ago and has since torn, shifted or been pulled aside by
someone running a duct. Ground moisture evaporates continuously into that space.
Some of it leaves through the vents when they are clear, and a great deal of it
goes upward into the joists, subfloor and sill plates.

That is the mechanism behind most of what we get called about. Wood at elevated
moisture content supports [dampwood termites](/services/termite-control/),
[moisture ants](/services/ant-control/), anobiid beetles and fungal decay — none
of which can establish in dry lumber. Add a failed vent screen and you have
[rodents](/services/rodent-control/) as well, in a space nobody looks at.

The crawlspace is where the majority of a Whatcom County pest problem physically
lives, and it is the part of the house homeowners have least information about.

## When restoration is warranted

Not every crawlspace needs this work, and we would rather say so than sell it.

**It is warranted when** insulation has been used as nesting or is saturated,
when there is significant droppings and urine contamination, when the vapor
barrier is missing, shredded or was never installed, when insulation has fallen
off the joists and is lying on the ground holding moisture, when there is
standing water or persistently saturated soil, or when moisture readings in the
framing are high enough to support decay and wood-destroying insects.

**It is not warranted when** the barrier is intact and sealed, insulation is dry
and in place, and there is no contamination or active moisture source. A dusty
crawlspace is not a problem. A dry one with old, harmless spider webbing is not a
problem either.

We will tell you which one you have, and we will show you photographs rather than
asking you to take our word for it, because you are not going to crawl under
there to check.

## The order matters more than anything

This is the single most important thing on this page.

**Rodents out first.** Exclusion and trapping, confirmed quiet over several
visits. New insulation installed while a population is still active is nesting
material you paid for. Any company willing to re-insulate before the animals are
gone is arranging to sell you the same job twice.

**Then remove.** Contaminated insulation, droppings, nesting material, old torn
barrier, construction debris, and any wood left on the ground by whoever built
the house.

**Then treat the surfaces.** Appropriate disinfection of the soil surface and the
framing where contamination was heaviest.

**Then the moisture work.** Vapor barrier properly lapped, sealed and run up the
foundation walls where appropriate. Vent screening that is both rodent-proof and
actually open to airflow. An access door that seals and latches and can still be
opened by someone who needs to get in.

**Then re-insulate**, into a space that is now dry and closed.

Skip a step or run them out of order and the result is a well-insulated,
freshly sealed home for the next population.

## The health side, stated plainly

Rodent droppings and urine carry pathogens, and dried material becomes airborne
when it is disturbed. That is the whole reason this work has a method rather
than being a matter of a shovel and a bin bag.

The one to name specifically is **hantavirus**, which in the western United
States is associated with deer mice — making it a rural and forest-edge concern
here rather than a downtown one. The transmission route is inhaling aerosolized
material from dried droppings, urine and nesting.

So the practical rule for anyone thinking of doing this themselves: **do not
dry-sweep and do not vacuum.** Ventilate the space first, wet the material down
with a disinfectant, use gloves and appropriate respiratory protection, and bag
it rather than sweeping it.

We are not raising this to frighten anyone into hiring us. It is a genuinely
manageable risk with the right handling, and if you would rather do it yourself
you should know how.

## What you get beyond pest control

A meaningful part of the value here has nothing to do with insects, and it is
worth counting when you are deciding whether the job is worth it.

**Insulation that works.** Batting that has been nested in, compressed or
saturated has lost most of its R-value. Replacing it restores the performance you
already paid for once.

**Warmer floors.** The most common thing customers report afterward, and it is
simply the insulation doing its job again.

**Less decay in the framing.** A dry crawlspace does not support fungal decay in
the joists and sill plates above it. Over a decade that is a structural
maintenance saving rather than a comfort one.

**Better air.** Air moves upward from a crawlspace into the living space through
every penetration in the floor. A contaminated crawlspace is contributing to what
you breathe upstairs.

**Fewer secondary pests.** Rodent nesting hosts mites and dermestid beetles, and
when the rodents leave those move inward looking for somewhere else to be.

## Vapor barrier or full encapsulation?

Worth being straight about, because encapsulation is heavily marketed and is
frequently more system than a Whatcom County house needs.

**A vapor barrier** is sheeting across the ground, lapped and sealed, that stops
soil moisture evaporating into the structure. Combined with working vents and
sensible drainage, this is the right answer for most homes here and it is
dramatically cheaper.

**Encapsulation** seals the entire space — ground and walls — and generally adds
a dehumidifier or conditioned air. It has real applications: a crawlspace with a
persistent groundwater problem that cannot be drained, or one where ducts and
equipment live and need to be inside the thermal envelope.

The honest position is that a properly installed barrier, screened vents and
corrected drainage solves the great majority of what we see. Where the bigger
system is genuinely warranted we will say so, and where it is not we will tell
you that too — and encapsulating a space that still has a drainage problem
underneath it is a way of sealing the problem in.

## Around the county

**Older Bellingham, above all.** The Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York and South Hill stock. Post-and-pier, low clearance, bare soil or a barrier from another
era, and a century of gutter history. The highest concentration of this work in
the county, and often the only real fix for a recurring insect problem upstairs.

**Around Lake Whatcom** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor. Sloped lots
push water toward foundations, and 1970s construction against a forest edge
brings rodents in through vents that have been open for years.

**Rural and agricultural** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Larger footprints,
outbuildings, and deer mice rather than house mice, which raises the handling
requirements.

**Waterfront and seasonal** — Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. Homes
standing empty through the wet months, where nobody notices a vent screen that
failed in November until April.

**Newer construction** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain. Usually better
detailed, and when we do find a problem there is normally a specific cause: a
downspout discharging under the house, or irrigation running against the
foundation.

## What comes out, and where it goes

Worth describing, because "cleanout" is vague and people reasonably want to know
what is actually happening under their floor.

**Contaminated insulation.** Batting that has been nested in, urine-saturated or
has fallen from the joists comes down and is bagged. On a badly affected
crawlspace this is the bulk of the volume, and it is the part that most changes
how the space smells afterward.

**Droppings and nesting material.** Wetted down first, never swept dry, bagged
and removed rather than raked to one side.

**The old barrier.** Torn plastic that has been shifted and walked on for
decades traps moisture underneath it rather than blocking it, so it comes out
before the new one goes down.

**Construction debris.** Offcuts, form boards, buried lumber and the general
archaeology of whoever built the house. Every piece of wood on the ground is a
colony site for [moisture ants](/services/ant-control/) and dampwood termites
within a few feet of the structure.

**Occasionally, the unexpected.** Abandoned ducting, a forgotten pet, stored
belongings from a previous owner, and on older properties the occasional buried
oil line. We tell you what we find rather than quietly bagging it.

Material is removed from the property. Nothing is pushed into a corner and
covered with new sheeting, which is a real practice and one you would never see.

## Timing

Late summer and early autumn is the best window, for the same reason it is the
right time for [exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/): it gets ahead of
the rodent push that comes with the September and October rain, and the space is
at its driest.

Winter is workable and occasionally better for diagnosis, because a drainage
problem is actually running in February rather than sitting invisible in August.
If you want to know what your crawlspace really does, that is when to look.

## Attics, which are the same job upside down

Everything above applies to attics with three differences worth knowing.

**Contamination spreads further.** Attic insulation is usually blown-in rather
than batted, so rodent contamination disperses through the material instead of
staying in identifiable sections. That tends to make removal all-or-nothing
rather than sectional.

**The entry points are higher.** Soffit returns, roofline junctions at a change
of pitch, gable vents and anywhere a branch or fence gives access to the roof.
The [exclusion work](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) happens on a ladder
instead of on your back.

**Squirrels and bats are not our work.** Squirrels do far more structural damage
than rats, chewing framing and wiring rather than just traveling it. Bats carry
seasonal restrictions — exclusion cannot happen during the maternity period, so
the work waits for the window rather than the schedule. Getting them out is a wildlife specialist's job rather than ours, and it is timing-led. We will do the restoration once the animals are properly out, but the removal is somebody else's job and we will say so.

The energy argument is stronger in an attic than a crawlspace, because that is
where most of a house's heat loss occurs. Replacing contaminated attic insulation
is usually the single largest efficiency gain available on an older Bellingham
home, and it happens to also be the pest fix. See
[attic insulation](/services/attic-insulation/).

## Rentals, sales and property management

Two situations where this work carries weight beyond the pest problem.

**Rentals.** Bellingham Municipal Code 6.15 requires rental properties to pass a
certified inspection, and the checklist flags foundation openings larger than a
quarter inch. A crawlspace restoration that includes vent screening and a sealed
access door addresses that directly, and the photographic record documents the
condition at a point in time. Turnover is the cheapest moment to do it — empty
unit, easy access, no scheduling around a tenant.

**Sales.** A crawlspace with contaminated insulation and a missing barrier is
something a buyer's advisor will find and price against, usually at more than the
work costs. Doing it before listing removes a negotiating lever. Doing it after
purchase is often the first thing a new owner of an older Bellingham home should
budget for.

## Questions worth asking any company

If you are getting more than one quote for this work — and you should — these
are the questions that separate them, whoever you end up hiring.

**Did they actually go under the house?** A quote produced from the access hatch
is a guess. Ask what the clearance is, what the barrier condition is, and where
the entry points are. Anyone who has been under there can answer.

**What is the sequence?** If exclusion and trapping do not come before
re-insulation, the job is being sold in the wrong order and you will be paying
for it twice.

**What barrier material, and how is it sealed?** Lapped, sealed and secured, or
loose sheeting laid on the ground. It makes the difference between a barrier and
a tarp.

**What happens to the old material?** Removed from the property, or pushed to a
corner and covered.

**Will you see photographs?** You are paying for work in a space you will never
enter. If nobody is offering to show you, ask why.

**What is out of scope?** Structural repair, drainage excavation and plumbing are
other trades. A company that says it will handle everything is either
subcontracting it or planning to work around it.

## After the work

A restored crawlspace does not need attention often, but it is worth knowing what
to keep an eye on.

**Check the vent screens once a year.** Spring is a good habit. They are the
first thing to fail, usually from weather rather than animals, and a screen that
has come loose reopens the whole envelope.

**Watch what other trades do.** A plumber, an electrician or an HVAC contractor
working under the house will move the barrier to get where they need to be, and
very few of them put it back properly. It is worth asking them to, and worth a
look afterward.

**Keep the exterior working.** Gutters clear, downspouts discharging away from
the foundation, and soil and bark mulch kept below the siding line. Most
crawlspace moisture originates outside the crawlspace.

**Note anything that changes upstairs.** A recurring insect problem in one room,
a floor that feels colder than it did, or a musty smell that returns are all
worth mentioning, because they are usually the crawlspace telling you something.
