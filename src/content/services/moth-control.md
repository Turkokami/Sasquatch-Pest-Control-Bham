## Two completely different problems

The word "moth" covers two situations that share nothing except the insect order.

**Clothes moths** feed on animal fibers — wool, cashmere, silk, fur, feathers,
leather. The larvae do the damage, in dark, undisturbed places, and the adults
avoid light and fly poorly.

**Pantry moths** breed in stored food. The larvae live in the package, and the
adults fly around the kitchen and toward lights, which is why you notice them.

They need different searches, different treatments and different prevention, and
the first thing we establish is which one you have. Frequently it is neither —
most moths people find indoors are harmless outdoor species that came in through
an open door and are circling a light.

## Clothes moths

**The two species.** The webbing clothes moth is the common one, leaving silk
tubes and mats on the fabric. The casemaking clothes moth constructs a small
portable case it drags around and feeds from — finding those little cases attached
to a garment or a rug is unmistakable once seen.

**Adults are small, plain and buff-colored**, roughly a quarter of an inch,
without conspicuous markings. They fly weakly, often in short low flutters, and
they hide when disturbed.

**They do not feed at all.** Adult clothes moths have no functional mouthparts.
They exist to mate and lay eggs. So killing the adults you see does nothing about
the damage, which is being done by larvae elsewhere — a point that explains why
so many households swat moths for a season and keep finding holes.

**Larvae do everything.** They eat keratin, which is the protein in wool, silk,
fur, feathers, hair and leather. They do not eat cotton, linen, viscose or
synthetics — unless those fabrics are soiled, in which case the residue supplies
what they need and blended fabrics get damaged along the way.

## Why they avoid light, and why that matters

This is the single most useful diagnostic on this page.

Clothes moths are negatively phototactic — they actively avoid light. So a moth
circling your lamp, tapping at a window, or gathering at a porch light is almost
certainly not a clothes moth.

It also tells you where to look. Damage happens where light does not reach and
where nothing is disturbed: the back of a closet, the underside of a rug beneath
furniture that never moves, inside a stored box, in an attic, under a bed, inside
a garment bag, in the folds of stored blankets, and in the seams and collars of
clothing hanging undisturbed at the end of a rail.

If a garment is worn regularly it is rarely damaged. The items that get eaten are
the ones nobody has touched since last winter.

## The soiling point

Worth understanding, because it explains the pattern people find baffling — one
ruined sweater beside five identical untouched ones.

Clothes moth larvae are drawn to keratin fibers carrying **sweat, body oils, food
residue, or urine**. Those residues supply moisture and additional nutrients, and
they make one garment substantially more attractive than the clean item next to it.

So the classic loss is the wool item worn once at the end of the season and put
away without cleaning. It goes into storage carrying exactly the cues that attract
egg-laying, and it comes out in autumn with holes while everything around it is
fine.

**Clean before storing** is therefore the single most effective preventive measure
available, and it costs nothing beyond what you would do anyway.

## What actually works on clothes moths

**Find and clean the source.** Everything in the affected area gets inspected and
cleaned. Dry cleaning or a hot wash kills all stages on washable items.

**Heat or cold for what cannot be washed.** Several days in a freezer, ideally
with a warm-up and a second freeze, kills eggs and larvae on delicate items. Some
items tolerate heat instead. This is how valuable textiles get treated without
chemicals anywhere near them.

**Vacuum thoroughly and repeatedly.** Carpet edges, under and behind furniture,
closet floors and shelves, along baseboards, and inside heating vents where lint
and hair accumulate. Empty it outside afterward.

**Treat harborage, not fabric.** Targeted application to closet structure, carpet
edges, under fixed furniture and along baseboards. We do not spray clothing or
bedding.

**Pheromone monitors** to confirm the population is actually falling rather than
relying on whether anyone has seen a moth lately. On a valuable collection these
are worth keeping in place permanently.

**Store properly afterwards.** Sealed containers rather than cardboard, garment
bags that actually close, and cleaned items only.

## Mothballs and cedar, honestly

Both are widely trusted and both are widely misused.

**Mothballs** are a fumigant. They work only in a sealed container where the vapor
can build to an effective concentration — and at that concentration the container
is not somewhere you want to put your face. Scattered in an open closet they
produce a smell and very little else. They are also worth keeping well away from
children and pets, and the residual odor in fabric is difficult to remove.

**Cedar** has genuine repellent properties when the oils are fresh, and it can
deter egg-laying to a degree. It does not kill larvae already present, the
effectiveness fades within a year or two as the oils dissipate, and a cedar closet
full of soiled wool will still be eaten. Light sanding restores some of the
effect.

Neither is a treatment for an active infestation. Cleaning, freezing and sealed
storage are.

## Pantry moths

**The Indianmeal moth** is effectively the only one that matters here. Distinctive
once you know it: the outer two-thirds of the forewing carries a reddish-brown
coppery luster and the inner third is pale gray, so the wing reads as two colors
meeting along a line. UC IPM and Clemson Extension both describe the split at
those proportions, and it is the single feature that settles the identification —
a moth about half an inch across the wings with a pale base and a coppery outer
portion is this species and not much else. If the whole wing is one dull color,
you are looking at something other than a pantry moth, and the
[Indianmeal moth](/pest-library/indianmeal-moth/) profile has the comparison in
more detail.

**Adults do fly to lights** — the opposite of clothes moths — which is why they are
noticed. They also fly through the house, so people find them in rooms far from the
kitchen and conclude the problem is everywhere. It is not; the source is one
package.

**Larvae live in the food** and produce **silk webbing** that binds the contents
together into a clumped, matted mass. That webbing is the reliable evidence. Grain,
flour or cereal that looks slightly clumped and stringy is infested.

**The ceiling corner clue.** Mature larvae leave the food to pupate, and they crawl
upward and outward — frequently to the junction of wall and ceiling, the top of a
door frame, or a shelf edge, where they spin a small cocoon. Finding cocoons in
ceiling corners well away from the kitchen is completely normal and does not mean
the infestation has spread. It means the larvae walked.

**Where the source hides.** Same list as the
[pantry beetles](/services/beetle-control/): pet food, birdseed, decorative dried
items, and spilled material in a cabinet seam. Plus bulk grains, nuts, dried
fruit, chocolate, and spices.

## Finding a pantry moth source

**Check everything, including sealed-looking packages.** Larvae chew through paper
and thin plastic, and they exploit folded seams without chewing at all.

**Look for webbing** rather than for insects. It is far easier to see, and it is
diagnostic.

**Do the four suspects first** — pet food, birdseed, decorative botanicals,
spilled material under a shelf liner. These account for most of the "we threw
everything out and it came back" cases.

**Discard infested material out of the house**, not into the kitchen bin.

**Vacuum the cabinet structure**, particularly the shelf-support holes drilled
into cabinet sides, which are a standard harborage.

**Re-store in airtight glass, metal or rigid plastic.**

**Freeze bulk purchases** for a few days on arrival.

**Then monitor.** Pheromone traps for Indianmeal moth catch adult males and tell
you whether the population is declining. They are a monitoring tool, not a
control — a trap alone will not clear an infestation, and any product sold as
though it will is overselling.

## Protecting a valuable collection

Where the items at risk are genuinely valuable — heirloom textiles, antique rugs,
a wardrobe of good wool, taxidermy, historic garments — the approach shifts from
treatment to conservation, and the priorities change.

**Environment first.** Stable, moderate humidity and temperature. Clothes moths do
better in warm still conditions, and a cool, ventilated, monitored space is
inhospitable without anything applied to it.

**Sealed storage, correctly done.** Archival containers rather than cardboard,
acid-free tissue rather than plastic film in direct contact with textiles, and
nothing put away that has not been cleaned.

**Freezing as the primary intervention** for items that cannot be washed or dry
cleaned. It kills all life stages without exposing the item to anything chemical.

**Monitoring rather than spraying.** Pheromone traps placed and checked on a
schedule tell you whether anything is active, which on a collection is worth far
more than a treatment applied on principle.

**Inspection and handling.** Items lifted, unfolded and examined periodically —
because the whole problem depends on things sitting undisturbed. Regular handling
is genuinely preventive.

We will not spray a valuable textile, and where the right answer is a textile
conservator rather than a pest control company, we will say so.

## What we will not do

**Spray your clothes or bedding.** Application belongs on structure — closet
framing, carpet edges, baseboard voids — not on things worn against skin.

**Treat a closet and call it finished.** Without cleaning, the larvae and eggs on
the garments are untouched, and the population continues on the fabric.

**Sell mothballs as a solution.** They have a narrow legitimate use in sealed
containers and are misapplied almost universally.

**Fog a pantry.** Food storage does not get treated with product; infested
material gets removed and the structure gets cleaned.

**Promise a single visit.** Both of these have hidden stages and long
development times, and the honest plan involves a follow-up.

## The moths you can ignore

Most moths that appear indoors here are neither of the two problems on this page,
and it is worth being able to dismiss them.

**Anything circling a lamp or at a window at night** is almost certainly an
outdoor species that came in through a door. Clothes moths avoid light; pantry
moths do go to light, but they are small, two-toned and come from the kitchen
rather than from outdoors.

**Large moths** are outdoor species without exception. Clothes moths and Indianmeal
moths are both small — around a quarter to half an inch.

**Moths in a garage, shed or outbuilding** in numbers are generally outdoor
species using shelter, unless there is stored feed, birdseed or grain, in which
case check the storage.

**Moths in spring around new foliage** are outdoor species with a seasonal
emergence, and nothing to do with a building.

The two questions that settle it: does it go toward light, and is there webbing?
Light-seeking with no webbing anywhere is almost never a problem worth treating.

## Around the seasons

**Late winter and early spring.** Stored winter woolens come out and the damage
from the previous storage season is discovered. This is when most clothes moth
calls arrive — the damage happened months ago in a dark closet.

**Spring and early summer.** Adult activity increases with warmth. A good window
for cleaning and re-storing, and the right time to deal with anything found in
March.

**Summer.** Peak pantry moth activity, because warmth speeds development in a
kitchen cupboard. Also the season when bulk buying and outdoor birdseed storage
generate new introductions.

**Autumn.** Storage season. Everything put away now decides what comes out in
spring, which makes this the highest-leverage moment of the year: clean before
storing, use sealed containers, and the following February looks completely
different.

**Winter.** Indoor heating keeps both groups active. Clothes moth larvae feed
quietly through the season in undisturbed storage, which is exactly why the
damage is a surprise.

## What it looks like when it is working

Both problems have long enough cycles that people struggle to tell progress from
luck, so it is worth naming the markers.

**For clothes moths.** No new damage on items that were previously being eaten.
Pheromone monitors catching nothing over consecutive checks rather than one.
Cleaned and sealed storage staying clean when opened a season later. The absence
of adults is the weakest signal of the three, because adults are short-lived and
sporadic anyway.

**For pantry moths.** No webbing in newly opened packages. Monitors quiet over
several weeks. No new cocoons appearing in ceiling corners or on shelf edges —
that last one is a good practical indicator, because it means no larvae are
completing development.

**For both.** The improvement should hold across a season, not a fortnight. A
pantry that is clear in July and infested again in September had a source that
was never found, and that is worth returning for rather than re-treating.

We would rather set that expectation now than have you judge the work on the wrong
timescale.

## Before you call

**Look for webbing** and tell us whether you found any. It is the fastest way to
separate a clothes moth problem from a pantry one, and it changes what we bring.

**Keep a specimen or a photograph**, and note whether the moths were near a light
or came out of a dark closet. Those two facts usually settle the identification
before we arrive.

**Do not clear everything out first.** It is a natural instinct and it removes the
evidence. Where the damage is and what is stored beside it tells us where the
larvae have been feeding, and an emptied closet tells us nothing. Leave it as it
is and we will work through it with you.
