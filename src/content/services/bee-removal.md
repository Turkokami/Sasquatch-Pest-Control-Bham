## Start here: it is probably not bees

Most calls we receive about bees are about yellowjackets.

The confusion is understandable — yellow and black, flying, stinging — but the
distinction decides everything, because we treat yellowjackets and we do not kill
honey bees.

**Honey bees** are golden brown, fuzzy, and moderately sized. They visit flowers.
They have no interest in your food, your drink, your bin or your barbecue. They
sting once and die, so they are reluctant to do it. A honey bee at a picnic is
almost always a yellowjacket.

**Yellowjackets** are smooth, shiny, brightly marked with a narrow waist, and
noticeably faster and more assertive. They are hunting protein early in the season
and sugar late, which is exactly why they show up at your table in September. They
sting repeatedly. See [wasp and hornet control](/services/wasp-control/).

**Bumble bees** are large, round and very hairy, often with bands of yellow, black
and sometimes orange. Slow, loud and famously placid.

**Mason and mining bees** are small, solitary, often metallic blue-green or dark,
and active in spring. No colony, no defense, effectively no sting risk.

If you can photograph it, send it. Identification is free and it frequently ends
the conversation with "you do not need us."

## Why we will not kill honey bees

This is a firm position and worth explaining rather than just asserting.

**Practically, it makes the problem worse.** A colony inside a wall represents
pounds of wax comb, brood and honey. Kill the bees and all of that stays in the
cavity. Without the colony maintaining it, the comb warms, the honey ferments and
runs, and it soaks into the drywall and framing — producing staining, odor, and a
long-term food source that attracts ants, wax moths, carpet beetles and rodents.
Households that pay to have a wall colony poisoned frequently pay again a year
later for a considerably messier job.

**Ecologically, it is a bad trade.** Pollinator pressure is real, and destroying a
functioning colony that a beekeeper would happily collect is difficult to justify
when a better outcome exists.

**And there is usually a better option.** Swarms get collected. Established
colonies get removed properly. Neither requires killing anything.

## Swarms

A swarm is a colony in transit — a queen and a large cluster of workers that have
left a hive to establish a new one. They gather on a branch, fence post, wall or
vehicle in a dense mass, sometimes thousands of bees, while scouts look for a
permanent cavity.

Two things worth knowing.

**They are usually docile.** Swarming bees have no nest, no brood and no stores to
defend, and they are full of honey for the journey. They are not looking for
trouble, though they should not be disturbed.

**They usually leave on their own** within a day or two.

The right response is generally to leave it alone and, if it is somewhere
inconvenient, contact a local beekeeper. Beekeepers frequently collect swarms at
no charge because a free colony is genuinely valuable to them. We will point you
in that direction rather than selling you a service.

What we would ask you not to do is spray it. A swarm is the easiest possible
outcome — a colony that has not yet moved into your wall — and killing it wastes
that.

## Established colonies in a structure

A different proposition entirely, and the reason this page exists.

If scouts chose your wall cavity, soffit, chimney or floor void, the colony builds
comb and becomes permanent. Signs are steady directional traffic at one point on
the building over weeks rather than days, a low continuous hum audible from
inside, warmth in a wall area, and in an established colony, honey or staining
appearing through drywall or siding.

**Resolving it properly is a cut-out.** The cavity is opened, the colony and all
of the comb are removed, the cavity is cleaned to remove residue and pheromone,
and the structure is repaired and sealed so the site cannot be reoccupied — which
it will be, because a cavity that has held bees smells like home to the next
scouts.

That is carpentry as much as pest work, and it is frequently done by a beekeeper
who specializes in removals, sometimes working alongside a contractor. We will
tell you honestly what the job involves and who is best placed to do which part,
rather than offering a spray as a shortcut.

**What we will not do** is treat it and leave the comb in the wall. That is the
thing that produces the year-later phone call.

## Bumble bees

Our default recommendation is to leave them, and here is why that is not laziness.

Bumble bee colonies are small — dozens to a few hundred individuals rather than
tens of thousands. They are annual: the colony dies out in autumn and only new
queens overwinter, elsewhere. **The nest is never reused.** They are docile unless
the nest is directly disturbed, and they are among the most effective pollinators
in this region for exactly the crops the Whatcom County agricultural corridor
depends on.

So a bumble bee nest under a shed, in a compost heap, in an old rodent burrow or
in wall insulation is a temporary situation that resolves itself by autumn, and
the sensible response is usually to keep away from it and wait.

Where it genuinely sits in a bad place — immediately beside a doorway, in a
child's play area, or where someone in the household has a sting allergy — we will
discuss options. Even then, the first option considered is whether the area can be
worked around for the remainder of the season.

## Mason bees, mining bees and the harmless ones

Every spring we get calls about these, and they need no service at all.

**Mason bees** are small, dark or metallic, and solitary — every female is her own
queen with her own nest, and they have no colony to defend. They nest in existing
holes: hollow stems, beetle exit holes, and the tubes people put up specifically
to attract them. They are early-season pollinators of real value in orchards and
gardens, they do not damage wood, and they are close to incapable of a meaningful
sting.

**Mining bees** nest in small individual burrows in bare soil or lawn, often in
loose aggregations that look alarming and are not a colony. Active for a few weeks
in spring, then gone. They do not damage lawns.

If either turns up on your property, the honest advice is to enjoy them. We are
not going to sell you a treatment for a solitary pollinator, and if someone else
has offered to, that is worth knowing about them.

## Wood-boring bees

The other genuine bee-related structural issue, and it belongs on a different
page.

If you are seeing round, clean holes roughly the diameter of a pencil in fascia,
trim, deck rails or siding — often with coarse sawdust below and a large bee
hovering nearby — that is a wood-boring bee. It is a structural question rather
than a pollinator one, though the damage accrues slowly and is frequently
overstated.

See [wood-boring bee and beetle control](/services/carpenter-bee-control/).

## What we actually do on a bee call

**Identify, free of charge.** Species and situation. A large share of these calls
end here with advice.

**Point you to a beekeeper** for a swarm or an established honey bee colony,
because that is the right outcome and we would rather give it to you than sell
around it.

**Handle it as wasps** when it turns out to be yellowjackets, hornets or paper
wasps, which is most of the time. See
[wasp and hornet control](/services/wasp-control/).

**Deal with the structure** where a colony has been removed — sealing the cavity,
screening the entry, and closing the other openings on the building so it is not
reoccupied. That is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work and it is the part
most often skipped, which is why some buildings get colonies repeatedly.

**Advise on prevention.** Capping chimneys, screening vents and soffits, and
closing the cavity openings that scouts find attractive.

## Preventing a colony from moving in

Scouts look for a dry, enclosed cavity of the right volume with a small defensible
entrance. Buildings offer plenty, and the ones that get colonies tend to get them
repeatedly.

**Cap the chimney.** An uncapped flue is one of the most reliable colony sites on
a house, and it is the hardest to resolve afterwards because access is poor.

**Screen the vents.** Gable, soffit, ridge and dryer vents with intact metal
screen rather than broken plastic louvers.

**Close the gaps.** Where siding meets trim, around window and door framing, at
soffit returns, and any hole a previous colony used. That last one matters most —
a cavity that has held bees retains pheromone that actively attracts new scouts,
so a former colony site should be cleaned and sealed rather than just emptied.

**Check after re-roofing or siding work.** New gaps get created by trades, and
spring scouts find them quickly.

That work is [exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/), and it
closes the same openings that let in
[wasps](/services/wasp-control/), [rodents](/services/rodent-control/) and cluster
flies. One job, several problems.

## If someone in the household is allergic

This changes the calculus and it is worth saying separately.

A known sting allergy makes proximity to any colony — bee or wasp — a genuine
medical consideration rather than a preference. In that situation we do not tell
people to tolerate a bumble bee nest beside a doorway, and the default "leave them
alone" advice on this page does not apply.

What we will still do is find the least destructive answer that resolves the risk.
For a swarm that means a beekeeper, quickly. For an established honey bee colony
it means a proper removal rather than poisoning. For a bumble bee nest in a bad
location it means discussing options rather than assuming the worst one.

Tell us when you call. It changes the urgency and it changes what we recommend.

## Bees on the ground in autumn

A common call that needs no service.

Late in the season, bumble bee and honey bee workers reaching the end of their
lives are frequently found on the ground, moving slowly or not at all. Nests are
also breaking up in autumn, which puts more individuals in the open.

This is normal seasonal mortality, not a sign of a nest nearby, poisoning or
anything wrong with the property. A single bee moving slowly on a path in
September is a bee at the end of its life.

If you want to help one, a drop of sugar water on a spoon occasionally revives a
bee that is simply out of fuel. That is the entire intervention, and it is not
something we charge for.

## Why we take this position at all

It would be easier commercially to treat every stinging-insect call the same way.
A wall colony sprayed is a quick job at a good margin, and most customers would
never know the difference until the honey came through the ceiling a year later.

We do not do it for three reasons, and they are worth putting in writing.

**It is not the right work.** A treatment that leaves pounds of comb and honey in
a cavity has not solved anything — it has converted a bee problem into a moisture,
staining and secondary-pest problem, and handed the customer a larger bill later.

**The alternative genuinely exists.** Beekeepers want swarms. Removal specialists
do cut-outs. Referring someone to a better outcome costs us a job and buys
something worth more than the job.

**And pollinators are not an abstraction here.** Whatcom County's berry and tree
fruit production depends on managed and wild pollinators, and the agricultural
corridor a few miles north of our office is where that dependence is most direct.
Destroying functioning colonies in that context is difficult to defend.

If another company offers to exterminate honey bees in your wall, the question
worth asking is what happens to the comb.

## What we need to know when you call

**A photograph if at all possible.** It resolves the identification faster than
any description, and identification decides everything else.

**Where they are going in.** One consistent point on the building, several points,
or nowhere in particular. Steady directional traffic at a single spot over weeks
means an established colony; bees moving around a garden means foragers.

**How long it has been happening.** Days suggests a swarm. Weeks or a season
suggests a colony with comb.

**Whether you can hear anything.** A continuous hum audible from inside a wall,
particularly when the room is quiet at night, is a strong indicator of an
established colony.

**Whether anyone is allergic.** It changes the urgency and the recommendation.

**And whether anything has been sprayed already.** It affects what a beekeeper is
willing to collect, because a treated colony is generally not salvageable — which
is another reason not to spray first and call afterward.

## Bees and pesticide use generally

One more thing worth stating, because it applies to work well beyond this page.

Our routine treatments are targeted rather than broadcast — perimeter, entry
points, harborage and structure rather than blanket application across vegetation
and flowering plants. That is partly because it works better, and partly because
broad application over anything in bloom is how general pest treatment harms
pollinators.

In practice that means we avoid treating flowering plants, we work around
blooming periods where a treatment is near them, and where an application has to
happen close to forage we favor timing when bees are not active. On agricultural
accounts near managed hives that coordination matters more, and it is worth
telling us if there are hives adjacent to a property we are working.

None of that is a certification or a slogan. It is a set of ordinary practices
that a company can follow or ignore, and we would rather tell you which we do.
