## The single most common way in

If we had to pick one defect that accounts for more rodent entries into
Whatcom County homes than any other, it would be a failed crawlspace vent
screen.

The reasoning is simple. Older housing here almost universally has a vented
crawlspace, which means a row of openings deliberately cut through the
foundation at ground level. Those openings were screened when the house was
built. The screening was often a light metal mesh, sometimes a plastic or
fiberglass insect screen, occasionally nothing at all beyond a decorative
louver.

Then thirty, fifty or eighty years happen. Metal corrodes in a wet climate.
Plastic goes brittle in sunlight and cracks. A string trimmer catches an edge. A
pressure washer punches a hole. A contractor removes one to run a pipe and never
replaces it properly. Somebody's dog digs at one.

And a failed vent screen is not a small defect. The opening behind it is sized
for airflow, which means it is very generously sized for an animal. Extension
guidance gives a rat slightly more than half an inch to pass through and a mouse
slightly more than a quarter, and a torn vent screen leaves far more room than
either. A crawl vent with a hole in it is, functionally, a door left open.

The mesh spec follows from a second figure rather than from those two.
Washington's Department of Fish and Wildlife notes that a rat needs only a
quarter-inch hole to chew larger and squeeze through, so half-inch cloth over a
vent gives a rat an edge to work on as well as letting every mouse straight
past. Quarter-inch cloth denies both, which is why it is the specification on
this page and not a cautious over-build.

The good news is that this is one of the cheapest and most permanent fixes
available on a building.

## Why not just close them

The intuitive response is to block the vents entirely, and it is a genuinely bad
idea in this climate.

A vented crawlspace works by exchanging air with the outside, which carries away
the moisture that comes up out of the ground and in through the foundation.
Close the vents on a traditional crawlspace and that moisture has nowhere to go.

What follows is predictable and expensive: humidity rises, framing takes on
moisture, decay establishes in the sill plate, rim joist and joists, insulation
loses its function, and the whole community of moisture-driven insects moves in —
moisture ants, springtails, sowbugs, millipedes, silverfish, and in the worst
cases dampwood termites. Structural decay in this climate takes capacity out of
a building faster than rodents do.

So the requirement is specific: exclude animals while preserving airflow. That
rules out boards, foam, plastic sheet, and anything else that closes the opening.
It leaves mesh.

There is a legitimate alternative approach — a fully sealed and conditioned
crawlspace with the vents closed, the ground covered and the space managed as
part of the building envelope. That is a substantial building project with its
own requirements, done properly by somebody who understands it. It is not the
same as putting a board over a vent, and we will say so if anybody has suggested
otherwise.

## What actually works as a material

The specification matters more than the labor here, and most of the failures we
find are material choices rather than workmanship.

**Galvanized or stainless hardware cloth, quarter-inch mesh.** This is the
answer for the great majority of situations. Quarter-inch because a house mouse
gets through anything larger. Metal because rodents chew, and galvanized or
stainless because this climate destroys unprotected steel.

**Nineteen gauge or heavier.** Very fine hardware cloth exists and it is not
worth using here; it corrodes faster and it can be worked apart at the edges.

**Fastened, not leaned.** The mesh has to be secured around its full perimeter
into sound material, with fasteners appropriate to the substrate — screws and
washers, masonry fasteners into concrete or block, or a purpose-made frame.
Mesh held in place by friction or by a couple of nails at the corners gets pushed
in.

**Fitted behind or over a sound louver**, depending on the vent design, so that
the mesh is protected from mechanical damage and the appearance is acceptable.

**Where the vent frame itself has failed** — rusted through, rotted, or loose in
the opening — a complete new vent assembly is usually better value than trying
to screen a frame that is disintegrating.

## What does not work

We spend a fair amount of time removing previous attempts, so here is the list.

**Fiberglass and plastic insect screening.** Stops flies. Does not stop anything
with teeth. This is the most common material we find failed.

**Standard aluminum window screen.** Marginally better and still chewed through
or pushed aside without much effort.

**Expanding foam.** Rodents go through it quickly, it blocks ventilation where
used in quantity, and it degrades in sunlight. It has a legitimate use as a
filler behind a metal barrier and none as a barrier.

**Steel wool alone.** Better than foam, and it compresses, rusts and works loose
over time. Reasonable as a temporary measure or as packing behind mesh; not a
repair.

**Plywood or sheet material over the opening.** Excludes animals and creates a
moisture problem.

**Chicken wire.** The mesh is far too large and the wire too light. It stops
nothing you care about.

**Half-inch hardware cloth.** Stops rats, admits mice. On a property with mice —
which is most properties — the job is half done and it will not be obvious why.

## The rest of the ground-level picture

Vents are the headline and they are rarely the only opening, so a proper job
looks at the whole foundation line.

**The crawl access hatch.** Second on the list and often worse than the vents. A
great many are a sheet of plywood resting in a rebate, warped, or missing
altogether. Utility work has usually had it off and back on several times. A
properly fitted, latching, animal-resistant access cover is a genuinely
worthwhile item.

**Utility penetrations.** Water supply, waste, gas, electrical, cable, heat pump
line sets — every one of them passes through the foundation somewhere and most
were sealed to keep weather out rather than animals. These need closing with
metal and an appropriate sealant, not foam alone.

**The gap under a garage door**, particularly at the corners where the seal has
hardened, and the connection between an attached garage and the rest of the
house.

**Where grade has risen against the building**, burying vents or bringing soil
into contact with siding, which both blocks ventilation and offers a route.

**Deck and porch skirting**, which is usually decorative rather than sealed and
which creates a protected void connected to the crawl area.

**Where an addition meets the original structure**, a junction built at a
different time to a different standard, and one of the most reliable places to
find an opening nobody has ever seen.

**Foundation cracks and gaps at the sill**, where a rat can enlarge an existing
defect.

## Sequence matters

There is one rule that governs the order of this work and it is not negotiable.

**Do not seal a crawlspace with an active population inside it.**

Close the openings on a building that has rodents under the floor and you have
not excluded them; you have enclosed them, with your insulation, your wiring and
your ducting, and no way out. What follows is worse damage, animals dying in
inaccessible places, and eventually a hole chewed somewhere new — sometimes
upward into the building rather than back outside.

So the order is: establish whether anything is living down there, trap and remove
it, confirm activity has stopped, and then seal. Where the crawlspace is clear,
the sealing can go ahead immediately.

We check before we close anything, every time, which occasionally means telling
somebody who wanted vents rescreened this week that there is a fortnight of
trapping first.

## What else the vents affect

Rescreening does more than keep rodents out, which is worth knowing when
weighing the cost.

It excludes the other things that use those openings. Cats, which get under
houses and cannot always get out. Snakes and amphibians. Wasps and yellowjackets
looking for a nesting cavity. And, in the buildings where it happens, larger
wildlife, which is outside what we handle but which arrives the same way.

It reduces windblown debris. Leaf litter accumulating in a crawl area holds
moisture and supports insects.

It keeps the ventilation working as designed, because a vent full of leaves or
buried by landscaping is not ventilating anything. Part of doing this properly is
clearing what has accumulated and making sure the openings are actually open.

And it is one of the few pieces of pest work that is genuinely permanent.
Correctly specified metal mesh, correctly fastened, lasts decades. Compare that
with a recurring treatment plan and the arithmetic is not close.

## Automatic and foundation vents

A note on the vents themselves, since people ask.

Some buildings have automatic vents, designed to open and close with temperature.
They are a reasonable idea and they have a failure mode: the mechanism seizes,
usually in the closed position, and the crawlspace loses its ventilation without
anybody noticing. Where we find them we check whether they still operate.

Some have manually operated louvers that somebody closed one winter and never
reopened. This is more common than it should be and it produces exactly the
moisture problem described above.

And some vents have been fitted with covers — foam blocks, magnetic covers,
purpose-made plugs — as a heat-saving measure. In a traditional vented crawlspace
in this climate, we would advise against them, because what you save on heating
you pay for in decay.

If you want a crawlspace that is sealed rather than vented, that is a legitimate
and different project, and it involves ground cover, air sealing, insulation at
the walls rather than the floor, and usually mechanical drying. Doing half of it
is worse than doing none of it.

## Doing it yourself

This is well within reach of a competent homeowner and we will not pretend
otherwise.

Buy quarter-inch galvanized hardware cloth. Cut pieces with a two-inch overlap
all round. Fasten with screws and fender washers into the framing or with masonry
fasteners into concrete, at intervals close enough that the mesh cannot be pushed
in — every three or four inches around the perimeter. Trim the edges cleanly,
because cut hardware cloth is genuinely sharp. Wear gloves.

The parts most people get wrong: fastening only at the corners, using a mesh size
that admits mice, and doing it before checking whether anything is already under
the house.

If you are doing it yourself, at least look in the crawlspace first with a good
light, and if you find droppings, runs or nesting material, deal with that before
you close anything.

## How to tell whether yours have failed

You can assess this yourself in about fifteen minutes with a flashlight and it is
worth doing before anybody quotes anything.

Walk the full perimeter of the building and find every vent. Most houses here
have between four and a dozen, and there is nearly always one somewhere awkward —
behind a shrub, under a deck, tight against a fence, or buried by a raised bed
— that nobody has looked at in years. That is usually the one that has failed.

At each vent, look for a hole, a torn corner, a section of mesh pushed inward, or
screening that is missing entirely. Push gently on the mesh with a finger.
Corroded metal breaks under light pressure and brittle plastic cracks.

Look at the bottom edge in particular. Water runs down a wall and collects there,
so that is where metal rusts through first and where debris packs in.

Check what the vent is made of. If you can see woven plastic or fiberglass, that
is not excluding a rodent regardless of its condition.

Look for evidence around the opening: greasy smear marks on the concrete, small
droppings, a worn track in the soil leading to it, gnawed edges, or disturbed
material immediately outside.

Then shine a light through into the crawl area. If you can see clearly through a
vent with no mesh in the way at all, you have found your answer.

Photograph anything you are unsure about. It saves a conversation.

## What it looks like when it has been used

Once you know what to look for, an active entry point is fairly obvious, and
knowing helps you judge urgency.

**Smear marks.** Rodents travel repeatedly along the same routes and their fur
leaves a greasy dark deposit on concrete, block and timber at the point of
passage. A dark polished patch at the edge of a vent opening means regular
traffic.

**A worn path.** On soil or bark mulch, a route used nightly for weeks shows as a
flattened track leading to the opening.

**Droppings** immediately outside or just inside the vent.

**Gnawing.** Where an animal has enlarged an opening rather than found one, the
edges of timber or plastic show tooth marks.

**Nesting material** caught at the edges — insulation fibers, shredded paper,
dry grass — pulled through and dropped.

**Insects.** Flies at a vent in numbers can indicate something dead inside, which
changes the job.

None of this means panic. It does mean the vent is currently in use rather than
merely defective, and it moves the trapping conversation ahead of the sealing
one.

## Landscaping is half the problem

A surprising share of failed and non-functioning vents are not failed at all.
They are buried.

Grade rises over time. Beds get topped up, mulch gets added each spring, gravel
gets spread, a lawn is built up, and after twenty years the soil level against a
foundation is several inches higher than it was. Vents that were a foot above
grade end up at grade or below it.

The consequences stack. A buried vent does not ventilate, so the crawlspace gets
damper. Soil against the foundation holds water where it does the most harm.
Bark mulch packed against an opening is both a moisture source and excellent
rodent harborage sitting directly at an entry point. And soil bridging up to
siding gives insects a route past the foundation entirely.

The remedy is unglamorous: cut the grade back to expose the vents properly, keep
a clear band of ground against the building, pull mulch and bedding away from the
wall, and cut back shrubs so the foundation line can be seen and can dry.

We raise this on nearly every visit because it costs nothing but an afternoon and
it improves the crawlspace, the rodent situation and the decay risk at the same
time. It is also the thing most likely to be undone next spring when somebody
tops the beds up again.

## What it costs and what it saves

We will not put a figure on a page, because vent counts, access and condition
vary too much to be honest about it. What we can set out is the shape of the
decision.

Rescreening a set of crawl vents in metal is a one-off cost in the low hundreds
for a typical house, sometimes less. Replacing failed vent assemblies adds
modestly to that. A properly fitted crawl access cover is an inexpensive item.

Against that, consider what an unaddressed entry produces over a few years:
repeated trapping visits, contaminated insulation, gnawed wiring, damaged ducting
and, in the worst cases, a full crawlspace cleanout and reinsulation running into
several thousand.

And unlike a treatment plan, this does not recur. Correctly specified mesh,
correctly fastened, is still doing its job in twenty years.

That arithmetic is why we lead with exclusion rather than with recurring service.
It is a smaller invoice today and it is the recommendation that keeps people from
needing us.
