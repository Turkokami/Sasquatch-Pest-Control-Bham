## Why the insulation is the target

An attic is not inherently attractive to a rodent. It is hot in summer, cold in
winter, exposed, and a long way from food.

What makes it worth the climb is the insulation. Fiberglass batts and blown-in
material are, from an animal's point of view, an unusually good building
resource: soft, dry, endlessly available, easy to shape, and warm once it is
packed around a nest. A roof space with a foot of loose-fill in it is a
warehouse full of bedding.

So attic rodent activity is nearly always insulation activity. The animals are
not simply passing over the material — they are living in it, tunnelling
through it, dragging it into heaps and compressing it flat along the routes they
use most.

That is why an attic job is different from a crawlspace job or a kitchen job.
The question is not only how to remove the animals and close the building. It is
what condition the insulation is in afterwards, and whether it can stay.

## What it costs you in heat

This is the part that rarely gets explained properly, and it is usually the
larger number.

Insulation does not work because of what it is made of. It works because it
traps still air in a large volume of low-density material. Anything that reduces
that volume reduces the performance almost proportionally.

Rodents reduce it three ways. They compress it, flattening runs through
loose-fill and matting batts down along the routes they travel. They remove it,
pulling material out of place to build nests, which leaves bays partly or
entirely bare. And they wet it, because urine saturates and the material then
settles and stays settled.

The visual from a hatch is misleading here. An attic can look more or less
insulated while a substantial share of its effective value has gone, because
compression is not obvious from a distance and bare joist bays are hidden behind
the ones nearer the opening.

For a house in this climate, where the heating season is long and mild rather
than short and severe, degraded attic insulation is a steady cost that runs for
as long as it goes unaddressed. That is worth weighing against the price of
putting it right, and it frequently changes the answer.

## What is up there

The realistic list for a Whatcom County attic is shorter than people fear, and
it includes one thing we do not handle.

**Roof rats.** Confident climbers and the species most associated with upper
structures. They will use branches, downspouts, cable runs, rough siding and
trellis to reach a roof, and they enter at the line where the roof meets the
wall. In an attic they are active mainly after dark.

**Norway rats.** Less common overhead but entirely capable of it, particularly
in buildings where an interior wall cavity gives them a route up from the
crawlspace without ever going outside.

**House mice.** Common, and easily overlooked because they are quiet. Mouse
activity in an attic often shows as scattered droppings and fist-sized nests
tucked into corners and along the top plates rather than as obvious damage.

**Deer mice.** More likely where a property adjoins woodland or open ground, and
more seasonal. They are the species that makes careful handling of dry nesting
material worthwhile, and we treat all attic material to that standard rather
than trying to tell them apart by eye.

**Something else entirely.** Attics are also where squirrels, bats and birds end
up, and all three are outside what we do. If that is what you have, we will say
so, at no charge, and point you toward somebody properly equipped rather than
taking a job we should not take. Daytime activity overhead is the usual tell,
and it is worth mentioning on the phone before anybody drives out.

## How they get to the roof

Attic entry is a roofline problem, and it comes down to a familiar set of
defects.

**Soffit and fascia junctions.** The single most common. Where the soffit meets
the fascia board, or where it returns at a corner, there is very often a gap
that has been there since construction and that nobody has ever been in a
position to see.

**Open rafter tails.** On older construction the space between rafters at the
wall top is sometimes simply open, or blocked with material that has since
failed.

**Gable and roof vents.** Louvred gable vents with damaged or missing screens
are effectively doors. So are turbine and box vents where the flashing has
lifted.

**Roof-to-wall junctions.** Where a lower roof — a porch, a garage, an addition
— runs into an upper wall, the detail is complicated and frequently imperfect.
An animal on the lower roof is already halfway there.

**Vegetation.** Branches touching or overhanging a roof are a bridge, and so is
ivy or dense climbing growth on a wall. Cutting back to a clear gap is often the
cheapest single measure available.

**Utility penetrations at height.** Service entries, satellite and cable
brackets, plumbing vents and heat pump line sets all pass through the envelope
somewhere, and sealing at height is done to a poorer standard than sealing at
ground level in most buildings.

## When the insulation has to come out

We want to be plain about this, because attic insulation replacement is one of
the easiest things in this trade to oversell.

**It should come out when** the material is matted or compressed across a
meaningful area rather than in one spot; when nesting material and waste are
distributed through it rather than confined; when there is urine saturation,
which you can generally smell before you can see it; when the material has been
extensively tunnelled so its structure is gone; or when odor persists in the
living space below and the attic is the source.

**It generally does not need to come out when** the activity was brief and
recent; when droppings are scattered on material that is otherwise intact,
lofted and dry; when the affected area is small enough to deal with in isolation;
or when what you have is old, historic sign from a problem that has already
ended.

**The in-between case is real and common.** Frequently the right answer is to
remove and replace the worst areas, clean and treat the remainder, close the
building properly, and look again in a year. That is a smaller job than a full
cleanout and in a lot of houses it is the honest recommendation.

Where removal is warranted, it is worth doing properly: material out, waste and
nesting removed, surfaces treated, the space allowed to dry, and only then
reinsulated to a specified depth. Blowing new material on top of contaminated
insulation is a shortcut that buys you the same job again later with the odor
still in it, and we will not do it that way.

## The order the work goes in

**Animals out first.** Trapping in the attic and in the connected voids, on the
runs and at the entry points, in numbers, checked and adjusted. We do not use
bait inside the envelope: an animal that dies in a soffit or a wall top is a
second problem, and one that is genuinely difficult to reach in a roof.

**Building closed second.** Roofline sealing in materials that hold — metal mesh
behind vents, sheet metal at soffit and fascia gaps, proper vent covers,
blocking at rafter tails — plus the vegetation cut back. Working at height is
most of what makes attic exclusion a different job from crawlspace exclusion, and
it is not a place for expanding foam.

**Insulation last.** Removal, cleaning and replacement once nothing is living up
there and nothing can get back in. Doing it in any other order means doing it
twice.

## The ceiling plane, which almost nobody seals

There is a second boundary in this job that gets far less attention than the
roofline, and in an occupied house it matters just as much.

The ceiling below an attic is full of holes. Recessed light housings. Bathroom
and kitchen extract fans. The attic hatch itself, which in most houses is a
sheet of board resting in a rebate. Plumbing vent stacks passing through.
Electrical boxes. The gap at the top of an interior wall where the drywall stops
short of the top plate. Chimney and flue chases, which are frequently open the
full height of the building.

Individually these are small. Collectively they are why an attic problem becomes
a living-space problem: odor comes down through them, insects that have been
breeding in nesting material come down through them, and in some houses the
animals themselves come down through them into a wall or a ceiling void over a
bedroom.

Sealing the ceiling plane is not glamorous and it is not usually what somebody
calls about, but it is worth doing at the same time as the insulation work
because the attic is already open and the material is already out. It also pays
for itself independently of the pest question — air leakage through those same
penetrations is one of the larger heat losses in a typical house here, and warm
moist air pushed into a cold roof space is where a good deal of attic
condensation comes from in the first place.

## Damp attics make everything worse

A wet roof space is a worse pest problem than a dry one, and in this climate wet
roof spaces are not rare.

Insulation that has taken on moisture settles, loses loft and stops performing.
It also becomes more attractive as nesting material rather than less, because
damp fiberglass packs and holds shape. Timber that stays damp supports mold and
eventually decay, which in a roof structure is a considerably more expensive
problem than any animal.

The usual causes are mundane. Bathroom and kitchen fans that discharge into the
attic rather than through the roof or the wall — genuinely common, and something
we find on a meaningful share of the buildings we look at. Blocked or
insufficient soffit and ridge ventilation, sometimes blocked by insulation
installed carelessly against the eaves. Air leakage from below, as above. A
minor roof or flashing defect that has never been traced.

So part of an honest attic assessment is not about pests at all. If your fan
discharges into the roof space we will tell you, because putting new insulation
into a wet attic is money spent on something that will fail again.

## What we will not sell you

**A full insulation replacement for scattered droppings.** If the material is
dry, lofted and structurally intact, cleaning the affected area is the
proportionate response. We would rather do the smaller job and keep the
relationship.

**New insulation blown over contaminated material.** It hides the problem, keeps
the odor and guarantees a bigger job later.

**A wildlife job.** If what is in your roof has fur beyond a rodent's, or wings,
we are not the right company. That answer costs you nothing and it is the one
you will get.

**A quarterly plan instead of closing the roofline.** Recurring service does not
address an open soffit. Anybody proposing it as though it did is selling
subscription rather than resolution.

**Insulation work before the building is closed.** New material installed while
the entry is still open is a donation to next winter's population.

## Timing, and the case for doing it in summer

Attic rodent activity in this county follows the same seasonal curve as
everything else — pressure rises from late September, peaks through the autumn
and stays elevated into the new year. Most calls come when the noise starts,
which is to say once the animals are already in.

There is a reasonable argument for doing the roofline work in July or August
instead. The weather is workable, the attic is accessible, the population is not
yet pushing in, and the sealing is being done to an empty space rather than
around a live problem. Insulation work in particular is far easier in a dry
season.

Almost nobody does this, because in August nothing is wrong. It is still the
cheaper version of the same project, and if you have had an attic problem once,
the summer after is the time to make sure it does not happen twice.

There is also a practical scheduling point. Attic and roofline work is
weather-dependent in a way that crawlspace work is not, and in a Whatcom County
November the number of days suitable for working at height is limited. A job
booked in the autumn frequently waits on the weather.

## Signs from below

Most people never go into their attic, so the evidence usually arrives
indirectly.

Noise overhead in the evening and before dawn, particularly directly above a
bedroom ceiling. Sound that moves along the ceiling line is an animal on a run.

A stain appearing on a ceiling with no plumbing above it and no roof leak,
which is occasionally urine saturation working through.

An odor that develops slowly in an upstairs room, strongest near a ceiling
light, an attic hatch or a bathroom fan — all of which are unsealed penetrations
into the roof space.

Droppings appearing on top of stored boxes in a loft, or around the hatch when
it is opened.

Heating bills that have drifted upward without an obvious explanation, in a
house where nothing else changed.

And insects: an established rodent presence supports flies and beetles that feed
on waste and nesting material, and a sudden appearance of either in an upstairs
room sometimes points upward.

## What stored belongings tell you

Most attics double as storage, and the boxes are often better evidence than the
insulation.

Cardboard is nesting material as much as insulation is, and a rodent will work
into a box far enough to shred the contents without doing much to the outside.
Clothing, linen, camping gear, upholstered items and anything with padding are
all targets. Paper records, photographs and books get chewed for material rather
than eaten.

Look for shredded edges, small heaps of fibrous debris beside a box, gnawed
corners at the base where a box meets the deck, and droppings sitting on the
tops of boxes along a route. Plastic tote lids get gnawed at the rim, which is a
useful sign because it tells you the animal is a rat rather than a mouse.

The lesson people usually take from this is worth stating: after an attic
problem is resolved, move anything you care about out of cardboard and into
rigid sealed containers. It removes a resource, it makes future evidence easier
to read, and it saves the contents.

## Safety, briefly

If you are going up to look, the standard precautions matter more in an attic
than almost anywhere else, because the material is dry and the space is
unventilated.

Ventilate before you go in. Do not sweep, brush or dry-vacuum nesting material
or droppings. Wet material down with a disinfectant solution and let it sit
before disturbing it. Use a fitted respirator rather than a dust mask. Wear
gloves, bag material rather than piling it, and wash afterwards.

Beyond the animals, attics have their own hazards — walking surfaces that are
not walking surfaces, exposed wiring, low clearances, and nails through the roof
deck at head height. Plenty of people are entirely capable of doing this
themselves. Plenty of others should not.
