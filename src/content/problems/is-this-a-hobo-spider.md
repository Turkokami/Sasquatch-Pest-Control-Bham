## The question behind the question

Nobody really wants to know the species. What they want to know is whether the
large fast brown spider in the bathtub can hurt them, and whether they should be
worried about their children or their dog.

So we will answer that first, plainly: no spider that commonly lives in Whatcom
County homes presents a meaningful danger to a healthy person. The species most
often called a hobo spider is usually not one, and the hobo spider itself is no
longer regarded by the people who study this as medically significant.

The longer version is worth reading, because the reputation attached to this
particular spider has done a great deal of unnecessary damage, and the way it
came apart is instructive.

## How the reputation was built and taken apart

The hobo spider arrived in the Pacific Northwest from Europe some decades ago and
spread through the region. In the late 1980s a small body of work proposed that
its bite caused necrotic skin lesions — tissue death — similar to those
attributed to the brown recluse.

That claim was picked up widely. It reached public health guidance, it reached
newspapers, and it reached the general awareness of anybody living in this part
of the country. For years it was simply what everyone knew.

It has not held up. Subsequent researchers were unable to reproduce the original
findings. The case reports underpinning the claim were re-examined and found to
be weak, largely because the wounds involved were attributed to a spider that
was rarely, if ever, actually caught in the act. Studies of verified bites — cases
where the spider was captured and identified — did not produce the lesions.

The Centers for Disease Control removed the hobo spider from its list of
venomous spiders, which is about as clear a public retraction as that kind of
guidance produces. We are deliberately not attaching a year to that. The date
usually quoted for the removal cannot be traced back to any agency page, and a
correction that leans on an unverifiable detail hands the argument straight back
to whoever wants the old story kept. What can be checked is the removal itself,
which the regional insect management handbook published jointly by Washington
State University, Oregon State and the University of Idaho records, and the
current federal guidance on venomous spiders for outdoor workers, which names
the black widow and the brown recluse and nothing else.

Washington's own health department has moved the same way. The Department of
Health states that hobo spiders are not considered dangerous as previously
thought, describes them as shy and reluctant to bite, and says directly that
there is no evidence hobo spider venom has caused skin necrosis or necrotic
wounds in people. That is the state agency for the state you are standing in,
not a contrarian reading of the literature.

Meanwhile, in Europe, where the same spider has lived alongside people for far
longer, it has never had this reputation at all.

None of that means a hobo spider bite is pleasant, and we are not offering
medical advice. It means the specific fear — that this spider causes rotting
wounds — rests on evidence that did not survive scrutiny.

## Why identification is genuinely hard

We are frequently sent photographs and asked to confirm. We answer honestly,
which usually means saying it cannot be done from the image.

Hobo spiders belong to a group of funnel weavers that look very similar to each
other. Separating them reliably requires magnification and examination of
anatomical details — the reproductive structures, the pattern on the underside of
the abdomen, features of the pedipalps in mature males. These are not things
visible in a phone photograph of a spider on a wall.

The features people commonly rely on do not settle it. The chevron pattern on
the abdomen is the test that circulates most widely, and the thing worth
noticing about it is that neither WSU Extension's identification factsheet nor
UC IPM's pest note on the species uses it at all. Both skip the markings and go
to the reproductive structures. Size overlaps as well, and it overlaps in the
reassuring direction: the giant house spider is generally the larger animal, so
a big frightening spider is an argument against the hobo rather than for it.

Leg banding deserves a paragraph of its own, because a real character has been
buried under a bad folk test. WSU's key uses banding as an exclusion and nothing
else. Visible dark rings on the legs rule the hobo spider out, because its legs
are uniformly colored, and that is a genuine piece of information you can use
from a foot away. What people do with it instead is run it forwards — plain
legs, therefore a hobo — and in that direction it is worthless, because a great
many harmless funnel weavers have unremarkable legs too. Ruling a species out is
not the same as ruling one in, and most of the confident kitchen-table
identifications made in this region have come from reversing that test.

The one field character that does real work needs the spider on its back, which
usually means looking up through the base of a clear container. The sternum is
the plate on the underside between the leg bases. WSU's key gives the hobo
spider a pale stripe running down the middle of it, and gives the giant house
spider and the barn funnel weaver three or four pairs of pale spots along the
sides instead. Spots mean it is not a hobo. On a live animal moving at the speed
these things move, that is harder than it sounds.

Arachnologists themselves generally will not identify these to species without a
specimen and a microscope. So when a pest control company confidently tells you
what you have from across a room, that confidence is not coming from the spider.

## What you probably have

In this region, the large brown spiders found in houses are most often one of
these.

**Giant house spider.** Closely related to the hobo, considerably more common in
homes here, and larger — a leg span that genuinely surprises people. Fast, which
is what makes it alarming. Entirely harmless. This is what the majority of "hobo
spider" sightings turn out to be. UC IPM goes further than mere abundance and
records that in parts of the Pacific Northwest this larger species has
competitively displaced the hobo spider. We say displaced rather than eaten on
purpose: giant house spiders do take other spiders, but the published claim
about the two of them is about which one holds the ground, and we are not going
to dress a competition result up as a hunting story.

**Other funnel weavers.** A group of similar-looking spiders that build the flat
sheet webs with a funnel retreat at one edge, in corners, window wells, wood
piles and vegetation.

**Cellar spiders.** Very long thin legs, small body, in loose tangled webs in
corners and crawlspaces. Harmless, and they eat other spiders.

**Cross orb weavers.** The large garden spiders that build the classic wheel webs
outside in late summer, often across doorways overnight. Striking, completely
harmless, and one of the more useful animals on a property.

**Wolf spiders.** Ground hunters that do not build webs, sometimes large,
sometimes seen carrying an egg sac or young. Harmless.

**Black widow.** The western black widow is a Washington spider, and the state
Department of Health describes it as common to eastern Washington. Its range
follows the drier, warmer country east and south of the Cascades, which is
roughly the opposite of what this county offers it, so on this side of the
mountains it is uncommon rather than routine. Where one does appear here it is
in something dry and undisturbed — the underside of stored material, a woodpile,
a meter box, a crawlspace corner — and not in living space. Identifiable by the
glossy black body, the globular abdomen and the red hourglass on the underside.
It is the one local spider worth treating with respect, and even so, bites are
rare and serious outcomes rarer.

## The brown recluse question

Because it comes up in nearly every conversation: brown recluse spiders do not
live in western Washington.

Their established range is the central and southern United States. We have never
encountered one in Whatcom County. Nationally, the occasional specimen turns up
outside the range having arrived in freight or in somebody's belongings, and
those cases are notable precisely because they are rare.

Meanwhile a very large number of skin wounds get attributed to brown recluse
bites in places where the spider does not exist. Studies looking at this have
found that the great majority of such wounds turn out to be something else
entirely — bacterial infections including staph, other skin conditions, and a
range of causes with nothing arachnid about them.

The practical consequence is that if you have a wound you are worried about, the
right response is medical attention rather than spider identification. A doctor
can treat the wound. Knowing what spider was or was not in the room does not
change the treatment and can delay it.

## Reading the web instead of the spider

If you want a practical way to narrow things down without catching anything, the
web is easier to identify than its occupant and it is right there in the corner.

**A flat horizontal sheet with a funnel or tube at one edge**, usually in a
corner, a window well, against a foundation, or in low vegetation. That is a
funnel weaver, which is the group the hobo and the giant house spider both
belong to. The spider waits in the tube and rushes out across the sheet. These
webs are not sticky; they work by tangling and by speed.

**A classic wheel-shaped web with radiating spokes**, usually rebuilt daily and
often across a doorway or between shrubs. Orb weaver, almost certainly a cross
orb weaver here. Outdoors, seasonal, harmless and worth admiring.

**A loose, messy, three-dimensional tangle** in a corner of a ceiling, a
crawlspace or a garage, with a very long-legged spider hanging upside down in
it. Cellar spider.

**A small, dense, irregular tangle low down in a dark undisturbed place** —
under a shelf, behind stored material, in the corner of a crawlspace — with a
notably strong, almost crackly silk. This is the one worth a second look, because
that description fits a black widow. Look for a papery tan egg sac about the size
of a pea.

**No web at all, spider on the ground or a wall, moving purposefully.** A hunter
— a wolf spider, or a wandering male of a web-building species during the autumn
walk.

That taxonomy of webs will resolve most household questions faster than trying to
photograph a spider that is moving.

## Why so many in autumn

The seasonal wave that drives most spider calls here has a simple explanation.

Male spiders of several species mature in late summer and leave their webs to
find females. They walk, often considerable distances, and they walk into
buildings. That produces a few weeks in late summer and autumn where people
suddenly see spiders every day — in bathtubs, crossing floors, on walls — having
seen almost none all year.

They are not moving in for the winter and it is not an infestation. It is a
mating season, and it ends.

There is a second reading of those same weeks, and it comes from the reference
collection for this region rather than from the pest control trade, so it is
worth carrying. The Burke Museum's arachnology material holds that house spiders
are largely a distinct indoor-adapted lineage rather than garden spiders that
wandered in, and that generally fewer than five percent of the spiders seen
indoors have ever been outdoors at all. Read that way, the male crossing your
hallway did not enter the house in September. He hatched in it, or in the
crawlspace under it, and what changed this month is that he stopped sitting in a
web and started walking.

Both readings are probably true in some proportion, and the proportion is a
question for people with a museum collection rather than a work van. For a
household it does not matter which one dominates, because they recommend the
same things. Under either, the animals were in and around the building long
before anybody noticed them. Under either, the gap at floor level is worth
closing, because it is a route in from the foundation and a route up from the
crawlspace at the same time. Under either, the insect supply is the lever. And
under either, the season stops in November on its own.

The bathtub thing has its own explanation, since everyone asks: a spider that
walks into a smooth-sided tub or sink cannot climb back out. It did not come up
the drain. It fell in and got stuck.

## What actually reduces spiders indoors

Spiders follow their food. A house with a lot of spiders has a lot of insects,
and dealing with the insects does more than dealing with the spiders.

**Exterior lighting.** This is the largest lever and the least used. Bright white
lights near doors and windows draw insects in quantity, and everything that eats
insects follows. Switching to warmer color temperatures, using motion sensors, or
moving a light away from a doorway reduces the whole chain.

**Door sweeps and weatherstripping.** Most spiders indoors walked in at floor
level. Closing that gap is genuinely effective and it helps with several other
problems at once.

**Screens repaired**, and window frames sealed where they have moved.

**Webs knocked down regularly**, inside and out. A web that is removed
consistently is not rebuilt indefinitely, and the effort of doing it weekly for a
month is repaid.

**Vegetation off the walls.** Shrubs and climbers touching a building are both
habitat and bridge.

**Clutter reduced** in garages, crawlspaces and storage areas, which is where the
few spiders worth avoiding actually live.

**Moisture addressed**, because damp ground produces insects in volume and the
whole food chain scales with it.

Treatment has a place — a targeted application at entry points and harborage
before the autumn wave, and specific attention where a black widow is found in a
crawlspace or a woodstore. What it will not do is make a wooded lot in Whatcom
County spider-free, and anybody promising that is selling a subscription.

## Where they come in, specifically

Since sealing is the measure that actually works, it helps to know the openings
that matter for spiders in this county's housing.

The gap under an exterior door, particularly a garage side door or a basement
door where the sweep has hardened or worn at the corners. This is the single
biggest one at ground level.

Sliding door tracks, which are almost never sealed at the ends and which open
directly into living space.

Window frames on older stock where the trim has moved away from the siding, and
window wells that hold leaf litter and become a spider habitat in their own
right.

Crawlspace vents and the crawl access hatch, which connect a damp insect-rich
space to the floor structure above it.

Utility penetrations — the holes cut for water, waste, gas, power, cable and heat
pump lines — which in most buildings were sealed to keep weather out rather than
arthropods.

Unscreened or poorly screened attic and gable vents.

And the route people never think of: dryer and bathroom vent outlets whose flaps
have stopped closing.

Every one of those is also a rodent, moisture or heat-loss issue, which is why
closing them is worth doing regardless of how you feel about spiders.

## The case for leaving most of them alone

We will make this argument even though it costs us work.

Spiders are predators of the insects people actually mind — flies, mosquitoes,
moths, and a good deal else. A cellar spider in a crawlspace corner and an orb
weaver on a porch are doing useful work, for free, continuously.

They are also, with the single local exception of the black widow, harmless.
Spider bites in general are far less common than people believe; most spiders
cannot readily penetrate human skin, and the great majority have no interest in
biting anything that is not prey.

Our practical recommendation for most households is to remove the ones you do
not want to live with — the ones in bedrooms, the ones over a bed, the webs
across a doorway — and leave the rest. That is a completely legitimate approach
and it costs nothing.

## What the internet gets wrong about this

A brief note on sources, because this is a topic where searching makes people
more frightened rather than better informed.

A great deal of what circulates about hobo spiders online predates the
retraction. Articles from the 1990s and 2000s repeating the necrosis claim are
still indexed, still shared, and still read as current. Nothing marks them as
superseded.

Photographs labeled as hobo spiders are frequently something else, because — as
above — most people cannot identify them and the labels get copied along with the
images.

Wound photographs attributed to spider bites are, in the great majority of
documented cases, something with a different cause. That is not a fringe view; it
is the consistent finding when researchers follow up on such cases.

And the general shape of the coverage rewards alarm. An accurate article saying
that the large spider in your basement is harmless does not travel the way a
frightening one does.

If you want to check something, the useful sources are the university extension
services in this region and the arachnologists who publish on it. Washington
State University's extension material on Pacific Northwest spiders is a good
starting point and it reflects the current position rather than the retracted
one.

## Where we do treat without hesitation

**Black widows in a location people reach into.** A woodpile, a crawlspace, a
garage shelf, the underside of outdoor furniture, a shed used regularly. Worth
dealing with and worth being told about.

**Heavy autumn pressure at entry points**, where a targeted application before the
wave takes the edge off a genuinely difficult few weeks.

**Any situation involving someone with a serious anxiety about spiders.** That is
a real quality-of-life issue and we do not think it is silly. The approach is the
same — entries, lighting, webs, insects — but the priority is different.

**Crawlspaces and garages** with heavy webbing and populations, where the
underlying moisture and insect situation usually needs attention as well.

## Catching one safely

If you want a specimen identified, catching it intact is straightforward and does
not require touching anything.

The glass-and-card method works on almost any spider: put a glass or a jar over
it, slide a piece of stiff card underneath, and lift. From there it goes into a
sealed container. If you want it identified rather than released, a small jar of
rubbing alcohol preserves it perfectly and keeps the features that matter intact;
a squashed spider on a tissue usually cannot be identified at all.

If you would rather not get that close, a vacuum with a hose captures them
reliably, though it damages the specimen.

For photographs, the useful angles are from directly above showing the whole
animal, from the side, and — if you can manage it through the base of a clear
container — from underneath. Something for scale in the frame helps a great deal.
Even then, we may tell you it cannot be resolved to species, and that answer is
more useful than a confident guess.
