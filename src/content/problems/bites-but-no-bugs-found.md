## A common and genuinely difficult situation

Marks are appearing overnight. They itch. They look like bites. And the bed, the
mattress, the frame and the room have been gone over repeatedly with nothing to
show for it.

This is one of the more frustrating things we get called about, and it deserves
a straight, unhurried answer rather than either a quick reassurance or a quick
treatment.

The frustration is real: something is happening to somebody's skin, in their own
bed, and the obvious explanation is not confirming itself. People lose sleep
over this in a very literal sense.

What follows is what a thorough search actually establishes, what else produces
the same marks, and where the question sensibly goes next.

## Why bites alone are weak evidence

This is the part that surprises people most, so it is worth setting out
carefully.

Skin reactions to insect bites vary enormously between individuals. With bed
bugs specifically, a substantial proportion of people show no visible reaction at
all, and among those who do, the timing varies — a mark may appear hours or
days after the bite rather than immediately.

That produces situations that make no intuitive sense. Two people in the same
bed, one covered in marks and one with nothing. Marks appearing in a pattern
that seems to track nights spent at home, when in fact the delay means they
track nights from earlier in the week.

It also means the reverse is true: marks that look exactly like bites are
frequently not bites at all. Dermatologists are clear that no skin lesion can be
identified as a bed bug bite by appearance alone, and the "breakfast, lunch and
dinner" line of three that gets repeated everywhere is a rough pattern rather
than a diagnostic feature.

So a mark on skin tells you something is happening. It does not tell you what,
and it cannot be the whole basis for treating a bedroom.

## What a proper search establishes

The reason a negative search carries weight for bed bugs in particular is that
bed bugs are poor at hiding their evidence even when they are good at hiding
themselves.

A population leaves dark spotting — digested blood — on sheets, along mattress
seams and piping, on the frame and behind a headboard. It leaves pale shed skins
in harborage points, five per insect as they grow. It leaves eggs glued into
cracks. On anything beyond the very earliest introduction, it leaves all three.

Their harborage is also predictable. They stay close to a sleeping person and
they favor cracks, seams and rough or fabric surfaces over smooth open ones. A
thorough search covers the mattress on both faces, the box spring inside and
out, the frame joints and bolt holes, the headboard including its back, the
bedside furniture, the carpet edge, loose baseboard and the seams of any
upholstered item in the room.

If all of that produces no spotting, no skins and no insects, bed bugs become a
very unlikely explanation. Not impossible in the first week or two after an
introduction — but unlikely, and worth saying plainly rather than hedging into a
treatment.

## Other things that bite at night

If it is an insect, these are the realistic candidates and each leaves its own
signature.

**Fleas.** Bites are typically concentrated around ankles and lower legs rather
than on skin exposed while sleeping. They are associated with pets, or with a
property where an animal has been living under a building or in a roof space.
Fleas jump, which bed bugs cannot do, and they are visible on a pet or on light
socks worn around the house.

**Mosquitoes.** Obvious in summer, less obvious when one has come inside and is
resting in a room. Bites are individual and randomly placed, and there is no
evidence trail anywhere.

**Mites associated with a rodent or bird nest.** These are worth taking seriously
because they explain a specific and confusing pattern: biting that started
suddenly, affects one room, and has no visible insect. When a nest in a wall,
attic or soffit is abandoned or its occupants die, the mites that lived on the
animals disperse looking for a host and end up in the room below. They are
extremely small and often not seen. The tell is other evidence of a nest — noise,
droppings, an odor, activity at a roofline — and the fix is dealing with the nest
rather than treating skin.

**Biting midges and gnats**, occasionally, particularly near water or in late
summer.

**Scabies**, which is not an insect we handle at all — it is a mite living in
skin rather than in a building — and it is a medical matter entirely. It is
worth knowing it exists, because it produces intense night-time itching with no
insect ever found in the room, and no amount of pest control will help.

## Things that mark skin without biting

This is the category people rarely consider, and it accounts for a good share of
the situations where nothing is found.

**Carpet beetle larvae.** Common in this housing stock. The larvae are covered in
fine bristly hairs which they shed, and those hairs — together with the
hemolymph — cause an acquired allergic reaction, one that Penn State describes
as building over long exposure rather than striking on first contact, in
some people that produces itchy welts genuinely indistinguishable from bites. No
bite has occurred. The larvae live in carpet edges, closets, under furniture and
in stored textiles, feeding on wool, feathers, pet hair and general lint. If a
search finds larvae or their shed skins, that is a real finding with a real fix.

**Contact reactions.** New detergent, fabric softener, a change of bedding,
mattress materials, a new fabric. These produce marks where fabric touches skin,
which in bed happens to be exactly where bites would be.

**Dry skin and winter conditions.** Heated indoor air in a Whatcom County winter
is drying, and dry skin itches, gets scratched at night, and produces marks in
the morning that look like the cause rather than the effect.

**Reactions to medication**, which can produce hives and marks with no external
cause at all.

**Ordinary skin conditions.** Eczema, folliculitis, hives from any number of
triggers.

We are not doctors and we will not offer an opinion on which of these it is.
What we can do is establish, definitely, whether there is an arthropod in the
room, because that is the question a doctor cannot answer and we can.

## What to gather before the visit

A few things make the search far more productive, and all of them are free.

**Catch something, if you can.** This ends the whole discussion in thirty
seconds. A piece of clear tape pressed over anything you see, or a specimen in a
sealed bag, is worth more than any description. Even a fragment, a shed skin or
something unidentifiable is useful.

**Photograph the marks, dated.** Not because we can diagnose from them — we
cannot — but because the sequence over a fortnight shows a pattern, and a
pattern is informative. A doctor will want the same thing.

**Keep a short log.** Which nights produced marks, which did not, who in the
household was affected, and where you slept each night. Marks that appear
regardless of where somebody sleeps point away from a room. Marks tied to one
bed point toward it.

**Note anything that changed.** New bedding, a new mattress, a change of
detergent, a new pet, recent travel, second-hand furniture, a new medication,
building work, or a period when there was noise or activity in the attic or
walls. Any of those can be the answer, and people frequently do not connect them
because the timing was a few weeks off.

**Do not clean the bedroom thoroughly first.** This is the counterintuitive one.
Stripping and laundering everything before a search removes the exact evidence
the search is looking for. Change the sheets if you need to, but keep the old
ones rather than washing them, and leave the mattress, the frame and the room
edges as they are.

## What if there are pets

Pets change the picture enough to be worth a section, and they are the most
commonly missed explanation in this situation.

Fleas are the obvious candidate, and a household can have a flea problem while
the pet itself shows very little. The reliable checks are combing the animal
over a light surface and looking for flea dirt — dark specks that turn rusty red
when moistened — and looking at the places the animal sleeps rather than at the
places people do. Bites concentrated on ankles and lower legs are the human
signature.

A pet that goes under a building or into a crawlspace can also bring things in
from there, and a cat with access to a roof space is a route for anything living
in it.

The other pet-related situation is an animal that has recently stopped using a
sleeping spot, or a pet that has died or been rehomed. Fleas left behind
without a host emerge and seek one, which produces a sudden onset of biting in a
household where the obvious cause has just been removed.

If there are animals in the house, tell us. It redirects the search
considerably.

## Why we will not treat on suspicion

We get asked to "just treat it to be safe" fairly often, and we decline. Here is
the reasoning.

Applying insecticide in a bedroom, around a bed somebody sleeps in, is not a
neutral act. It is justified when there is something to treat and not otherwise.

It also does not work as reassurance. If the cause is not an insect, the marks
continue after treatment, and the household is now both still affected and
several hundred dollars worse off, with the added anxiety of a treatment that
appears to have failed.

And it forecloses the more useful path. A definite negative is what lets
somebody take the question to a doctor with confidence rather than continuing to
search a mattress every night.

A pest control company that will treat anything on request is not doing you a
favor.

## What a negative result is worth

People occasionally feel they have paid for nothing when a search finds nothing.
We would put it differently.

You have paid to convert an open-ended worry into a definite piece of
information. Before the search, every night carries the question. After it, one
whole branch of possibility is closed off, and it is the branch that was keeping
people awake.

You have also avoided an unnecessary treatment, which costs considerably more
than the search did.

And you have something concrete to bring to a doctor: a professional search of
the sleeping area found no evidence of bed bugs, fleas or other biting
arthropods. That is genuinely useful clinical context and it saves a
conversation.

The verification fee credits against treatment when there is a treatment. When
there is not, the fee stands, and it has bought a real answer.

## What we do when the first search is clear

If nothing is found and the marks continue, there are sensible further steps and
we will talk them through rather than leaving it there.

**Monitoring.** Interceptor cups under the bed legs and light-colored encasements
on the mattress and box spring. Together these make a very early or very sparse
population show up over a few weeks, and they cost little. If something appears,
we come back and there is now something definite to treat.

**Widening the search.** Occasionally the sleeping area is not where the problem
is — a favorite armchair, a couch used for napping, a vehicle, or a second
property. Where somebody has an unusual routine, the search follows it.

**Looking for a nest.** If mites from an animal nest are a plausible explanation,
that is a structural search rather than a bedroom one: the attic, the soffits,
the wall voids and the crawlspace, looking for current or recent occupation.
Where the animal is a rodent, that is squarely our work and it is a different job
from a bed bug search. Where it turns out to be a bird, the answer changes, and
the section below explains why.

**Checking the rest of the room for carpet beetles.** Closet floors, under
furniture, carpet edges, stored wool and textiles. This is a genuinely common
finding and an easy one to miss if somebody is only looking at a bed.

**Referring on.** Where the search is clear and there is no structural
explanation, the honest recommendation is a doctor, and specifically a
dermatologist if it continues. We will say that plainly and without implying
anything about the person asking.

## The nest-mite scenario in more detail

Of all the explanations on this page, this is the one most often missed, and
where the nest belongs to a rodent it is the one we are genuinely the right
people to call about, so it is worth expanding.

Mites that live on a rodent or a bird in its nest do not normally trouble people.
They stay with their host. The problem arises when the host leaves or dies — a
nest abandoned at the end of a season, or a rodent population that has been
dealt with — and the mites are left without one. They disperse, moving outward
and downward through a structure, and they will attempt to feed on whatever they
encounter.

The pattern this produces is distinctive once you know it. Biting that begins
abruptly, with no travel and no new furniture to explain it. Concentration in one
room, often an upstairs bedroom directly below a roof space or beside an
exterior wall. No insect ever seen, because these mites are close to the limit of
what an unaided eye resolves. And frequently, a preceding history — noise in the
ceiling that stopped a couple of weeks ago, a rodent problem that was treated, a
bird's nest in a soffit that has finished for the season.

The important part is that treating the bedroom is not the answer. The answer is
finding and removing the nest, dealing with the material, and closing the
opening that allowed it. Once the source is gone the dispersal ends, and it is a
self-limiting problem rather than an ongoing one.

If the history above sounds familiar, say so on the phone. It changes what we
come out to look at.

## Which nest, and whose job it is

The two animals produce the same experience in a bedroom and they do not produce
the same answer about who to call, so the distinction is worth drawing before
anybody books anything.

**A rodent nest is ours.** Mice or rats in an attic, a wall void, a soffit or a
crawlspace are ordinary work for this company, and the biting downstairs ends
when the nest and the material are removed and the opening is closed behind
them. The wider shape of that job sits on
[rodent control](/services/rodent-control/) and
[exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/), and the mites
themselves have their own profile at
[rodent mites](/pest-library/rodent-mite/), which covers the dispersal pattern
and the timing in more detail than this page has room for.

**A bird nest is not.** The mites off a bird nest behave the same way and the
principle of the fix is the same — the nest comes out, the material goes with
it, the opening closes. But a nest in a soffit, a vent, a chimney or an eave is
bird work, and bird work is not something we take on. Several of those species
carry legal protections with seasonal timing attached, and doing it properly is
a specialty in its own right rather than an add-on to a pest visit.

So where the nest turns out to be a bird's, we will say so plainly, tell you
what we think is happening in the room, and point you to a company set up for
that work. If you are not sure which you have, the noise history usually decides
it: scratching and gnawing at night reads as rodent, while daytime scrabbling
and chirping above a bedroom ceiling in spring reads as birds.

## Being straightforward about this

One last thing, said with care.

People in this situation are sometimes treated dismissively — by pest control
companies who take the money and by others who imply the whole thing is
imagined. Neither is acceptable and neither is what happens here.

Something is producing marks. That is real, it is uncomfortable, and it is worth
getting to the bottom of. Our part of that is narrow and definite: we can
establish, thoroughly and honestly, whether there is an arthropod in the sleeping
area or in the structure around it. If there is, we deal with it. If there is
not, we say so clearly, explain what that does and does not rule out, and point
you toward the person better placed to answer the rest.
