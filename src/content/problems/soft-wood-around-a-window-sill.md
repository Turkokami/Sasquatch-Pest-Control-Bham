## What a soft sill is telling you

Somebody goes to repaint a window, or cleans a sill, or leans on one, and the
wood gives. Sometimes paint has bubbled. Sometimes a screwdriver goes in with
almost no resistance and comes out with damp fibrous material on it.

That is decay, and decay in a building is always a water story.

Wood does not soften on its own. It softens because decay fungi have colonized
it, and those fungi need the wood genuinely wet — the USDA Forest Products
Laboratory puts serious decay at moisture content above the fiber saturation
point, which averages around thirty percent. The number worth remembering is
the safe one rather than the dangerous one: wood held below about twenty
percent is not at risk, which is why every practical remedy on this page is
about keeping timber dry rather than about killing anything.

So the finding is really three findings at once. There is water getting somewhere
it should not. It has been there long enough for decay to establish, which is
generally years rather than months. And the timber involved has lost strength it
will not get back.

Whether an insect is also present is a genuine question, and it is the third
question rather than the first.

## The probe test, done properly

This is the most useful thing a homeowner can do before anybody comes out, and
it costs nothing.

Take something blunt and reasonably rigid — a flat screwdriver, an awl, a
sturdy skewer. Not a knife blade, which cuts rather than probes and gives a
misleading result.

Push it into the wood with firm steady hand pressure. Do not hammer it.

**Sound timber** resists. The tip makes an impression and stops. You can feel it
refusing.

**Early decay** allows the tool in a few millimeters with more give than it
should have, and the surface may feel spongy.

**Advanced decay** lets the tool sink in with little resistance, often an inch or
more, and what comes out on the tip is soft, stringy or crumbly.

Test at several points, not one: the middle of the sill, both ends where it
returns into the jamb, the underside if you can reach it, the bottom corners of
the frame, and the trim and siding immediately below the sill. Then test
somewhere you believe is sound, to calibrate what resistance should feel like.

Mark where it is soft. The pattern tells us as much as the depth.

## Where the water is getting in

At a window opening, the list is short and the same defects recur across the
county's housing.

**Failed sealant.** The bead around the frame hardens, cracks and pulls away.
This is the most common single cause and the cheapest to correct once the damage
is repaired.

**Missing or failed head flashing.** The metal or membrane above the opening that
directs water out over the trim. On a lot of older construction it was never
installed at all, and water simply runs down behind.

**A sill without slope.** Sills are supposed to shed water outward. Where one was
built flat, has settled flat, or has been built up with paint layers, water sits
on it and works into the end grain.

**No drip edge.** Without one, water running off a sill wraps back underneath by
surface tension and into the framing below.

**Failed glazing seals**, letting water into the frame from the glass side.

**Paint film breakdown.** Paint is a moisture barrier only while it is intact.
Once it cracks it becomes a moisture trap, holding water against the wood rather
than letting it dry.

**Something above.** An overflowing gutter, a downspout discharging near the
wall, a sprinkler head hitting the siding, or a roof detail sending water down
that elevation.

**Interior condensation.** Particularly on older aluminum-framed windows, where
winter condensation runs off the metal onto the timber below. No exterior leak
exists at all, and people are frequently surprised by this one.

## Which insects, and what they mean

Once wood is wet and softening, it becomes habitable for several things, and
each leaves a distinct signature.

**Carpenter ants.** The most common in a window frame here. They excavate
galleries to nest in rather than eating the wood, leaving smooth chambers and
pushing out coarse fibrous debris that contains insect body parts. What is in
your frame is usually a satellite nest, with the main colony outdoors in a
stump, a log or a woodpile.

**Moisture ants.** Small and yellowish, occupying wood that is properly wet
rather than merely damp. They build a distinctive spongy carton material from
chewed wood and soil. They are a strong indicator of a serious water problem and
are less structurally aggressive than carpenter ants.

**Dampwood termites.** Larger, requiring high moisture content, and cutting
galleries across the grain as well as along it. Their galleries tend to be packed
with dark material rather than swept clean, and they produce hard six-sided
pellets. Treating wood-destroying organisms is within our licensed categories,
and where a finding has been identified we handle that side of it.

**Wood-boring beetles.** Fine floury powder and small round exit holes. Often
historic and often not requiring treatment at all, depending on species and
moisture.

**Nothing.** Genuinely common, and worth saying. A substantial share of soft sills
are decay alone. In that case there is no pest treatment to sell and the job
belongs entirely to a carpenter and whoever fixes the water.

## How far it goes

This is the question people most want answered and the one that most often
surprises them.

Water that gets past a sill runs down. It follows the framing, and it wets
whatever is below before it evaporates or drains. So visible softness at a sill
is frequently the top of a taller column of damage rather than the whole of it.

The members typically involved, in order as you go down: the sill itself, the
sill horns and the lower ends of the jambs, the rough framing under the opening,
the cripple studs below it, the sheathing behind the trim, and — where the run
has been long and the window is on a lower floor — the bottom plate and even the
rim joist.

None of that is visible without opening something up, which is a carpenter's
work rather than ours. What we can do is probe everything accessible, look at the
wall from inside where there is access, check the crawlspace or basement below
that elevation, and give you an informed view of how far to expect the repair to
extend once it is opened.

The honest position is that nobody knows precisely until the trim comes off. What
we can tell you is whether the evidence suggests a sill and trim job or something
larger, and that distinction is worth having before you take quotes.

## Why filler and paint make it worse

The instinct to fill and repaint is strong, and it is worth explaining why it is
usually the wrong move.

Decayed wood has lost structural material. Filling the void does not restore it;
it puts a rigid material into soft surroundings, where it will eventually work
loose because the wood around it continues to fail.

Paint over decayed timber seals moisture in. Wood that could previously dry
between rain events now cannot, and the moisture content stays above the
threshold that keeps the fungi active. The decay continues under a smooth
surface, which is why the same sill fails again in two years looking worse.

And the cosmetic repair removes the evidence. The soft area that told you where
the water was is now hidden, and the next person to look at it — a buyer's
inspector, a contractor, you in three years — finds a much larger problem with no
warning signs.

Filler has a legitimate place: small superficial defects, nail holes, minor
weathering, in timber that is otherwise sound under a probe. It is not a repair
for wood that a screwdriver sinks into.

## What a real repair looks like

For reference, since it will be somebody else doing it.

The affected timber is cut out back to sound material — not sanded, not filled,
cut back — and replaced with new stock, preferably a durable species or
appropriately treated material.

The water entry is corrected at the same time: flashing installed or repaired,
sealant renewed properly, a drip edge added, slope restored to the sill.

New timber going into a location with a history is worth borate-treating before
it is closed up. It is inexpensive at that stage and effective for a long time.

Finish is applied to all faces including the back and the end grain before
installation, because end grain is where water enters timber fastest and it is
the surface most often left bare.

And whatever was causing the water from above — the gutter, the downspout, the
sprinkler, the roof detail — gets dealt with, or the new sill starts the same
clock.

## The signs that come before softness

Decay announces itself for a while before a probe confirms it, and knowing the
early signals lets people catch this at the cheap stage.

**Paint failing in one specific area.** Not general weathering across an
elevation, but blistering, cracking or peeling concentrated at a sill, a bottom
corner or a joint. Paint fails where the substrate is wet, so a localized
failure is a localized moisture problem.

**Staining.** Dark streaks or patches on the sill or the trim below it, often
running downward from a joint or a corner.

**A joint that has opened.** Where two pieces of trim meet, or where the sill
meets the jamb, a gap appearing means movement, and movement in exterior joinery
usually means moisture cycling.

**Discoloration of the wood itself** where it is exposed — gray, brown or
unusually dark patches, sometimes with a slightly velvety look.

**Fasteners weeping rust stains**, which indicates persistent moisture around
them.

**Cubical cracking.** Later-stage brown rot breaks wood into small rectangular
blocks across and along the grain. If you can see that pattern, the timber is
well past salvageable.

**Fungal growth**, from a white stringy mat to visible fruiting bodies. Less
common on an exterior sill than in a crawlspace, and unmistakable when present.

**Condensation on the interior**, pooling on the sill on cold mornings.

Any one of those in isolation is worth a probe test on a dry afternoon. It takes
five minutes and the difference between catching this at paint-blistering and
catching it at soft-under-a-screwdriver is often several thousand dollars.

## Which windows in which houses

The building stock in this county fails in patterns, and knowing yours helps
predict what you will find.

**Pre-war and early post-war wood windows.** Old-growth material, dense and
naturally durable, in single-glazed frames with putty glazing. They last
remarkably well, but the putty and the paint film need maintaining, and where
that lapsed for a couple of decades the bottom rails and sills are where it
shows. The good news is that the timber itself is usually worth repairing rather
than replacing.

**Mid-century aluminum in wood surrounds.** The condensation case described
below. Often no exterior defect at all, and the damage on the inside.

**1970s and 1980s softwood.** Faster-grown, less durable material, frequently in
frames that have had several coats of paint over failing substrate. These fail
more readily than the older stock.

**Replacement units in original openings.** A very common and frequently
troublesome situation. Where a new window was fitted without correcting the
flashing and the sill detail behind it, the visible unit is modern and sound
while the original framing continues to get wet. People spend money replacing a
window that was not the problem, then find the same softness returning below the
new one.

**Newer construction.** Generally better detailed, with its own failure mode
where housewrap, flashing tape and sealant meet at the head and jambs. When those
fail the water is inside a well-sealed assembly that cannot dry, which can
produce faster damage than an older, breathable wall.

## The condensation cases

Worth a separate note because they are commonly misdiagnosed and the repair is
different.

On older aluminum-framed windows, and on some steel-framed ones, the frame
conducts cold straight through. In a Whatcom County winter that produces
condensation on the interior face of the metal, which runs down and collects on
the timber sill below. Over years it produces exactly the same soft sill as an
exterior leak.

The tell is that the damage is on the interior side and the exterior detailing
looks intact. Also common: mold or staining on the interior sill and the lower
part of the frame, and moisture visible on the metal on cold mornings.

The remedy is not sealant. It is reducing indoor humidity — extract fans that
actually discharge outside, crawlspace vapor barrier, ventilation — and, in the
longer run, replacing or improving the window assembly.

If your problem is on the inside, say so when you call. It changes the whole
diagnosis.

## When it comes up in a sale

Soft exterior joinery is one of the most frequently flagged items in a property
transaction here, and it produces a predictable sequence.

A buyer's inspector probes the sills, finds movement, and writes it up. The
report notes decay and, where insects are present, notes those too. What follows
is usually a request for repair and, where a wood-destroying organism has been
identified, a treatment.

Those are two different trades. The repair is a carpenter's. The treatment of a
wood-destroying organism is within what we are licensed to carry out, and we
handle that part.

The formal document a transaction may call for is not something we issue, and we
are not structural pest inspectors. That work belongs to a separately licensed
professional, and we will point you to one rather than blur the boundary to keep
a job.

For sellers, the practical advice is unglamorous and reliable: soft sills do not
improve while a property is listed, they photograph badly, and they invite a
larger concession than the repair would have cost. Dealing with them before
listing, in your own time and with your own choice of contractor, is nearly
always cheaper than negotiating over them.

For buyers, the useful question to ask is not whether there is decay but how far
it goes. A sill and trim replacement is a modest job. The same finding with a
compromised bottom plate behind it is a different conversation, and nobody can
tell you which you have until something is opened.

## Keeping the next one from happening

Once a sill has been repaired properly, the maintenance that keeps it sound is
modest and almost nobody does it.

Walk the exterior joinery once a year on a dry day, ideally in late summer. Look
at every sill, every bottom corner, every joint, and the trim below each opening.
Probe anywhere the paint is failing.

Renew sealant before it fails rather than after. It has a working life measured
in years, not decades, and replacing a bead costs a fraction of replacing a sill.

Keep the paint film intact, particularly on horizontal surfaces and end grain,
which are where water enters fastest. A sill that is repainted every few years
rarely fails.

Keep gutters clear and downspouts discharging well away from walls. A remarkable
share of window decay is really a gutter problem that nobody connected to the
window six feet below it.

Cut back vegetation touching the wall. A shrub against siding keeps that
elevation from ever drying.

Check where sprinklers land. Irrigation hitting a wall daily through a summer
does more damage than the rain does.

And on any window that has already been repaired once, put a note in a calendar
to look at it again in two years. Locations that failed once tend to be the
locations that were always going to.
