## It is almost certainly not sawdust

Someone notices a small heap of pale wood-colored material on a floor, a
windowsill, a stair tread or the top of a baseboard. It looks like sawdust,
so that is what it gets called, and quite often it gets swept up and forgotten.

Then it comes back, and that is when we get the call.

Debris that reappears after being cleared is not sawdust. Sawdust is a
by-product of somebody cutting timber, and once you have cleaned it up it stays
cleaned up. Material that accumulates again over days or weeks is being actively
pushed out of the wood by something living inside it, and identifying what that
something is, before anybody treats anything, is the whole of the job at this
stage.

There are four realistic answers in a Whatcom County building. They look
superficially similar and they are easy to tell apart once you know what to look
for.

## Look at the texture first

Pick a small quantity up between finger and thumb, or better, put some on a
sheet of white paper where you can see it properly.

**Coarse and fibrous, like shredded wood or wood wool.** Carpenter ants. The
material is excavated rather than eaten, so it retains a fibrous structure, and
individual pieces are visibly wood shavings rather than powder.

**Fine, soft and floury, like talc or very fine flour.** Wood-boring beetles.
Larvae eat their way through the timber and what comes out has been through a
digestive system, so it has no structure left at all. Some species produce a
slightly gritty version, but the defining quality is that it is a powder rather
than a shaving.

**Hard, granular pellets that feel like coarse sand or fine gravel.** Termites,
specifically the drywood and dampwood species. The pellets are distinctly
six-sided under magnification, uniform in size, and often colored like the wood
they came from.

**White, chalky, angular and mixed with paper fiber.** Not an insect at all —
this is drywall or plaster debris, and it points at something mechanical:
settlement, a fastener working loose, or a route being made by an animal rather
than an insect.

## Then look at what is in it

This is the step that resolves most remaining ambiguity, and it needs nothing
more than good light.

Carpenter ant frass contains insect parts. Discarded legs, wing fragments from
spent reproductives, pieces of head and thorax from dead colony members. The
colony housekeeps by pushing everything out through the same slits it uses for
debris, so the pile is a mixture of wood fiber and body parts. Finding fragments
in the pile is close to conclusive.

Beetle powder contains nothing but powder. No fragments, no fibers, no
inclusions.

Termite pellets are uniform and clean. If your pile is entirely made of similar
small hard granules with no dust and no fragments, that is what you are looking
at.

And if the pile contains chewed paper, insulation fiber, fabric threads or
plastic shreds, you have moved into a different category altogether — that is
nesting material rather than excavated wood, and it means a rodent.

## Where the pile sits

The location narrows it further, because each of these has habits.

**Below trim, baseboard, casing or a stair stringer**, in a heap that keeps
returning: most consistent with carpenter ants working a gallery in framing or
in the trim itself. The debris exits through a slit, often at a joint or a
nail hole, and falls straight down.

**Below a window sill or the bottom corner of a frame**: carpenter ants again in
most cases, with water damage behind it.

**Under exposed structural timber in a crawlspace or a garage, on the concrete
below a beam or joist**: could be any of the three insects, and this is where
identification matters most because it is structural material.

**Scattered around small round holes in a wood surface**: wood-boring beetles.
The holes are the exit holes of emerging adults, typically between one and three
millimeters across depending on species, and their presence with fine powder
below is diagnostic.

**In a loft or against a ceiling, mixed with other material**: worth checking for
the rodent answer before assuming an insect.

## The four candidates in more detail

**Carpenter ants.** The most common of the four here by a wide margin. They
excavate galleries to nest in and do not consume the wood. Galleries are
characteristically smooth, almost sanded, and kept clean, which is why the
debris ends up outside. Activity peaks in spring and summer, and it is strongly
associated with timber that has been wet. Critically, what is in your wall is
usually a satellite nest, with the main colony outdoors in a stump, a log, a
dead limb or buried wood — so treating the debris site alone is not a
resolution.

**Wood-boring beetles.** A group rather than a species, and the important
question is which one, because the outlook differs enormously. Some infest only
freshly felled or recently milled timber and die out on their own once the wood
is seasoned — treating those is spending money on a problem that is already
ending. Others will reinfest seasoned timber repeatedly and do need
intervention. Fine powder and small round exit holes are the shared signature;
telling the harmless from the persistent needs a look at the wood, the hole size
and the moisture.

**Termites.** Present in this region, and in this county that means the dampwood
species associated with wet, decaying timber. The soil-nesting subterranean
species that builds mud tubes and works up from ground contact is documented in
western Washington only as far north as Seattle, per WSU Cooperative Extension
bulletin EB0787, so it belongs on the list as an outside possibility rather than
as a co-equal candidate. Termite damage differs visibly from carpenter ant
damage: the galleries are rough rather than smooth and are typically packed with
mud and debris rather than swept clean. Where a report from an inspector has
identified a wood-destroying organism in your building, the treatment side of
that is within what we are certified to carry out.

**Something mechanical or rodent-related.** Worth keeping on the list, because
we are called out to plaster dust and to rodent nesting debris often enough that
it is not a remote possibility.

## What to do before anyone comes out

Three things, none of which takes long, and all of which make the visit more
useful.

**Collect a sample.** A tablespoon in a sealed bag or a small jar. Do not wet
it. A sample tells us more in thirty seconds than a description does in ten
minutes, and a photograph of a pile on a dark floor rarely resolves texture.

**Clear the rest and date it.** Note the day you cleared it. Whether it returns,
how quickly, and how much, distinguishes an active gallery from historic debris
that has simply been sitting there. A pile that does not return in three weeks
is a very different situation from one that rebuilds in four days.

**Look up.** Debris falls, so the source is above the pile, not at it. Follow the
line vertically and look for the opening — a slit, a small round hole, a gap at
a joint. Photographing that opening is worth as much as the sample.

If you can also tell us whether there has been a leak, a roof problem, a burst
pipe or persistent condensation anywhere near that spot in the last few years,
you have probably explained the whole thing.

## The openings tell you as much as the pile

Once you have followed the debris upward, the shape of the hole it came out of
is its own identification.

**A narrow slit or an irregular gap at a joint**, often where two pieces of trim
meet or where a fastener sits. Carpenter ants do not make round holes for debris
disposal — they use existing gaps and openings they have widened, and the result
looks like a crack rather than a bore.

**A clean round hole between one and three millimeters across.** A beetle exit
hole, made by an adult chewing out of the timber after developing inside it.
Round is the key word. Several of them scattered across a surface is typical.

**A larger oval or D-shaped hole.** A different group of beetles, generally
associated with hardwood, and much less common in the framing and trim of houses
here.

**A hole plastered around the edges with mud or a hard packed material.** That
is a termite characteristic and it moves the finding up the priority list.

**A ragged opening with fibrous or shredded edges at the base of trim.** Not an
insect. Rodents gnaw and the edge shows it.

Photograph the opening with something for scale — a coin, a pencil tip — and you
have given us most of what we need before anyone has driven anywhere.

## When the trim itself is the problem

Modern trim materials change this picture and it is worth knowing which you
have.

Finger-jointed softwood trim, which is standard in a great deal of construction
from the last few decades, is made of short pieces glued together. It takes on
water readily at the joints once the paint film fails, and it softens quickly,
which makes it more attractive than solid stock.

MDF and composite trim behaves differently again. It does not support carpenter
ant galleries well, but it fails badly when wet — swelling, delaminating and
losing all strength — and the framing behind it then takes the water instead.
Debris below MDF trim is more often the material itself disintegrating than
anything living.

Solid old-growth trim in older houses is dense, slow to wet and slow to decay,
and where it is failing there is usually a specific and locatable water source
rather than general weathering.

Painted trim hides everything until the paint film breaks, at which point the
timber underneath has often been wet for years. Probing gently with something
blunt at a suspicious point tells you more than looking does — sound wood
resists, softened wood gives.

## The crawlspace version of this finding

Debris found on a crawlspace floor, on a vapor barrier or on concrete below a
beam is the same evidence in a more consequential location, and it deserves a
different level of attention.

The material below you there is structural. Joists, beams, posts, the sill plate
and the rim joist are what hold the building up, and they are also the parts of
a house most likely to be damp in this climate, which puts them squarely in the
range of everything discussed above.

Two additional signs matter down there. Mud tubes — pencil-width earthen tracks
running up a foundation wall, a pier or a post — are a subterranean termite
signature and are worth acting on. And a general softness in the sill plate or
rim joist, where a blunt probe sinks in rather than stopping, indicates decay
that has usually been running long enough to matter.

We look at the crawlspace on essentially every visit for exactly this reason,
even when the reported problem is a small pile of dust upstairs. A remarkable
proportion of the answers are down there.

## What we will not sell you

**A treatment before an identification.** If we cannot tell you what you have,
we are not going to quote you for treating it.

**A beetle treatment for timber that has already seasoned out.** Where the
evidence points to an infestation that is ending on its own, that is what we
will tell you, and there is no charge for the conversation.

**A whole-structure treatment for a single localized finding.** The scope
should follow the evidence.

**Recurring service in place of a repair.** Where the driver is a leak, a
drainage fault or a failed flashing detail, a quarterly visit does not address
it and we will not pretend it does.

**Work on a wood-destroying organism finding without seeing it ourselves.** We
treat what is genuinely there. If somebody else's report says one thing and the
timber says another, we will raise it rather than proceed quietly.

## Why identification decides the price

Homeowners sometimes find it strange that we will not quote from a description,
and it is because the four answers carry very different work.

Carpenter ants mean an outdoor search for a parent colony, treatment at both the
satellite and the source, and a moisture correction. It is a moderate job, and
the durable version of it is not the cheap version.

Beetles mean identifying the species and the moisture condition, and the answer
is frequently that no treatment is warranted at all because the infestation is
in timber that has already seasoned past the point where it can continue.

Termites mean a considerably more serious conversation about structure, extent
and ground contact.

Rodent or mechanical debris means we are not the answer at all, or we are the
answer to a completely different question.

Anybody willing to quote a treatment from a phone description of a small pile of
dust is guessing, and a guess in this situation has a decent chance of being
both expensive and useless.

## Damage, in proportion

People are usually more frightened by the pile than the situation warrants, and
occasionally less frightened than they should be, so here is the proportionate
view.

Carpenter ants work slowly. A season of activity in a wall does not usually
produce structural consequence. Years of activity in chronically wet framing
can, and by that point the decay that attracted them has generally taken more
material than the insects have.

Beetle damage in most cases here is cosmetic and historic. In the minority of
cases where it is not, it is worth taking seriously.

Termite damage is the one that can move fastest and is most likely to reach
structural significance, which is why identification matters rather than
reassurance.

And in every case, the thing driving all of it in this climate is water. Dry
timber in a well-drained, well-ventilated building is not attractive to any of
these organisms. Most of what we recommend after an identification is about
keeping wood dry, and that recommendation is worth more over a decade than any
treatment.

## Seasonality, and what it adds

When the debris appears is another piece of evidence, and it is free.

Carpenter ant activity here runs from early spring through late summer, with the
strongest signs as the weather warms and again around the swarm period. Debris
that begins accumulating in April and stops in October fits that pattern
closely. Activity that continues through a cold January in a heated building is
still possible but suggests a nest well inside the conditioned envelope rather
than in an exterior wall.

Beetle emergence is tightly seasonal too, generally late spring into summer,
which is when the exit holes and the powder appear. Debris that shows up once a
year in the same weeks and then stops is very characteristic.

Termite swarming in this region tends to run late summer into autumn for the
dampwood species, and spring for subterranean species.

Debris that accumulates steadily and indifferently all year, with no seasonal
shape at all, is more likely to be mechanical or rodent-related than insect.

So a note of the date each time you clear a pile builds, over a few months, a
picture that no single visit can give us. It is the cheapest diagnostic
available and almost nobody does it.
