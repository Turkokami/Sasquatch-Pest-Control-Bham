## It happens on a schedule

There is a specific and consistent pattern to this, and once you see it as a
calendar event rather than an invasion it becomes considerably easier to live
with.

Every year, from somewhere around the last week of August through October, a
significant proportion of the region's spiders reach sexual maturity at
approximately the same time. The males then abandon the sedentary life they have
led up to that point and go looking for females.

They do this on foot. A mature male funnel weaver or orb weaver covers
substantial ground over those weeks, moving mostly at night, following edges and
surfaces, going through and around whatever is in the way.

Houses are in the way. A building sitting in this landscape is a large obstacle
with a great many horizontal gaps at ground level, and a proportion of the
spiders walking past will go under a door rather than around a foundation.

Then, in late October and November, it stops. The males have found females or
have not, and either way their season is over. The wave ends as reliably as it
began.

## What it is not

Several intuitive explanations for this are wrong, and they matter because they
lead to the wrong response.

**It is not spiders coming in from the cold.** Spiders in this region are adapted
to the winter here and do not need to be indoors to survive it. Most native and
established species overwinter perfectly well outside, as adults, juveniles or
eggs. The idea that they are seeking central heating is intuitive and it is not
what is happening.

**It is not an infestation.** An infestation is a population arriving and
building up inside a structure. This is not that. What runs through these weeks
is movement by animals that reached maturity on a schedule, and it subsides
without anything having established itself.

**It is not a sign that anything has gone wrong with your house.** The same wave
happens to well-sealed new construction and to drafty older stock; the
difference is quantity, not principle.

**It is not because you did something differently this year.** Year-to-year
variation is driven by the preceding summer's weather and insect abundance far
more than by anything a household changes.

## Did they walk in, or were they already inside?

The usual account of this — ours included, until we went back and checked it — is
that the spiders walked in from outside. There is a second account, from a source
that outranks most of what gets published on the subject, and the two belong side
by side rather than one instead of the other.

The Burke Museum holds the state reference collection for arachnology in this
region, and its position is that house spiders are largely a distinct
indoor-adapted lineage rather than outdoor spiders that came in. Generally fewer
than five percent of the spiders seen indoors, on that reading, have ever been
outdoors at all — which is also why the museum points out that carrying one out
to the lawn is not returning it anywhere. The male crossing a kitchen floor in
September hatched in the building or in the crawlspace beneath it, and September
is simply the month he stopped sitting in a web and started walking.

The first account is not wrong either. Mature males living along a foundation, in
a window well, under a deck or in a woodpile do cross thresholds in these weeks,
which is precisely why a worn door sweep changes what a household sees. Both
things happen. How the total divides between them is a question for people with a
collection and a microscope rather than for a pest control company, and we would
rather say that than pick the version that sells better.

What matters is that the two readings recommend the same work. Under either one
the population was in and around the building well before anybody noticed it.
Under either one the gap at floor level is worth closing, because the same
threshold is a route in from the yard and a route up from the crawlspace. Under
either one the insect supply is the lever, because spiders settle where they can
eat and nowhere else. And under either one the whole thing stops in November
without a treatment having been involved.

## Which spiders

The autumn walkers here are a fairly consistent cast.

**Giant house spiders.** The large, fast, brown, long-legged ones that produce
most of the alarm. Males walking in autumn are what people find in bathtubs and
running across floors at night. Harmless.

**Other funnel weavers**, including the hobo spider, which is closely related and
whose fearsome reputation has been substantially retracted by the researchers and
agencies that once promoted it.

**Cross orb weavers.** Outdoors rather than in, but very visible in this season
because the females are at full size and their wheel webs appear across doorways,
between shrubs and along fence lines. They are the reason people walk into a web
on the way to the car in September.

**Cellar spiders**, which are indoors year round rather than seasonally, but get
noticed more when everybody is looking.

**Wolf spiders**, ground hunters that come in at floor level and are sometimes
mistaken for something more serious because of their size and speed.

The one worth genuine caution locally is the western black widow, and it is not
part of the autumn walk at all. The state Department of Health describes it as
common to eastern Washington, and the range follows the drier, warmer country
east and south of the Cascades, so on this side of them it is uncommon. Where
one does turn up here it is sitting in an undisturbed dry place — a woodpile, a
meter box, a crawlspace corner, the underside of stored material — rather than
crossing a living room in September.

## Why the bathtub

This gets asked more than anything else on the subject, so: they do not come up
the drain.

Modern plumbing has a water trap in it — the U-bend under every fixture — that a
spider cannot pass. What happens instead is that a spider walking across a
bathroom at night encounters a smooth-sided ceramic or acrylic basin, walks in,
and cannot get out again. The sides offer no purchase.

Sinks and tubs are simply pitfall traps, and the spider you find in the morning
walked in from the room.

If you would like fewer of them, leaving something in the tub that offers a route
out — a towel over the edge, a face cloth — genuinely works.

## Why some rooms and not others

The distribution inside a house is about routes rather than about preferences.

**Ground floor beats upstairs**, comfortably, because entry is at floor level.

**Rooms with exterior doors** take the most, particularly where a door sweep has
worn, hardened or lifted at the corners.

**Basements and garages** are entry-rich: bigger gaps, more penetrations, less
sealing, and doors that are opened frequently.

**Bathrooms and utility rooms** collect them because of the smooth basins and
because of the plumbing penetrations that connect to the floor structure.

**Rooms on the wooded side of a building** take more than rooms facing a street.

**North-facing walls** tend to be damper and support more of the insect life that
spiders follow.

Upstairs sightings usually mean either a route up through a wall cavity or a
spider that has been in the building a while.

## Why some years are worse

The variation is real and it is predictable in hindsight.

A warm summer produces more insects, more insects support more spiders, and more
spiders reach maturity to walk in September. A cool wet summer does the reverse.

The weather during the dispersal matters as much. A warm dry September has
spiders moving actively night after night. A cold wet one suppresses movement and
spreads the same number of individuals over a longer, less noticeable period.

Local disturbance contributes too. Clearing, construction, a hedge coming out or
a lot being developed nearby displaces populations, and buildings within a
couple of hundred feet see the difference.

And moisture on the property compounds everything. A damp crawlspace, poor
drainage, vegetation against walls and standing water all support insect
populations, and the spider numbers track the insects.

## The timing nobody uses

Here is the practical point of this whole page.

The work that reduces the autumn wave is most effective when it is done in the
second half of August, before the dispersal begins. Sealing an entry point in
late August means the spiders never get in. Sealing it in late September means
sealing around the ones that already have.

Almost nobody calls in August, because in August nothing is wrong. The calls
start in the third week of September, when the wave is already running.

If this happens to your house every year — and for most houses in this county it
does — then next year's version of it is decided by what gets done at the end of
next summer. That is worth putting in a calendar.

## What actually reduces it

In rough order of how much difference each makes.

**Door sweeps and thresholds.** The single largest lever. A gap you can see
daylight through under an exterior door is a highway. Replacing worn sweeps,
particularly on garage side doors and basement doors, is cheap and immediately
effective.

**Exterior lighting.** Bright white light near doors and windows pulls in insects
in volume, and the spiders follow the food. Warmer color temperatures, motion
sensors instead of dusk-to-dawn, and moving fixtures away from entrances all
reduce the whole chain rather than one link of it.

**Weatherstripping and window seals**, especially on older stock where frames
have moved.

**Screens repaired**, and sliding door tracks closed at the ends.

**Utility penetrations sealed** — the holes cut for water, waste, gas, power,
cable and heat pump lines.

**Vegetation pulled back off the walls.** Shrubs, climbers and overhanging growth
are both habitat and bridge, and a clear band around a building helps with
several problems at once.

**Webs removed regularly**, inside and out, through the season. Consistent
removal genuinely discourages rebuilding in the same spot.

**Crawlspace moisture addressed**, because a wet underfloor space is an insect
factory and everything above it scales accordingly.

**Clutter reduced** in garages and storage areas, which is where the spiders you
would actually rather avoid choose to live.

## A month-by-month view

If it helps to think of this as a yearly cycle rather than an annual surprise,
here is roughly how the spider year runs in this county.

**January to March.** Very little visible. Overwintering adults are inactive,
egg sacs are waiting, and the few spiders indoors are established residents like
cellar spiders. A good time for sealing work, because nothing is moving and the
weather is bad enough that indoor jobs are welcome.

**April and May.** Egg sacs hatch and enormous numbers of tiny spiderlings
disperse, some of them by ballooning on silk threads. Almost none of this is
noticed. Insect populations begin to rise.

**June and July.** Growth. Spiders are outdoors, small, and building the webs
that will be conspicuous later. Orb weaver webs start appearing in gardens
toward the end of this period. Insect numbers peak, which determines how many
spiders make it to autumn.

**August.** Females reach full size and their webs become obvious. Males begin
maturing. The last two weeks are the window where preventive work does the most
good, and the point at which almost nobody thinks about it.

**September.** The wave. Males walking, indoor sightings daily in some houses,
orb webs across every doorway in the morning. This is when the phone rings.

**October.** Continuing but easing through the month. Egg sacs are laid. Weather
increasingly suppresses movement.

**November and December.** Effectively over. What you see indoors from here is
resident rather than transient.

Two things fall out of that. The useful maintenance months are the quiet ones,
and the season you are experiencing in September was determined by a summer you
have already had.

## Where treatment fits

We do treat for this, and we will be clear about what it does.

A targeted exterior application in late summer, at entry points, around door and
window frames, at the foundation line and under eaves, reduces how many spiders
cross the threshold and shortens how long they survive once inside. Applied
before the wave, it makes a noticeable difference to a bad season.

What it does not do is prevent the dispersal. Spiders will continue to walk
across your property because they live in the landscape around it, and no
treatment applied to a house changes that.

We also do not treat orb weavers outdoors as a matter of course. A wheel web
across a doorway is easily removed with a broom, the spider will rebuild
somewhere else, and it spends the rest of its short life removing flies from your
garden.

Where somebody has a serious phobia, the priority shifts and we will say so
without treating it as an unreasonable request. It is a genuine quality-of-life
issue and the approach — entries, lighting, webs, insects — is the same, just
pursued harder.

## What does not work

There is a large market in autumn spider remedies and most of it does nothing.

**Peppermint oil, and essential oils generally.** Widely recommended online.
There is little evidence of any lasting repellent effect at domestic
concentrations, and what effect exists evaporates within days. It smells
pleasant, which is most of its appeal.

**Chestnuts on windowsills.** A folk remedy with no supporting evidence
whatsoever. It persists because the season ends on its own a few weeks after
people try it.

**Ultrasonic plug-in devices.** No credible evidence of effect on spiders or on
anything else. Reviewed repeatedly and consistently found not to work.

**Vinegar sprays.** Kills what they land on directly, does nothing afterwards.

**Diatomaceous earth scattered around a room.** A real mechanism against
insects that cross it repeatedly, but spiders walk on the tips of their legs and
cover ground quickly, and it is ineffective once damp. It is also a respiratory
irritant when scattered loosely indoors.

**Conkers, sprays, sachets and gadgets generally.** If a product claims to make a
house spider-free through the autumn, the claim is doing the selling.

We mention these not to be dismissive of people trying things — everybody tries
something — but because money spent on them is money not spent on a door sweep,
which actually works.

## Living with the season

For most households the proportionate response is a small set of habits rather
than a program.

Keep a glass and a piece of card somewhere handy, because the catch-and-release
method takes ten seconds and works on anything.

Leave a towel over the edge of the tub in September.

Shake out shoes and gloves left in a garage or a shed, which is sensible year
round.

Knock down webs at doorways in the morning rather than walking into them at
night.

And know that the third week of October is roughly when it starts easing, and
November is when it is done. It is a season, and seasons end.

## The other autumn arrivals

Spiders are not the only thing walking in during those weeks, and it is worth
knowing what else the season brings because the calls tend to arrive together.

**Rodents.** The same cooling that triggers the spider dispersal drives the
annual rodent push, which runs from late September through November. If your
house is getting spiders under a door, it is worth checking the same openings for
the gaps that matter to a mouse.

**Cluster flies.** Large sluggish flies gathering on warm south and west walls in
October, then finding their way into attics, wall voids and upstairs rooms to
overwinter. They reappear on warm winter days, which confuses people.

**Stink bugs and boxelder bugs.** Congregating on sunlit walls in the same weeks
and using the same gaps to get into wall cavities and attics.

**Ladybird beetles**, which do the same thing and are more welcome.

**Wasps.** Not arriving so much as ending. Late-season colonies are at maximum
size and their foragers are looking for sugar, which is why they turn up around
bins and fruit in September before dying off with the frosts.

The reason to mention all of this on a spider page is that the remedy overlaps
almost entirely. Door sweeps, weatherstripping, sealed penetrations, repaired
screens and closed vents deal with the spiders, the mice, the flies and the
overwintering beetles at once. Work done in August for one of them is work done
for all five.

## If somebody in the house is frightened of them

We deal with this often and we would rather address it directly than skirt it.

A serious fear of spiders is common, it is not a character flaw, and for the
people who have it the six weeks from late August are genuinely difficult —
rooms avoided, sleep disrupted, a household reorganized around where something
was last seen.

If that describes your situation, say so when you call. It changes the
priorities. The measures are the same but the emphasis shifts: sealing done
before the season rather than during it, bedrooms and bathrooms prioritized over
a garage, webs cleared on a schedule rather than when noticed, and a treatment
timed to the front of the wave rather than the middle of it.

It also changes how we talk about the result. We will not tell you your house
will be spider-free, because it will not be and a promise like that fails
badly. What we will do is set out honestly how much of a reduction is
achievable, so that the season is planned for rather than endured.
