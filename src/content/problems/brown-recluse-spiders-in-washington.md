## The short answer, and the reason it is worth stating plainly

There are no brown recluse spiders in Washington.

Not rare here. Not occasionally here. Not here. The Washington State Department
of Health states that brown recluse spiders do not live in Washington, and the
Burke Museum at the University of Washington — which holds the state's
arachnology reference collection, well over a hundred thousand Washington
spiders — has never had a Washington brown recluse in it. The nearest
established population is in the south-central United States, most of a
continent from here.

We state it this flatly because the alternative has a cost. People in this
county spend real money on treatments for a spider that does not exist here, and
rather more importantly, people walk around with untreated skin infections
because somebody told them it was a spider bite and spider bites take time to
heal.

## Where the brown recluse actually lives

The south-central states, and the Burke Museum's distribution map stops well
short of anywhere near us.

The museum's position on reports from outside that range is blunt: of the
hundreds of brown recluse bite reports made each year in the United States, at
least 80% are false, and in the Pacific coast states it considers essentially
100% of them to be errors. It further estimates that around 95% of such cases
are not spider bites of any kind.

That is a strong claim and it comes from the institution that identifies the
specimens. When somebody sends a suspected recluse to an arachnologist in this
state, it comes back as something else, every time.

## What people actually have

Nearly always one of three things, and all three are harmless.

**A [giant house spider](/pest-library/giant-house-spider/).** Large, brown,
fast, and the animal behind most alarmed phone calls in Whatcom County. WSU
reports it outnumbers the hobo spider in the Puget Sound region and is far more
frequently encountered.

**A [hobo spider](/pest-library/hobo-spider/).** The one that inherited the
recluse's reputation in the Pacific Northwest, and which turned out not to
deserve it either. That page goes through what the evidence actually showed.

**A [wolf spider](/pest-library/wolf-spider/).** Hairy, striped, hunting on the
ground, and the species most often compared to a recluse in national articles.
It lacks the violin marking and has eight eyes rather than six.

The full list, in order of how likely each is in a house here, is in
[common spiders in Washington state](/guides/spiders-in-washington-state/).

## How to tell, if you want to be sure

Three checks, in order of how much they are worth.

**Count the eyes.** A brown recluse has six eyes arranged in three pairs. Almost
every spider in Washington has eight. On a sharp photograph this is visible, and
it is the only one of these checks that is genuinely diagnostic.

**Look at the build.** A recluse is small — well under an inch of body — smooth,
matte, uniformly tan, with long thin legs and no pattern on the abdomen. If your
spider is big, hairy, banded, striped or stocky, it is not one.

**Ignore the violin.** The fiddle marking behind the head is the famous
character and the one that causes the most trouble, because people find violins
on spiders that have no markings at all, and because several harmless species
have a darker patch in roughly that position.

## If you have a wound

See a doctor about the wound, and do not let the spider become the story.

The Burke Museum lists what actually produces the lesions blamed on recluses:
bacterial and fungal infections, lymphatic disorders, delayed hypersensitivity
reactions, and bites from ticks and other blood-feeding arthropods. Several of
those get materially worse during the weeks somebody spends waiting for a
"spider bite" to resolve on its own.

Nothing here is medical advice, and we are not qualified to give any. What we
can tell you is the entomology: if nobody saw a spider, the odds that a spider
was involved are poor, and the odds that the spider was a brown recluse in
Washington are effectively nil. That is worth saying to a clinician, and it is
worth saying before a course of treatment is chosen rather than after.

If you did catch the spider, keep it. A specimen in a jar settles in seconds
what a description argues about for weeks.
