## The window that matters

Bed bugs move by being carried. They do not fly, they do not travel far on their
own, and essentially every residential introduction arrives in something
somebody brought into the building — most often luggage, occasionally
second-hand furniture, sometimes a bag that spent a night somewhere else.

That gives you something valuable: a known moment of risk, and a short period
afterwards where an introduction is far easier to stop than it will ever be
again.

A single female arriving in a suitcase is a manageable event. The same
introduction discovered four months later, after several generations have spread
through a bedroom and into adjoining rooms, is a substantially larger and more
expensive piece of work.

So if you have just come back from somewhere and you are worried, the next few
hours are worth using well.

## What to do with the luggage tonight

The principle is simple: do not let the bags into a bedroom until they have been
dealt with.

**Choose the right place to unpack.** A garage, a utility room, a hard-floored
entryway or a bathtub. Hard surfaces are best because anything that falls off is
visible. Avoid carpet, upholstered furniture and, above all, the bed.

**Bag the laundry before it moves.** Everything washable goes straight into a
sealed bag and travels to the machine sealed, so nothing is shed on the way.

**Use the dryer.** This is the part people underuse. Sustained high heat kills
every life stage including eggs, and the dryer does more work than the wash
does. Thirty minutes on high is a reasonable benchmark for items that can take
it. Things that cannot be washed — some outerwear, shoes, soft bags — can often
still be tumbled on heat. The number behind that, since people ask: bed bugs and
their eggs die at sustained temperatures somewhere around 120 degrees
Fahrenheit, and a domestic dryer on its high setting runs comfortably past that.
A wash does not reliably, because much of the cycle runs cooler and eggs glued
into a seam sit protected inside wet fabric.

**Deal with the case itself.** Empty it completely, including every pocket and
compartment. Vacuum it thoroughly, paying attention to seams, piping, zip
channels and the wheel housings, then dispose of the vacuum contents outside in
a sealed bag. Wipe out hard-sided cases. Inspect the seams in good light with
something to magnify if you have it.

**Store the luggage away from bedrooms.** A garage, a shed or a basement rather
than a closet in the room you sleep in. Some people bag cases for storage, which
does no harm.

**Do not spray anything into your luggage.** Retail products are not effective
against bed bugs in this situation, and some of them are repellent, which can
scatter insects rather than kill them.

## What to look for over the following weeks

If something did come home with you, the evidence appears in a fairly consistent
sequence and mostly on the bed.

**Dark spotting.** Small, dark, ink-like marks on sheets, on the mattress
particularly along the piping and seams, on the bed frame and behind the
headboard. This is digested blood and it is the most reliable early sign.

**Blood smears.** Small rusty marks on sheets, usually from an insect crushed
during the night.

**Shed skins.** Bed bugs molt five times as they grow, and the discarded
exoskeletons are pale, translucent and bug-shaped. They collect in harborage
points.

**The insects themselves.** Adults are reddish brown, flat, oval and about the
size of an apple seed. After feeding they are noticeably swollen and darker.
Immatures are much smaller and paler, and newly hatched ones are close to
translucent.

**Eggs.** About a millimeter, pearly white, glued in clusters into cracks and
seams. Difficult to see without looking specifically for them.

**Bites.** Usually on skin exposed during sleep, often in a line or a loose
cluster, appearing overnight. But bites are the least reliable evidence on this
list, because human reactions vary enormously — some people develop obvious welts
and some develop nothing at all, and plenty of other things bite.

## Where to look

Bed bugs stay close to where people sleep, and their harborage preferences are
narrow and predictable.

Start with the mattress: all seams, the piping, the label, and both sides. Then
the box spring, which is a far better harborage than the mattress and is where
a great deal gets missed — the underside, the fabric edges and the corner
protectors particularly.

Then the frame and headboard. Joints, bolt holes, slats, the back of a headboard
and anywhere two pieces of timber meet. An upholstered headboard is a
first-class harborage.

Then the immediate surroundings: the bedside table, especially the underside and
the drawer runners; the edge of carpet where it meets the wall; loose baseboard;
behind a picture or a mirror above the bed; and the seams of any upholstered
chair in the room.

The rule of thumb is that they will be within a few feet of a sleeping person,
in a crack, in the dark, and near a rough or fabric surface rather than a smooth
one.

## What the timeline looks like

Patience is required here, and it is worth knowing why.

A single fertilized female arriving in a bag will begin laying within a few
days. Eggs hatch in roughly one to two weeks depending on temperature. The
nymphs need several blood meals over several weeks to reach adulthood.

That means an introduction is genuinely undetectable for a while, then produces
very sparse signs, and only becomes obvious once the population has been through
a couple of generations. Two or three months from arrival to noticeable is
entirely normal.

The practical implications: a clean check in week one is good news but not proof;
it is worth continuing to look for a couple of months after a high-risk trip;
and finding something early, in one room, is a much better position than most
people who call us are in.

## Things that get mistaken for them

A good share of the calls we take after a trip turn out to be something else,
and it is worth knowing the usual candidates before anybody panics.

**Carpet beetle larvae.** Small, elongated, bristly and often golden brown, found
in carpet edges, closets and around baseboards. They can also cause a skin
reaction in some people that is genuinely mistaken for bites. Very common and
completely different in significance.

**Spider beetles.** Small, rounded, reddish brown and shiny, and at a glance a
plausible bed bug. They are a stored-product insect rather than a blood feeder.

**Bat bugs.** Closely related and very similar in appearance, but associated with
a roost above rather than with travel. Rare, and it matters because the source
is entirely different.

**Fleas.** Bites are usually around ankles and lower legs rather than on
sleeping-exposed skin, and the insect jumps, which a bed bug cannot do.

**Booklice and psocids.** Tiny, pale, associated with damp rather than with beds.
Harmless.

**Skin conditions and other causes.** Contact dermatitis, hives, dry skin
reactions and any number of other things produce marks that look like bites. We
are not doctors and we will not diagnose, but we will tell you plainly when we
have searched thoroughly and found no insect evidence at all, because that is
useful information to take to a doctor.

The reason identification matters so much here is cost. Bed bug work is
expensive relative to most pest jobs, and treating a carpet beetle problem as a
bed bug problem is an expensive error in both directions.

## If nothing is found

This is a common and legitimate outcome, and it is worth saying what it means.

It means no evidence of activity was found at the time of the search. Given how
early an introduction can be, and how sparse the signs are in the first weeks,
it is strong reassurance rather than a guarantee.

What we would suggest in that situation is straightforward: keep looking for a
few weeks, use a light-colored mattress protector so spotting is easy to see,
keep the luggage stored away from bedrooms, and call again if anything changes.

We would not recommend a precautionary treatment, and we will not sell you one.

## If something is found

Then the good news is that you have found it early, and early is worth a great
deal in this particular problem.

The work is thorough rather than quick. Bed bug treatment involves careful
preparation, treatment of harborage rather than surfaces, and follow-up visits
timed against the egg-hatch cycle, because eggs are the hardest stage to affect
and a single-visit treatment will not deal with them.

Encasements on mattress and box spring are usually part of it — they trap
anything inside and make ongoing monitoring far simpler.

Laundry and heat treatment of textiles runs alongside.

And an honest scope: which rooms are involved, and whether adjacent rooms need
including. In a house, an early single-room introduction usually stays a
single-room job. In an apartment or a shared building, adjacent units are a real
consideration and we will say so.

## Reducing the risk next time

None of this requires traveling anxiously, and a couple of habits do most of
the work.

On arrival at a hotel or rental, put the bags in the bathroom — hard surfaces,
no harborage — before anything else. Then pull back the bedding and look at the
mattress seams and the headboard for two minutes. Dark spotting is what you are
looking for.

Use the luggage rack rather than the bed or the floor, and pull it away from the
wall.

Keep dirty laundry bagged during the trip rather than loose in the case.

On the way home, if you can, unpack in a garage or on a hard floor rather than
in a bedroom, and run the laundry through a hot dryer.

That is genuinely the whole protocol, and it takes about five minutes across a
trip.

## What not to do while you are worrying

The instinct to act decisively is strong here, and several of the obvious
actions make the situation measurably worse.

**Do not start sleeping in a different room.** This is the single most
counterproductive thing people do. Bed bugs orient toward where a person sleeps,
and moving to the sofa or a spare room spreads an insect that was confined to
one bed into two or three rooms. If you have an introduction, staying in your
own bed keeps it contained until it is treated.

**Do not throw out the mattress.** It is expensive, it rarely resolves anything
because the population is not confined to the mattress, and carrying an infested
item through a house or a shared hallway distributes insects along the route. If
a mattress genuinely has to go, it gets encased or wrapped first.

**Do not bug-bomb or fog.** Aerosol foggers are close to useless against bed
bugs, which live in cracks the product never reaches, and the repellent effect
scatters them further into a structure. In some cases people have turned a
one-room problem into a whole-floor problem this way.

**Do not spray retail insecticides around bedding.** Same reasoning, plus the
obvious concerns about treating a surface you sleep on.

**Do not use alcohol, kerosene or any flammable product on furniture.** People
have set fire to their homes doing this. It is not a hypothetical.

**Do not empty the room into other rooms.** Moving clothing, bedding and
belongings out in an unbagged panic is precisely how a contained problem
travels. If preparation is needed it should be done in a planned way, bagged, at
the point of treatment.

The general rule is that almost every drastic action available to a worried
householder spreads the problem, and almost none of them kills it.

## Second-hand furniture, briefly

Since it is the other main route in: any upholstered item, mattress, bed frame or
piece of soft furniture acquired second-hand deserves a proper look before it
comes into the house. Seams, joints, undersides, bolt holes and the underside of
drawers.

Free furniture on a curb is the highest-risk category there is, for reasons
worth thinking about — a fair proportion of it is on the curb precisely because
of this problem.

## Monitoring while you wait

If you want more than a visual check over the following weeks, there are two
things worth doing and one worth skipping.

**Light-colored mattress encasements** are the most useful single purchase. They
zip over the mattress and box spring, they trap anything already inside, they
remove the seams and piping that make those items such good harborage, and they
give you a plain pale surface where dark spotting is immediately obvious. They
also make a later treatment simpler and cheaper.

**Interceptor cups** under the bed legs are the other. They are shallow plastic
traps that an insect climbing toward the bed falls into and cannot climb out of.
They cost very little, they are entirely passive, and they turn a vague worry
into a definite answer over a few weeks. If you are going to monitor at all,
these are how.

Move the bed away from walls and keep bedding off the floor while monitoring, so
the legs are the only route up. That is what makes the interceptors work.

**Skip the retail detectors and the pheromone lures.** Performance varies
enormously, and a negative result from an unreliable device is worse than no
device at all because it produces false confidence.

## A word about how this feels

We deal with this problem often enough to say something that is not really about
insects.

People find bed bugs uniquely distressing in a way they do not find rodents or
wasps, and the reason is obvious: it involves the bed, the night, and the sense
that somewhere private has been intruded upon. Sleep suffers. People check
constantly, get up in the night with a flashlight, and lose weeks to it.

Two things are worth holding onto. First, this has nothing to do with
cleanliness, housekeeping or how anybody lives. Bed bugs are moved by travel and
they turn up in immaculate homes and expensive hotels as readily as anywhere
else. Second, it is a solvable problem with a known method, and the households we
treat come out the other side of it.

If you are somewhere between a worry and a certainty, getting a definite answer
is worth a great deal on its own, whichever way it comes back.
