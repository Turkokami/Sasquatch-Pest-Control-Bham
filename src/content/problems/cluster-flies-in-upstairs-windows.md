## What they are

The flies at your upstairs windows in October, and again on the first mild day
in February, are almost certainly cluster flies, and they are a completely
different proposition from the flies most people picture.

They are slightly larger than a house fly, dark gray rather than shiny, with a
covering of short golden hairs on the thorax that is visible up close. They are
noticeably sluggish — they move slowly, they are easy to catch, and they bump
against glass rather than flying purposefully.

Their life cycle has nothing to do with buildings. The larvae develop in soil as
parasites of earthworms, which means the population is generated by lawns,
pasture, field margins and any ground with a healthy earthworm community. A
property surrounded by grass and farmland is a property surrounded by cluster
fly habitat, and there is nothing to be done about that.

Adults emerge, feed on nectar and other plant material through the summer, and
then, as the season turns, look for somewhere to spend the winter.

That is where your house comes in.

## Why your house

Cluster flies overwinter in aggregations — the name is literal — and they select
sites by warmth and exposure.

In late September and October, on warm afternoons, they gather on the sunniest
walls of a building. In this hemisphere that means south and west elevations,
and the effect is strongest on light-colored walls that hold heat.

Having gathered, they work into any gap they can find and settle into the void
behind it. Attics, wall cavities, soffits, the space behind fascia, around window
frames, and the enclosed structure of an older roof are all used.

There they become dormant, in large numbers, and they wait.

The buildings that get this worst share a profile: tall, with large unshaded
south or west elevations, surrounded by lawn or open ground, and old enough or
complex enough to have plenty of small openings at height. Farmhouses,
substantial rural properties and buildings on the edge of open country are the
classic cases. It has nothing whatever to do with how a house is kept.

## Why they keep reappearing

This is the part that frustrates people, and the mechanism is simple once
explained.

The flies in your wall are dormant, not dead. Dormancy in insects is temperature
driven, and it is reversible. A run of mild weather, a sunny day warming a south
wall, or a heating system warming the structure from inside, will rouse some
proportion of them.

Once roused, they move — and their orientation behavior takes them toward light.
In an attic or a wall void, the light is a window. So they emerge into the room
through whatever gap connects the void to your living space: the edge of a
window frame, a ceiling light fitting, an unsealed attic hatch, a bathroom fan,
or the top of an interior wall.

Then they sit at the glass, sluggish and slow, and that is what you find.

Every warm spell produces another batch. From the inside it looks like an
ongoing infestation. In fact it is the same overwintering population being
periodically woken up, and it will continue until spring, when the survivors
leave to lay eggs in the soil.

## Telling them from the flies that matter

Two other flies get confused with these and one of them is worth acting on
urgently.

**Blow flies and bottle flies.** Shiny metallic blue or green, fast, and active
rather than sluggish. These develop in carrion. A sudden appearance of numbers of
metallic flies indoors, particularly in one room, frequently means something has
died in a wall cavity, an attic, a chimney or beneath a floor — often a rodent
that took bait somewhere it could not be retrieved. That is a real finding with a
real cause and it is worth investigating rather than swatting.

**House flies.** Smaller, grayer, active, and associated with waste, food and
organic matter. A sanitation and exclusion question rather than an overwintering
one.

**Fungus gnats.** Much smaller, delicate, and associated with overwatered
houseplants rather than with walls.

The practical distinctions: cluster flies are slow, dull-colored, appear at
windows in numbers, and are seasonal. Blow flies are shiny, fast, and mean
something else. Speed and shine are the two things to look at.

## Why timing decides everything

There is one honest thing to say about cluster fly work and it is about the
calendar.

The effective intervention is an exterior treatment applied to the walls they
gather on, before they enter — late August into early September, ahead of the
first cool nights that trigger the move. Applied then, it reduces the population
that gets in, sometimes dramatically.

The same treatment applied in mid-October is largely wasted, because the flies
are already inside the wall.

And once they are in the void, there is very little that reaches them. Products
applied into a wall cavity in winter have poor access to a dormant insect wedged
in a gap, and treating a void full of flies creates its own problem, which we
come to below.

So the shape of a cluster fly plan is: seal what you can now, whatever the
season, and put the exterior treatment in the diary for the end of next summer.
It is not the answer people want in November and it is the true one.

## Sealing, and what to seal

Exclusion is the durable half of this and it is worth doing regardless of when
you start.

**Around windows**, particularly upstairs ones on the sunny elevations. Gaps
where the frame meets the siding, failed sealant, and the trim returns.

**Soffit and fascia junctions**, which on most buildings have gaps somewhere
along their length and which lead directly into a roof space.

**Gable and roof vents**, where screening is missing, damaged or too coarse.

**Where an addition's roof meets a wall**, a detail that is rarely airtight.

**Utility penetrations at height** — service entries, satellite brackets,
plumbing vents, heat pump line sets.

**Under and around loose siding**, particularly the open ends of vinyl siding
sections and the J-channel.

**Chimney flashing and the gap around a flue.**

And on the inside, the openings that let them out of the void and into your
rooms: the edges of window frames, recessed light housings, bathroom and kitchen
fan surrounds, the attic hatch, and the gaps around plumbing and wiring where
they pass through ceilings.

Sealing the interior openings will not stop them entering the building, but it
does stop them entering the room, which for most households is the part that
actually matters.

## The other overwintering insects

Cluster flies rarely arrive alone. The same walls, the same weeks and the same
openings serve a small group of insects that all do the same thing, and a
building with one usually has several.

**Stink bugs.** Shield-shaped, brown or green, congregating on sunlit walls in
October and using the identical gaps. The brown marmorated species is the one
that has become widespread, and it produces the characteristic odor when
disturbed or vacuumed.

**Boxelder bugs.** Black with red or orange markings, gathering in large numbers
on warm walls, particularly where there are maples or boxelders nearby.

**Ladybird beetles.** Some species aggregate in buildings for the winter in
considerable numbers, often in attics and wall voids, and emerge on warm days
exactly like the flies do.

**Western conifer seed bugs.** Large, narrow, brown, with flattened leaf-like
hind legs. Alarming to find and completely harmless.

**Paper wasp and yellowjacket queens.** Mated queens overwintering individually
in sheltered spots, sometimes in attics and lofts. A single wasp in an upstairs
room in March is usually one of these rather than a nest.

The reason this matters is efficiency. The exclusion work is identical for all of
them — the same soffit gaps, the same window surrounds, the same vents at height
— and the exterior treatment window is the same too. Doing it once addresses the
whole group, which changes the economics of the job considerably.

If you have flies at the windows in October and stink bugs on the wall outside,
those are not two problems. They are one building detail.

## The dead fly problem

This is the part that gets overlooked and it produces a second wave of calls in
spring.

A substantial proportion of the overwintering population dies in the void. In a
building that has had this for years, that means an accumulation of dead flies
in an attic, a soffit or a wall cavity — sometimes a considerable one.

Dead insects are food. Carpet beetles and larder beetles feed on them, breed in
the accumulation, and then disperse. Their larvae end up in the rooms below,
where they damage wool, textiles and stored goods, and where the shed bristly
hairs of carpet beetle larvae cause skin irritation in some people that is
readily mistaken for insect bites.

So clearing the accumulation is part of doing this properly rather than an
optional extra. Where an attic is accessible, that means vacuuming out the
material and disposing of it. Where the accumulation is in a closed wall void, it
may not be reachable, which is one of the arguments for stopping them getting in
rather than treating them once they are there.

## What to do about the ones in the room now

Practical, immediate, and unglamorous.

**Vacuum them.** It is the most effective thing available to a householder. They
are slow and easy to collect. Empty the canister or bag outside into a sealed
bag afterwards, because they are not necessarily dead.

**Do not spray the window.** Aerosol at a window kills the ones you can see and
does nothing about the several hundred in the wall. It also leaves residue where
you least want it and adds to the accumulation of dead insects.

**A light trap** in an attic or an unused room catches a proportion of them
through the winter, since they orient to light. Worth it in a badly affected
building.

**Keep the interior openings closed** — the attic hatch, gaps at light fittings,
the fan surrounds — so that the ones that rouse stay in the void.

**Do not fog or bug-bomb.** Ineffective against insects in a cavity, and it
distributes product through living space for no benefit.

## What a realistic outcome looks like

We would rather set expectations than oversell this.

A property that gets cluster flies will continue to be a property that gets
cluster flies, because the source is the ground around it and neither of us can
change that. What a good job achieves is a large reduction: fewer getting in,
fewer emerging into rooms, and an attic that is not accumulating dead insects.

In a well-sealed building with a properly timed exterior treatment, most
households find the difference substantial and go from a daily nuisance to an
occasional one.

In an older complex building with a great many openings at height, the honest
answer is that complete exclusion is not achievable and the aim is management.
We will say that at the start rather than after two seasons of disappointment.

What we will not do is sell you a monthly interior treatment through the winter,
because it does not work on insects dormant in a wall.

## The rural and farm situation

A large share of the worst cluster fly buildings in this county are on acreage,
and there are a few things worth saying about that.

Pasture is ideal breeding ground. Earthworm populations in permanent grass are
high, which means the fly population generated around a farmhouse or a rural
property is much larger than around a house in town. That is not a criticism of
how the land is managed — healthy soil with plenty of earthworms is what you
want, and it comes with this.

Farm buildings compound it. Barns, equipment sheds, unheated outbuildings and
old structures with plenty of gaps all serve as overwintering sites, and they
sit close to the house. Sealing a house standing among five other buildings
reduces what gets into the house rather than what is on the property.

The buildings themselves are usually the hard kind: tall, complex rooflines,
older construction, large south and west elevations, and a great many openings
at height. Full exclusion is often not realistic and management is the honest
goal.

What works best on these properties is a well-timed exterior treatment on the
elevations that matter, sealing concentrated on the house rather than every
structure, and interior sealing so that what does get into the roof space stays
out of the rooms. That combination is affordable and it makes a real difference.
Chasing complete elimination across a working property is not.

## Windows themselves, and why upstairs

Since the complaint is nearly always about upstairs windows specifically, it is
worth explaining the geometry.

The flies enter high — at soffits, at roof lines, at upper-story window
surrounds — because that is where the warm gathering surfaces and the accessible
gaps coincide. So the void they occupy is an attic or an upper wall cavity.

When they rouse and move toward light, the nearest light is an upper window. They
travel a short distance through the void and emerge at the frame, which is why
the same two or three windows in a house produce almost all of them.

Older windows make this worse in two ways. The frame-to-wall junction on
traditional construction usually has a gap somewhere, and the frames themselves
are frequently in a wall assembly that communicates with the roof space. Sealing
around those specific frames, from the inside, is a small job with a
disproportionate effect on what you actually experience.

Curtains and blinds change the pattern too. A room kept dark produces fewer
emergences than one with a bright window, which occasionally offers a simple
mitigation in a spare bedroom nobody uses.

## What we will not sell you

**A winter interior treatment.** Product applied inside a house in January does
not reach insects dormant in a cavity, and it puts residue where people live for
no return.

**A monthly plan through the overwintering season.** The population is fixed once
they are in. Repeat visits do not reduce it.

**Fogging.** Ineffective against insects in voids and unpleasant in occupied
rooms.

**A promise of elimination.** The source is the ground around your building. We
can reduce what gets in substantially. We cannot make the flies stop existing.

**An attic cleanout sold as urgent when it is not.** Where accumulation is light
it can wait for the sealing work. Where it is heavy it genuinely should be dealt
with, and we will show you which you have rather than describing it.

## A note on chimneys and unused flues

One structural feature deserves a mention because it produces the most dramatic
version of this problem.

An open or poorly capped chimney is a large, warm, sheltered vertical void
running the height of a building, and overwintering insects find it readily. In
houses where a fireplace has been closed off but the flue left open at the top,
we sometimes find substantial accumulations, and the flies emerge into the room
through gaps around a blanked-off opening or a fitted stove.

Capping an unused flue properly — with a cowl that excludes insects and birds
while still allowing the ventilation the structure needs — resolves it, and it
is worth doing for several other reasons besides.

The same applies to disused vents, old extract ducts left in place after an
appliance was removed, and any pipe or duct that terminates in open air with
nothing on the end of it. These are easy to overlook precisely because nothing
uses them any more.
