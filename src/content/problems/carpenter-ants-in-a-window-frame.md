## Why it is the window

Carpenter ants around a window frame is one of the most frequent calls we take
in this county, and the reason is not really about ants at all. It is about
water.

Carpenter ants do not attack sound, dry timber by preference. They excavate
galleries in wood that has already begun to soften — wood that has been damp
long enough for decay to start breaking down its structure and make it workable.
Sound Douglas fir is hard going. Timber that has been wet for three winters is
not.

A window is the place in a wall assembly most likely to be in that condition.
It is a hole cut through the weather envelope, closed with a system of sealant,
flashing, glazing and trim, all of which have finite lives. When any part of it
fails, the water goes into the framing and stays there, because the same
construction that keeps rain out also keeps the wood from drying.

So the ants in your window frame are a symptom with two causes stacked on top of
each other. There is an insect problem and there is a building problem, and
solving only the first one is why this recurs for people.

## What you are actually seeing

The signs cluster in a few recognizable forms.

**Workers on the sill or the trim**, particularly in the evening. Carpenter ants
are largely nocturnal, so activity in the hour after dusk is more meaningful
than activity at noon. Seeing several in a short period in the same place is
more significant than seeing one.

**Frass** — the debris pushed out of the galleries. This is the most diagnostic
sign and it is often mistaken for ordinary dust or sawdust. Carpenter ant frass
is a coarse, fibrous material, more like shredded wood fiber than fine powder,
and it usually contains insect fragments: discarded legs, wing pieces and body
parts from the colony. It accumulates in a small conical pile below the opening
they are using, on a sill, a windowsill ledge, a floor beneath, or the top of a
radiator cover.

**A small round or slotted opening** in trim or in the frame itself, often at a
joint. These are the slits they push debris out through.

**Winged ants in spring**, which are reproductives leaving an established
colony. A swarm indoors is a strong indication that a mature nest is inside the
structure rather than outside it.

**A faint dry rustling** from inside the frame or the wall beside it on a warm
evening. People often think they are imagining this. Frequently they are not.

**Damaged, spongy or discolored wood** at the bottom corners of the frame and
along the sill — the water damage that made the site attractive in the first
place.

## Satellite nests and the parent colony

This is the concept that determines whether the work holds, so it is worth
setting out carefully.

A mature carpenter ant population is not one nest. It is a parent colony
containing the queen and the primary brood, plus a number of satellite nests
containing workers, older larvae and pupae, connected by foraging trails and
exchanging material continuously.

The parent colony is almost always outdoors, in wood with high moisture content:
a stump, a buried root, a dead limb in an otherwise healthy tree, a fallen log,
a landscape timber, an old fence post at ground level, or a woodpile that has
been in the same place for years.

The satellites are what colonize a building. They tolerate drier conditions than
the parent nest, they can be established quickly, and a window frame with soft
wood in it is an ideal site.

The practical consequence is straightforward. Treating the satellite in your
window kills what is in the window. If the parent colony is still sitting in a
stump forty feet away, it will re-establish, often within the same season, and
often in the same frame because the conditions that made it attractive have not
changed.

That is why a serious carpenter ant visit spends most of its time outside.

## Finding the parent colony

On a modest city lot this is usually quick. There are a limited number of
candidates and they are all within sight of the house.

We look at stumps, particularly old ones that are well into decay. Standing
deadwood and dead limbs in living trees. Fallen timber left in a corner of a
garden. Landscape ties and retaining structures, which are frequently in contact
with soil and rotting from the bottom. Fence posts at ground level. Deck framing,
especially the ledger and the joist ends. Firewood, and the ground beneath where
firewood used to be. Buried construction debris, which is more common than
people expect on lots that were developed in stages.

We also follow trails. Carpenter ants forage along consistent routes and will
use edges — a fence line, a foundation edge, a utility line, a branch touching
the building. Following a trail in the evening, when they are actually moving,
is often the fastest route to the nest.

On a large wooded property this becomes genuinely difficult and we say so
honestly rather than pretending. A parent colony can be a hundred feet into the
trees, and searching properly is real work rather than a lap of the house.

## The moisture side of the job

You can treat the ants perfectly and still be back here in three years if the
window stays wet, so the building work matters.

Typical causes at a window: sealant that has hardened, cracked or pulled away
from the frame; flashing above the opening that was never installed or has
lifted; a sill with insufficient slope, or one that has settled flat; a missing
drip edge, letting water run back under the sill and into the framing; failed
glazing seals; and paint or finish that has broken down and is holding water
against the wood rather than shedding it.

Less obviously: gutters overflowing above the window, downspouts discharging
against the wall, sprinkler heads hitting the siding, and vegetation growing
tight against the wall so the wall never dries.

We are not joiners and we do not pretend to be. What we do is tell you exactly
where the water is getting in and what has been compromised, so that whoever
does the repair is fixing the right thing. Sometimes it is a tube of sealant and
a drip edge. Sometimes a section of sill and trim needs cutting out and
replacing. Occasionally the framing behind is gone and it is a bigger
conversation.

## What the treatment actually involves

Done properly, the work has several parts and none of them is a spray on a
windowsill.

**Direct treatment of the satellite nest**, where we can locate the void it
occupies — typically through the existing openings, using a dust or foam that
distributes through the gallery system rather than sitting on a surface.

**Non-repellent product on the trails and at the structural interface.**
Repellent products cause a colony to split and relocate, which makes the problem
larger and harder to find. Non-repellents allow transfer between workers and
back toward the nest, which is what you want.

**Baiting where it fits.** Carpenter ants change dietary preference through the
season, which makes bait less reliable than it is for some other species, but in
the right conditions it is an effective way to reach a nest you cannot see.

**Treatment of the parent colony**, once located. This is the piece that
produces a durable result.

**Perimeter and harborage work.** Removing or relocating the woodpile, grinding
or removing stumps close to the building, clearing fallen timber, cutting
branches that bridge to the roof or wall, and correcting the ground contact of
landscape timber.

**The moisture correction**, which is usually somebody else's trade but is part
of the plan.

## Telling them apart from the alternatives

Two things get confused with carpenter ants and both change the answer.

**Moisture ants.** Smaller, usually yellowish, and associated with wood that is
not merely damp but properly wet and decaying. Where they turn up, the finding
is nearly always a water problem of some seriousness — a persistent leak, a
saturated sill, a rotting section of framing. They are less structurally
aggressive than carpenter ants and considerably more informative about the
building.

**Termites.** Dampwood termites are present in this region and they do consume
wood. The distinguishing signs are different: termite damage is packed with mud
and debris rather than swept clean, the galleries look rough rather than
sanded-smooth, and swarmers have four equal-length wings and a straight-sided
body, where carpenter ant swarmers have a pinched waist, elbowed antennae and
forewings noticeably longer than the hind pair.

We separate these on site, because the treatment differs and so does the
seriousness. If what you have is a wood-destroying organism that somebody else's
report identified, that is squarely within what we are licensed to treat, and we
will handle the treatment side.

## What people try first

Nearly everybody does something before calling, and some of it actively makes
the job harder.

**An aerosol from the hardware store, sprayed at the window.** This kills the
ants you can see. It is also usually a repellent formulation, which is the
specific thing you do not want: a repellent applied at a nest site frequently
causes budding, where the colony splits and relocates into two or three new
sites you now have to find. People who have sprayed for a couple of seasons
often present with a more distributed problem than they started with.

**Sealing the opening the frass is coming out of.** Understandable and
counterproductive. The opening is the most useful piece of information on the
building — it tells us exactly where the gallery is. Closing it does not close
the gallery; it just means the debris comes out somewhere less visible.

**Grocery-store ant bait stations.** These are formulated for sugar-feeding
household ants. Carpenter ants shift between protein and carbohydrate through
the season, and the small liquid stations are frequently ignored entirely. Not
harmful, rarely useful.

**Diatomaceous earth along a sill.** It has a genuine mechanism and it works
slowly on insects that walk through it repeatedly. It does nothing to a nest
inside a frame and it is useless once damp, which in a window context it will
be.

**Cutting the trim open to look.** Occasionally this reveals the gallery and is
informative. More often it makes a repair job out of a treatment job, and it
should wait until somebody has decided whether the frame is being repaired
anyway.

If you have already done any of this, say so when you call. It changes what we
expect to find and it is not something to be embarrassed about.

## The species you actually have

Two carpenter ants account for most of what we see in Whatcom County, and
knowing which one you have shifts where we look.

The larger and more familiar is the western black carpenter ant — big, matte
black, with major workers that are genuinely striking in size. This is the one
associated with parent colonies in stumps, logs and standing deadwood in
conifer country, and it is the species behind most window and wall complaints in
wooded parts of the county.

The second is a smaller, two-toned species with a reddish or brownish thorax
against a darker body. It is more of a ground nester, more likely to have a
parent colony under a rock, a slab, a landscape timber or a section of debris on
the soil, and it turns up more often on open lots than in the trees.

Both build satellite nests in buildings and both respond to the same approach.
The difference is search strategy: for the black species we work the trees and
the deadwood, and for the two-toned one we work the ground, the hardscape and
anything sitting on soil.

Size alone is a reasonable first cut, and workers within one colony vary
considerably in size — a point worth knowing, because people frequently believe
they have two different ants when they have one colony with a normal range of
worker sizes.

## Windows differ by building age

The buildings in this county fall into a few groups and each fails in its own
way.

**Pre-war and early post-war wood windows.** Single glazed, painted softwood,
often with the original sills. These have had a long time to take on water and
the sill and lower rails are typically where it shows. The wood is usually good
material and often worth repairing rather than replacing.

**Mid-century and 1970s stock.** Frequently aluminum frames in wood surrounds,
with heavy condensation on the metal in winter. That condensation runs down and
soaks the timber below, which produces exactly the softened sill carpenter ants
want, without any external leak at all. It surprises people, because the water
came from inside.

**Replacement windows fitted into old openings.** A very common situation and a
frequent source of trouble. Where a replacement unit was installed without
correcting the flashing and the sill detail behind it, the new window looks
sound while the original framing continues to get wet. We find this often, and
it is worth knowing before somebody spends money replacing a window that was not
the problem.

**Newer construction.** Better detailed generally, but with its own failure
mode where housewrap, flashing tape and sealant meet at the head and jambs. When
those fail, the water is inside a well-sealed assembly with no way to dry, which
can be worse than an old leaky wall that breathes.

## What we will not sell you

**A spray-and-go visit.** It treats what you can see and leaves the colony.

**A recurring plan over an unrepaired window.** Wet timber will keep producing
the same result, and a quarterly visit around it is a subscription.

**A whole-house treatment for one frame** when the finding is one satellite and
one water leak.

**A guarantee we cannot support.** Where the parent colony is on a neighboring
property or deep in woodland we cannot reach, we will tell you we are managing
the building rather than eliminating the source, and price it accordingly.

## When to act

Carpenter ant activity is most visible from spring through late summer, with a
noticeable peak as the weather warms and again in the swarm period. A single ant
in February in a heated house is more significant than a single ant in July,
because it suggests something is overwintering inside rather than wandering in.

The best time to deal with a window is when the frame is dry enough to assess
properly and repairs are practical, which in this county means the drier part of
the year. The worst time is to leave it for another winter and let the same wet
timber take on another season of water.
