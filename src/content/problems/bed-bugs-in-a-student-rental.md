## Two things make this different

Bed bugs behave the same way everywhere. What changes in a student rental is the
context around them, and it changes the outcome more than the biology does.

The first is the building. Most student housing in Bellingham is shared in some
form — a house with four or five bedrooms, a duplex, a converted older property,
or an apartment in a block. Bed bugs do not respect the boundary between one
bedroom and another, and they certainly do not respect the boundary between one
tenancy and the next. A population in one room has routes into the next along
baseboards, under doors, through the gaps around plumbing and wiring, and inside
shared wall cavities.

The second is that nobody clearly owns the problem. In an owner-occupied house,
one person finds it, decides, and pays. In a rental with several tenants and a
landlord, there is a genuine question about who arranges the work, who pays for
it, and who has to do the preparation, and that question routinely takes longer
to resolve than the treatment does.

Both of those need addressing, and the second one is usually the harder.

## Why September

Bellingham has a pronounced bed bug season and it is not driven by weather.

Move-in concentrates every risk factor into a few weeks. People arrive from all
over, with luggage that has been in a lot of buildings. Furniture changes hands —
mattresses, couches, bed frames — a substantial share of it second-hand, some of
it collected from a curb. Storage units are emptied. Belongings that spent the
summer somewhere else come back.

Move-out in June does the same thing in reverse, and it is when the furniture
that will be picked up in September goes onto the curb in the first place.

If you are furnishing a room cheaply, the single most useful habit is to treat
any upholstered item, mattress or bed frame as suspect until you have looked at
it properly. Seams, piping, joints, bolt holes, the underside of the frame, the
back of a headboard, and the underside of drawers. Free furniture on a curb is
the highest-risk category there is, and some of it is there for exactly this
reason.

## Confirming it before anybody argues about it

In shared housing there is real value in establishing whether this is actually
bed bugs before the conversation about money starts, because a large share of
suspected cases are something else.

The evidence to look for is the same as anywhere: small dark ink-like spotting
on sheets and along mattress seams and piping, rusty blood smears, pale shed
skins collecting in cracks, and the insects themselves — reddish brown, flat,
oval, roughly apple-seed sized as adults, much smaller and paler when immature.

Bites are the least reliable indicator. Reactions differ enormously between
people, which in a shared house produces a genuinely confusing situation where
one housemate has obvious welts and another sleeping ten feet away has nothing
at all. That is normal and it is not evidence that only one room is affected.

The things most often mistaken for them in this housing stock are carpet beetle
larvae, spider beetles, and skin reactions with a non-insect cause. Getting that
wrong in either direction is expensive.

If you find something, photograph it and keep the specimen in a sealed bag or a
piece of clear tape. A physical sample ends the argument faster than a
description.

## Treating one room in a shared house

We are asked this constantly and the honest answer is usually no.

Bed bugs disperse along the path of least resistance from a food source, which
means along the wall line, under a door, through the gap at a floor edge, and
through the openings around pipes and cabling. Bedrooms in a shared house are
frequently separated by a single wall with all of those features.

Treating one bedroom while the adjacent one is untreated means the treated room
gets recolonized. You have paid for a piece of work whose result is determined
by whether the person next door happened to be affected too.

What we recommend instead is a scope that follows the building rather than the
tenancy: the room with confirmed activity, the rooms sharing a wall with it,
shared living space with upholstered furniture, and whatever else the search
turns up. That is a bigger number on the first quote and a smaller number over
the year.

In an apartment block the same logic extends to the units above, below and
beside, which is a building manager's decision rather than a tenant's.

## The responsibility question

We are a pest control company and not attorneys, so we will describe how these
situations usually work rather than tell you what your rights are. For an actual
determination on a lease or a dispute, Washington has tenant advice services and
the university has housing resources, and either is a better source than us.

What we can say from doing the work:

Treatment arranged and paid for at the building level almost always produces a
better result than treatment arranged room by room, because scope follows the
insect rather than the tenancy.

Landlords who deal with it quickly spend less. A single-room introduction found
in October and treated properly is a fraction of the cost of a building-wide
population in February.

Disputes about who pays are the main reason these situations run for months.
During that time the population grows and spreads, and the eventual bill is
larger than the one being argued over.

Documentation helps everybody. Date what was found, photograph it, keep written
records of when the landlord was notified and what was said. That is useful
whether the eventual conversation is cooperative or not.

We are happy to provide a written account of what we found and what scope we
recommend, which tenants can pass to a landlord and landlords can use to
coordinate. That is often the most useful thing we do on the first visit.

## What housemates actually have to do

Preparation is where shared housing treatments fail, and it fails because it
only takes one person not doing it.

**Everybody keeps sleeping in their own bed.** This is the most important
instruction and the one most often ignored. Moving to a couch or a friend's
room spreads an insect that was confined to one bed into new rooms. If you have
bed bugs, staying put until treatment keeps them where they are.

**Laundry, done properly and all at once.** Bedding, clothing and soft items
through a hot dryer for a sustained cycle. The dryer does the work; the wash is
secondary. Clean items go into sealed bags and stay sealed until after
treatment, otherwise they are simply re-exposed.

**Clutter reduced, not redistributed.** Bagged and consolidated, not carried
into another room. Every bag moved unsealed through a house is a potential
transfer.

**Beds pulled away from walls, bedding off the floor.**

**Access on the day, and on the follow-up days.** Bed bug treatment is not a
single visit. Eggs are the hardest stage to affect and follow-up visits are
timed against the hatch cycle. A housemate who is unavailable for the second
visit compromises the whole job.

**Nothing sprayed by anybody.** Retail products and foggers scatter populations
and undo professional treatment. If one person in a house decides to handle
their own room with a can from the hardware store, everybody pays for it.

It is worth having this conversation as a household early and plainly, because
the alternative — half the house preparing properly and half not — produces a
result nobody is happy with.

## What you can do cheaply that genuinely helps

On a student budget, three things are worth the money.

**Mattress and box spring encasements.** Light colored ones. They trap what is
inside, remove the best harborage in the room, and give you a plain surface
where spotting shows immediately. They also make treatment simpler.

**Interceptor cups under the bed legs.** Passive traps that catch anything
climbing toward the bed. Cheap, and they turn suspicion into a definite answer
over a few weeks. Move the bed off the wall and keep bedding off the floor so the
legs are the only route.

**Hot laundering.** Free if you already pay for laundry, and genuinely effective
on textiles.

What is not worth the money: foggers, retail sprays, ultrasonic devices,
diatomaceous earth scattered around a bed, and rubbing alcohol, which is both
ineffective at any realistic dose and a serious fire risk in a bedroom. People
have started fires doing this.

Throwing out a mattress is also rarely the right spend. It is expensive, the
population is not confined to the mattress, and carrying it through a shared
hallway seeds the route it travels.

## What treatment actually looks like

For a shared house, the shape is fairly consistent.

A thorough search first, room by room, to establish the actual extent rather
than the reported extent. This is what the verification fee pays for and it
routinely changes the scope in both directions.

Preparation by the occupants, coordinated so it happens across the house at
once.

Treatment focused on harborage — seams, joints, voids, frames, the underside of
furniture, the edges of a room — rather than on open surfaces. Bed bugs live in
cracks and that is where the work is.

Encasements on beds in affected rooms.

Follow-up visits timed against the egg cycle. Anybody offering a single-visit
guarantee on bed bugs is either using a method that justifies it or
overpromising, and it is worth asking which.

Monitoring afterwards, usually with interceptors, so a residual population shows
up early rather than in three months.

The whole process typically runs across several weeks. That is normal and it is
not a sign anything is going wrong.

## Moving out with an infestation

This comes up every June and it deserves a direct answer, because the wrong
handling turns one household's problem into three.

If your unit has, or recently had, bed bugs and you are moving, the risk is that
you take them with you into the next place. Every item in a bag is a potential
vehicle, and a new tenancy is exactly the sort of clean slate where a small
introduction goes unnoticed for months.

The approach that works is to treat the move as a decontamination rather than a
packing exercise. Everything textile goes through a hot dryer before it is
packed. Clean items go into sealed bags or bins with lids, not open boxes, and
stay sealed until they are in the new place. Books, electronics, and anything
that cannot be heated get gone through individually — bed bugs use book spines,
the backs of picture frames and the inside of electronics as harborage.

Upholstered furniture and mattresses are the difficult category. If a mattress
or a couch was in an affected room, the honest advice is usually not to take it,
and definitely not to put it on a curb where somebody else will collect it.

Unpack in the new place somewhere hard-floored rather than in a bedroom, the
same as you would after a trip.

And tell the incoming tenants and the landlord what was there. It is
uncomfortable and it is the right thing — the next household will find out
eventually, and finding out in October after a treatment window has closed is
much worse for them.

## Storage units and summers away

The other seasonal pattern worth knowing about is what happens to belongings
between June and September.

A storage unit holding a mattress, a couch and boxes of textiles from an
affected room preserves the problem rather than ending it. Bed bugs survive
long periods without feeding — considerably longer in cool conditions than most
people assume — so three months in storage is not a solution, and unloading that
unit in September reintroduces everything into a new building.

If belongings from an affected room are going into storage, the useful sequence
is to heat-treat the textiles first, seal them, and think hard about whether the
upholstered items are worth keeping at all.

The same applies to anything left in a shared house over the summer while the
building sits empty. An empty house does not clear itself.

## If you are the landlord

A short note, because a fair proportion of the people reading this will be on
that side.

Speed is the whole economy of this problem. The difference in cost between
treating one room in week two and treating a building in month five is very
large, and it falls on you either way.

Scope by structure, not by tenancy. Treating only the room that complained is
the most common false economy in this trade.

Coordinate the preparation rather than delegating it, particularly in student
housing where turnover is high and nobody has done this before. A single sheet
of clear instructions and a common date does more for the outcome than anything
else.

Deal with the furniture cycle. Encasements as standard on landlord-supplied
mattresses cost little, last years, and remove the single best harborage in the
building.

And plan around September, because that is when this arrives.

## The older housing stock here

Bellingham's rental housing has a character that matters for this problem.

A large share of it is old — early twentieth century houses subdivided into
rooms, sometimes several times over, with the partition walls, added doors and
retrofitted plumbing that implies. Every one of those alterations left gaps, and
gaps are how bed bugs move between rooms.

Original baseboards and trim in these houses have separated from the wall over a
century of movement, and the gap behind a loose baseboard is close to ideal
harborage. So are the joints in old wood floors, the gaps where a floor meets a
wall, and the cavities behind the pressed-on trim of a much later refit.

Radiator and pipe penetrations from an older heating system, and the holes cut
for a later one, connect rooms vertically as well as horizontally.

And the furniture in these houses is often old, upholstered and has been through
several tenancies. A landlord-supplied couch that has been in a student house
for a decade has a history.

None of that makes the problem unsolvable. It does mean that a treatment in this
housing stock has to work the structure — edges, voids, trim, joints — rather
than the furniture alone, and it is why we search further from the bed here than
we would in a newer building.

## Living with it while it is being dealt with

Between the first visit and the last follow-up there are usually several weeks,
and people want to know how to get through them.

Keep sleeping in your own bed, with encasements on and interceptors under the
legs. This is both the containment strategy and the monitoring strategy.

Keep the bed away from the wall and keep bedding, clothing and bags off the
floor. The legs should be the only route up.

Do not start over-cleaning in a way that moves things between rooms. Vacuuming
edges and emptying the canister outside in a sealed bag is useful. Hauling
belongings from room to room is not.

Expect to see some activity between visits. It is not evidence the treatment
failed; it is what a population in decline looks like while eggs are still
hatching. What matters is the trend across the follow-ups.

Tell whoever is treating if anything changes — a new room reporting bites, a
housemate who did not prepare, furniture that got moved. The scope can be
adjusted, and late information is the usual reason a job needs a fourth visit.
