## A moisture problem with an insect in it

Dampwood termites are the termite of this region, and the single most important
thing to understand about them is that they are a consequence rather than a
cause.

Unlike species that build tubes from soil into a dry building, dampwood termites
need timber with high moisture content — wood that is already wet, and usually
already decaying. They do not create that condition. They find it.

So when we find dampwood termites in a crawlspace, we are looking at two
problems stacked together. There is an insect in structural timber, and behind
it there is water that has been present long enough for decay to establish. Of
the two, the water is generally the more consequential and always the more
urgent to fix.

That framing matters because it determines what a real solution looks like. A
treatment applied to wet wood in a wet crawlspace is a temporary measure. A
treatment applied after the water is corrected and the failed timber is replaced
is a resolution.

## What they are and what they look like

Dampwood termites in the Pacific Northwest are notably large as termites go, and
they are most often encountered in one of three forms.

**Swarmers, or alates.** The reproductive stage, and by far the most commonly
seen. Large winged insects, brownish, emerging typically in late summer and
autumn, often in the evening and often around lights. Their wings are four,
roughly equal in length, and they shed them readily — a scatter of discarded
wings on a windowsill or a crawlspace floor is a classic finding.

**Nymphs and workers.** Pale, soft-bodied, and living entirely within the wood.
They are rarely seen unless timber is opened.

**Soldiers.** Distinguished by a large dark head and prominent mandibles,
defending the colony. Also only found inside the wood.

The confusion is nearly always with carpenter ant swarmers, and the distinction
is straightforward once you know it. A termite has a broad, straight-sided body
with no pinched waist, straight bead-like antennae, and four wings of about
equal length. A carpenter ant swarmer has an obvious narrow waist, elbowed
antennae, and forewings noticeably longer than the hind pair.

Getting this right matters because the two carry different implications and
different work.

## Where they turn up

The list of locations in a Whatcom County crawlspace is short and consistent,
because it follows the water.

**The sill plate and rim joist.** The timber sitting directly on the foundation,
which takes moisture from splash-back, from a foundation wicking ground water,
from poor grading and from persistent crawlspace humidity. Structural, and the
most common finding.

**Posts and piers in ground contact**, particularly older wood posts set directly
into soil or standing on a pad that has silted over.

**Joists and subfloor under a bathroom, kitchen or laundry**, where a long-running
slow leak has kept an area wet for years.

**Beams and girders** where they bear on a foundation pocket that traps water.

**Deck ledgers and framing** where a deck was attached without flashing, keeping
the rim behind it permanently wet.

**Anywhere timber meets soil**, including buried construction debris, form boards
left in place, and stumps under an addition.

## Reading the damage

Termite work looks different from the other things that eat into timber here, and
the differences are visible without magnification.

**Galleries cross the grain.** Carpenter ants follow the softer grain and produce
smooth, clean, almost sanded chambers. Termites cut across growth rings as well
as along them, producing a more chambered, layered appearance.

**Debris stays in.** Carpenter ants housekeep, pushing frass out and leaving
galleries swept clean. Termite galleries are frequently packed with dark
material — fecal pellets and chewed wood — which is one of the more reliable field
distinctions.

**Pellets.** Dampwood termites produce hard, six-sided fecal pellets, and where
they are pushed out of a gallery they collect in small piles beneath. These feel
like coarse grit rather than powder or fiber.

**Softness.** Because the wood was decaying before the termites arrived, the
surrounding material generally gives under a blunt probe. Sound timber resists.

**Discarded wings.** Around windows, on a vapor barrier, in a corner of the crawl
area, particularly in late summer.

## What else is going on down there

A crawlspace that has produced dampwood termites will almost always show the rest
of the damp-building picture, and it is worth looking at all of it at once.

Decay fungi in the timber, sometimes visible as discoloration, a white or brown
growth, or a cubical cracking pattern where the wood has broken down.

Moisture ants, which occupy exactly the same conditions and are frequently found
alongside — small, yellowish, and another reliable indicator of wet decaying
wood.

Springtails, sowbugs, millipedes and silverfish in numbers, all of which need
moisture and none of which persist in a dry space.

Standing water or a saturated vapor barrier, or no vapor barrier at all.

Corroded vent screens and a hatch that no longer seals, which is a rodent
problem waiting to happen alongside everything else.

And the causes: grade falling toward the building, downspouts discharging at the
foundation, a plumbing failure, a hose bib dripping, blocked or buried crawl
vents, a fan discharging into an enclosed space, and roof water with nowhere to
go.

## What treatment involves

Assuming the identification is settled, the work has a defined shape.

**Locate the extent.** This means going the length of the crawlspace with a light
and probing rather than looking, because the affected area is nearly always
larger than the visible sign. Sill plate, rim joist, posts, beams, joists and
subfloor, methodically.

**Trace the moisture.** Where is it coming from, how long has it been there, and
what will stop it. This is not optional and it is not an upsell — it is the part
that decides whether anything holds.

**Treat the affected timber.** Direct application into galleries and into the
affected members, with borate-based products or other appropriate materials
depending on the situation. Treating wood rather than soil is the approach here,
because the colony is in the wood.

**Remove the moisture source.** Drainage, grading, downspouts, a vapor barrier,
crawl ventilation, the plumbing repair, the flashing detail. Usually somebody
else's trade and always part of the plan.

**Replace what has gone.** Timber that has lost structural capacity to decay does
not recover when it dries. Where the sill plate, rim joist or a post has failed,
it needs cutting out and replacing, and that is a builder's job.

**Follow up.** Once the space is dry and the repairs are done, a check confirms
that nothing has been missed and that the conditions have genuinely changed.

## Order of operations

The sequence saves money, so it is worth stating plainly.

Water first, or at least concurrently. Treating wet wood and leaving it wet
produces a repeat visit.

Structural replacement after the water is dealt with, so new timber goes into a
dry environment rather than the one that destroyed the old timber.

Treatment of remaining affected members alongside or after, with borate treatment
of new timber where it is going into a location that has a history.

And the crawlspace improvements — vapor barrier, ventilation, drainage — as part
of the same project rather than as a separate thought, because they are what
prevent the next thirty years repeating the last thirty.

## The other termite in this region

Dampwood is the species most people here encounter, but it is not the only one,
and the distinction changes the work substantially.

**Subterranean termites** occur in Washington, but the range statement matters
more than the presence does. WSU Cooperative Extension bulletin EB0787 puts the
subterranean termite only as far north as Seattle in western Washington, and
this county lies about ninety miles past that. So the species is documented in
the state and not documented this far up it, which is a meaningfully different
claim from "present in western Washington" and the one the source actually
supports.

Where it does occur it behaves differently in one crucial respect: it nests in
soil and travels from there into wood, maintaining a connection to the ground
for moisture. That connection shows as mud tubes — pencil-width earthen tracks
running up a foundation wall, a pier, a post or the inside face of a stem wall.
Tubes in a crawlspace would be a different and generally more serious
conversation, because a soil-based colony is not limited by the wetness of a
single piece of timber and the treatment has to address the ground as well as
the wood.

**Drywood termites** are largely a problem of drier, warmer regions and are not
established here. They occasionally arrive in furniture or timber brought from
elsewhere. Their signature is the hard six-sided pellets accumulating below small
kick-out holes in dry wood well away from any moisture.

Which sets the practical rule in this county, and it is the reverse of the one
most termite writing gives. Dampwood is the working assumption under a Whatcom
County floor. Subterranean is the low-probability exception, and mud tubes are
what would move it out of that category rather than what you start from.

Look for them anyway. Thirty seconds of a flashlight along a stem wall costs
nothing, tubes are unmistakable once you have seen one, and on the rare occasion
they are there the whole scope changes. So the tubes get looked at on every
crawlspace visit regardless of what the call was about, and that will not be
changing.

What that look is not is a way of clearing a building. Finding no tubes in a
crawlspace here is close to the expected result and therefore carries very
little information — it confirms the likely thing rather than ruling out the
unlikely one. The mistake worth naming is the opposite of the usual one: not
missing subterranean termites, but treating their absence as evidence that
nothing is wrong in timber that a probe is already sinking into.

## What the swarm means

Most households first learn about this from a swarm rather than from a
crawlspace, so it is worth explaining what that event is telling you.

A colony that has been established for several years — often quite a few —
begins producing winged reproductives. In late summer and autumn, usually on a
warm still evening, they emerge, fly a short distance, drop their wings and pair
off, looking for wet wood to start again.

Two things follow from that. First, a swarm is evidence of a mature colony, not a
new arrival. Whatever produced it has been in that timber for some time. Second,
the swarmers themselves are harmless and will mostly die within hours; killing
the ones on your windowsill accomplishes nothing about the colony that sent them.

Where the swarm emerges matters more than how many there are. Insects appearing
inside a room, or a scatter of shed wings on an interior windowsill, points to a
colony within the structure. Swarmers around an exterior light on a warm evening
may have come from a stump forty feet away.

Keep some. A dozen in a sealed bag settles the identification question
immediately and separates termite from carpenter ant without any guesswork.

## When a sale is involved

This finding shows up in transactions more than most, because a crawlspace is
exactly where a buyer's inspector looks and the sill plate is exactly what they
probe.

Where a report has identified dampwood termite activity and decayed structural

time than under a deadline with somebody else's estimate in front of you.

## Prevention, which is mostly plumbing and drainage

The measures that keep dampwood termites out of a building have almost nothing
to do with insects.

Keep the crawlspace dry. A complete lapped vapor barrier over the ground, open
and screened vents that are not buried by landscaping, and a sump where the
ground genuinely holds water.

Get roof water away. Gutters that work, downspouts extended well clear of the
foundation, and no discharge point that soaks the ground against a wall.

Grade away from the building. Soil should fall away, not toward, and it should
not be built up against siding.

Break ground contact. No timber sitting in soil — posts on proper pads, form
boards removed, debris cleared, and firewood stored away from the building rather
than against it.

Fix leaks promptly, including the slow ones under bathrooms and kitchens that
never show themselves anywhere visible.

Flash decks properly where they attach, because the ledger detail is one of the
most reliable producers of wet rim joist in this county.

Borate-treat new structural timber going into any location with a history of
moisture. It is inexpensive at the point of construction and effective for a
long time.

## How urgent is it, honestly

People understandably want to know whether their floor is about to fail, and the
proportionate answer sits between the two extremes people arrive with.

Dampwood termites work slowly compared with the reputation termites carry
generally. A colony does not consume a sill plate over a winter. Damage
accumulates across years, which is why the finding so often coincides with a
water problem that has also been running for years.

At the same time, the material involved is structural and it is at the base of
the building, and the decay that preceded the insects has usually taken more
capacity than the insects themselves. So this is not something to leave for
another season, and it is not something to panic about tonight.

The signals that raise the urgency: a probe sinking easily into a sill plate or
a post; visible sagging, springiness or movement in the floor above; standing
water in the crawl area; damage found in more than one location; or mud tubes,
which change the species question entirely.

The signals that lower it: localized damage in one member, wood that is wet but
still firm under a probe, a moisture source that is identified and easily fixed,
and a crawlspace that is otherwise dry and ventilated.

Either way the first step is the same and it is free: have somebody go under
there properly and tell you which of those two descriptions fits.
