## What you have actually found

Opening a drawer or pulling out a toe-kick panel and finding a scatter of small
dark pellets is a specific kind of unpleasant, and the first reaction is
usually to clean it up immediately and think about it afterwards.

That is understandable, and it is worth taking two minutes first, because what
is in front of you is the most useful evidence you are going to get. Once it is
wiped away, the questions we would ask — how much, how fresh, where exactly —
become guesswork.

The important thing to understand is what droppings in a kitchen mean as
distinct from droppings anywhere else. A rodent leaves droppings continuously as
it moves, so finding them in a garage or a crawlspace tells you an animal passed
through. Finding a concentration of them in a food storage area tells you
something is spending time there, and something spends time where it is being
fed.

## Reading the size

The species question is answered almost entirely by size, and it matters because
the response differs.

**House mouse.** Around a quarter of an inch, roughly the shape of a grain of
rice, tapered at both ends. Produced in genuinely large numbers — a single mouse
leaves dozens in a day — and scattered rather than deposited in one place. This
is by a wide margin the most common finding in a kitchen.

**Deer mouse.** Similar in size to a house mouse and difficult to separate on
the droppings alone. Context helps: deer mice are more likely where a property
adjoins woodland, open ground or a greenbelt, and they are more strongly
seasonal. Their droppings and nesting material warrant more careful handling,
which we come to below.

**Rat.** Half an inch to three quarters, distinctly thicker, with blunter or
rounded ends rather than sharp points. Fewer of them, because rats are larger
animals in smaller numbers. Norway rat droppings tend to be more capsule-shaped;
roof rat droppings are somewhat more slender and curved. Either way, if what you
are looking at is obviously larger than a grain of rice, the job changes.

**Not a rodent at all.** Cockroach droppings are much smaller, closer to coarse
ground pepper or coffee, and often accompanied by dark smearing. Bat guano
crumbles to a glittery dust because it is largely insect fragments, but that is
an attic finding rather than a kitchen one and it is outside what we handle. The
size test resolves nearly every case.

## Reading the age

Freshness tells you whether this is happening now or happened once.

Fresh droppings are dark — near black in most species — soft, and have a faint
sheen. Within roughly two to three days they lose the shine, lighten toward gray
or brown, and harden.

Old droppings are dull, dry, and crumble to dust under light pressure. Use a
disposable implement rather than a finger.

What you usually find is a mixture, and a mixture is the most common and least
welcome answer: it means activity has been going on for a while and is still
going on now.

If everything is uniformly old and dry, there is a reasonable chance you are
looking at a historic problem — a previous occupant's, or one that resolved when
something else changed. That still deserves a look at the building, because the
opening that let them in is almost certainly still there.

## Reading the pattern

Where they are is as informative as what they are.

**Concentrated in one place.** A resting or feeding spot rather than a route.
Look for what is being eaten within a couple of feet of it.

**A line along the back of a cupboard or a run of countertop.** A route. Rodents
travel with a body part in contact with a vertical surface where they can — it
is a security behavior — so droppings along the back edge of a shelf or the base
of a wall map their movement rather than their destination.

**Inside a drawer or a sealed-looking cupboard.** More significant than it
sounds, because it means the cabinet interiors are connected to somewhere else.
Cabinet runs are almost never sealed at the back or underneath, and the void
beneath a toe-kick frequently opens into the floor structure.

**Under or behind appliances.** The most productive place to look and the one
people check last. The warmth behind a refrigerator, the void under a
dishwasher, the gap beside a range and the space beneath a built-in oven are all
sheltered, warm, and near food.

**On the countertop.** Worth acting on quickly, for obvious reasons around food
preparation surfaces.

## Cleaning up without making it worse

This part has a right and a wrong method, and the wrong method is the intuitive
one.

Do not sweep and do not vacuum dry droppings or nesting material. Both actions
put fine particulate into the air in a small enclosed room, and that is the
route by which the small health risks associated with rodent waste actually
reach people. In this region the species of note for that is the deer mouse, and
because you cannot reliably tell a deer mouse dropping from a house mouse
dropping by eye, the sensible course is to handle all of it the same way.

The method that is actually recommended is simple. Open a window and ventilate
the space for a while before starting. Put on disposable gloves. Spray the
droppings and the surrounding area thoroughly with a disinfectant or a dilute
bleach solution and leave it to soak for several minutes rather than wiping
immediately. Pick everything up with disposable paper towels. Bag the towels and
the gloves, seal the bag, and put it in an outside bin. Disinfect the surface
again afterwards, and wash your hands properly even though you wore gloves.

For a larger accumulation, or for nesting material in an enclosed space, a
fitted respirator is a reasonable precaution rather than an overreaction. A
paper dust mask is not the same thing.

## The food question

The instinct to throw everything out is understandable and usually excessive.

Discard anything in cardboard, paper, a paper sack or thin film packaging that
is stored in the affected area, and anything showing gnawing or gnawed
packaging, regardless of where it is. Rodents chew into boxes and bags routinely
and the damage is often small enough to miss.

Keep anything in glass, metal, or rigid sealed plastic, once the outside of the
container has been washed. Tinned goods are fine. Sealed jars are fine.

Wash any dishes, utensils or cookware that were stored in an affected cupboard.

And afterwards, move dry goods into rigid containers with proper lids. This is
the single most effective thing a household can do to make a kitchen
uninteresting, and it is worth doing whether or not you ever see another
dropping.

## Where they are coming from

Here is the part that decides whether this recurs.

Rodents do not begin in a kitchen. They arrive there from somewhere else in the
building, and they got into the building through an opening in its shell.

The chain usually runs from an exterior defect into an unfinished space — the
crawlspace, the garage, a wall cavity, an attic — and then into the kitchen
through a service penetration. Kitchens are riddled with those: the hole cut for
a water supply under the sink, the waste line, the gas line behind a range, the
electrical feed for a dishwasher or disposal, the refrigerator water line, and
the gaps around all of them where a cabinet meets the floor and the wall.

Pull out a bottom drawer and look at the back of the cabinet with a flashlight
and you will very often find the answer sitting in plain sight — a half-inch gap
around a copper pipe that has been there since the house was built.

That is why we spend most of a rodent visit somewhere other than the room where
the droppings are. Cleaning the kitchen and setting traps in the kitchen deals
with what you can see. Closing the exterior opening and the interior penetration
is what stops it happening again next October.

## What we do about it

The work has a fixed shape.

We find the interior connection points first, which means looking under and
behind the cabinet run, at the toe-kick voids, at every service penetration, and
at how the kitchen connects to whatever unfinished space lies beneath or beside
it.

We work the exterior systematically for the way into the building: crawl vents
and their screens, the crawl access, foundation penetrations, grade and where
soil meets siding, the garage door seal, the junction where an addition meets
the original structure, and the roofline where a climber would work.

We trap rather than bait inside the building envelope. Traps go on the runs and
in the voids, correctly oriented, in sufficient numbers, and get checked and
adjusted rather than left. Placement is most of the skill in this. A trap in the
middle of a floor catches nothing; a trap set tight to a wall on an active run
catches on the first night.

We seal in materials that hold. Metal mesh, sheet metal, mortar and proper vent
covers, cut and fitted rather than stuffed. Expanding foam by itself is not
exclusion and a rodent will be through it before the month is out.

And we tell you what we found, in plain terms, including the parts that are not
our work — the drainage issue, the failed door seal, the neighbor's feed store.

## Things people try first, and how they go

Almost everybody does something before they call, and it is worth saying which
of those things help.

**Snap traps from the hardware store.** These work, and they are what we use.
What people get wrong is the number and the position. One trap in the middle of
a cupboard floor will sit untouched for a fortnight. Six traps set tight against
walls, on the runs the droppings have already mapped for you, with the trigger
end toward the wall, will produce results in a night or two. If you are going to
try this yourself, buy more than you think you need and put them where the
evidence is rather than where it is convenient.

**Glue boards.** We do not use them and we would suggest you do not either.
They catch non-target animals, they are slow, and they leave you dealing with a
live and distressed animal in a way most people find genuinely upsetting.

**Bait stations indoors.** Please avoid this in a kitchen. An animal that takes
bait will die somewhere it feels safe, which will be inside a floor, a wall or
a cabinet base, and the resulting smell runs for weeks and draws flies on top of
it. Trapping keeps you in control of where the animal ends up.

**Peppermint oil, ultrasonic plug-ins and similar.** There is no reliable
evidence that either deters an established rodent. Animals with a food source
habituate quickly. They are harmless to try and they are not a plan.

**Steel wool pushed into a gap.** Better than nothing and better than foam, but
it is a temporary measure rather than a repair. It compresses, it rusts, and it
can be worked loose. Where it is holding, it is worth having the same opening
closed properly at some point.

**A cat.** Occasionally effective as a deterrent, frequently not, and never a
substitute for closing the building. Cat food left out overnight is also one of
the more common things sustaining the population in the first place.

## What a small number of droppings really means

We get asked this a great deal, usually hopefully, so here is the arithmetic.

A single house mouse produces something in the region of fifty to seventy
droppings in a day. That number surprises most people, and it has a useful
implication: a light scatter of fresh droppings in one cupboard is far more
likely to mean a recent arrival than a small, contained, tolerable problem.

The same arithmetic works in reverse over time. A heavy accumulation across
several locations does not necessarily mean a large population — it can be one
animal that has been resident for a couple of months. Volume tells you about
duration at least as much as about numbers.

Either way, the practical conclusion is the same and it is the more reassuring
one: finding droppings early, in one place, is the cheapest and fastest version
of this problem. Mice reach breeding age in a matter of weeks and litters follow
quickly, so the difference between acting in the first week and acting in the
second month is substantial.

## Why it always seems to be autumn

Kitchen droppings are a strongly seasonal complaint in Whatcom County, and the
curve is consistent year on year.

Calls begin climbing as nights cool in late September, run hard through October
and November, and stay elevated through the winter. What has changed is not the
rodent population outside but its motivation. Vegetation dies back and removes
cover. Natural food declines. A heated building becomes a genuinely better
proposition than it was in August.

Which is why the mouse that appears in a kitchen in the second week of October,
in a house that has been trouble-free all summer, is not a sign that anything
about the house got worse. It is the same quarter-inch gap that was always
there, meeting a change in the weather.

The corollary is that the best time to do exclusion work is the late summer,
before the push starts. Almost nobody does it then, because nothing is wrong in
August. It is still the right answer.

## Commercial kitchens are a different matter

If the room in question is a restaurant, a café, a bakery, a taproom kitchen or
any food premises, the calculation changes and it changes quickly.

The consequences are regulatory as well as practical. Evidence of rodent
activity in a food establishment is a health inspector's finding, and it can
carry consequences up to and including closure. The reputational side is worse:
one photograph on a review site does more damage than the animal ever could.

The building side is also harder. Commercial premises tend to have more
penetrations, more delivery traffic, more waste stored outside, longer hours,
shared walls with neighboring units, and back-of-house areas that were built for
throughput rather than for sealing. In a shared building the problem is
frequently not yours at all — it belongs to the unit next door, and it reaches
you through a party wall.

Our approach in those settings is documentation and discretion alongside the
work: records that show a controlled program rather than a reaction, service
timed around operating hours, and a written account of the structural defects
that need a landlord's attention rather than a technician's. If that is your
situation, say so when you call and we will treat it accordingly.

## What to do next

If you have found droppings, leave a representative patch undisturbed if you
reasonably can, clean the rest by the method above, and get the dry goods into
sealed containers.

Then have somebody look at the building rather than the room. We will go through
the kitchen, the connected voids, the crawlspace properly with a light, and the
full exterior at ground level and up. You get a direct account of what is
happening, where it is getting in, what needs closing and what it costs, and you
decide from there.

Rodent assessment is free, nothing here requires a term agreement, and we would
much rather seal a building once than visit it quarterly forever.
