## Yes, and the species matters more than the answer

Washington has cockroaches. It does not have the outdoor roach population of
Texas or Florida, which is what most people mean when they ask, and the
distinction is worth drawing because it changes what finding one means.

WSU Pestsense covers four pest cockroaches for this state: the German
cockroach, the American cockroach, the brownbanded cockroach and the Oriental
cockroach. It describes the German cockroach as the one most commonly found in
the Pacific Northwest, and that is a fair description of what we see on jobs —
it is not close.

Alongside those there are native wood cockroaches, which live outdoors, wander
in, and do not establish. Telling those two situations apart is most of what
this page is for.

## The German cockroach, which is the real one

Small, striped, indoors, and fast.

WSU puts the adult at about half an inch long, pale brown, with two dark brown
lengthwise stripes on the shield behind the head. Those stripes are the single
most useful identification character in this whole subject, because they tell
you immediately that you are dealing with the species that will not simply walk
back out again.

The German cockroach lives indoors year-round. It cannot survive a Whatcom
County winter outside, which means a population in a building came from
somewhere else and stays where it is. It breeds faster than any other roach
here, it hides in warm tight spaces — behind and beneath kitchen appliances, in
the voids around pipework, in the hinge recesses of cabinet doors — and it is
most active at night.

How they arrive is boxes, bags, used appliances, deliveries and shared walls,
and [cockroach control](/services/cockroach-control/) goes through the entry
routes in detail. Apartment buildings move them between units through plumbing chases,
which is why
[apartment pest control](/blog/apartment-pest-control-bellingham/) treats a
roach problem as a building problem rather than a unit problem.

## The other three

Present here, and much less often the answer.

**American cockroach.** WSU calls it the largest cockroach in Washington,
reddish-brown, with adults reaching an inch and a half. In this climate it turns
up in warm damp commercial settings — boiler rooms, basements, drains — far more
often than in houses.

**Oriental cockroach.** One to one and a half inches, dark brown to black, with
shorter wings. Associated with cool damp places: crawlspaces, drains, under
porches. In a county built on wet crawlspaces, this one is more plausible than
people expect, and it is usually a moisture question first.

**Brownbanded cockroach.** Half to five-eighths of an inch, reddish-brown to
dark brown with lighter bands across the wings. Uncommon here, and it behaves
differently from a German cockroach — it spreads through a building rather than
concentrating in kitchens and bathrooms.

## Wood cockroaches, which are the ones you keep finding

The large roach on the porch in July is almost certainly not an infestation.

Wood cockroaches live outdoors in leaf litter, under loose bark, in woodpiles
and compost. Males fly, they are drawn to lights, and in early summer they
wander into garages, sheds and houses in ones and twos. The important part: they
do not breed indoors and do not become established there.

What gives them away is the pattern rather than the animal. A wood cockroach
appears singly, often during the day, often in a garage or near a door, usually
in June or July, and often near a light. A German cockroach appears at night,
near food or water, in a kitchen or bathroom, and rarely alone.

If the roaches you find are outdoor species, the work is exclusion and
housekeeping — firewood off the house, leaf litter pulled back, door sweeps that
touch — and not a treatment. That list is in
[sealing a building before fall](/guides/sealing-a-building-before-fall/).

## What a real infestation looks like

Not the roach. The things around it.

- **Droppings** like coarse ground pepper along the edges of shelves, inside
  cabinet hinges and behind appliances.
- **A musty, oily smell** in a kitchen, which becomes noticeable at a population
  size most people have already reached by the time they call.
- **Egg cases** — small, pale, ridged capsules wedged into tight corners and
  under the lips of shelves.
- **Smear marks** in damp areas where roaches travel repeatedly.
- **Daytime sightings**, which generally mean the harborage is already full.

Any two of those in a kitchen is a German cockroach population, whatever the one
roach you actually saw looked like.

## What we do, and the one thing not to do first

The one thing not to do is set off a fogger.

Total-release foggers are the most common reason a straightforward German
cockroach job becomes a difficult one. They do not reach into the voids where
the population actually lives, they push roaches deeper into wall and cabinet
cavities, and the residue they leave on surfaces makes the bait those roaches
would otherwise have eaten unattractive. We have been called to plenty of jobs
that were twice the work because of one.

What works is a baiting program with proper placement, repeated, with sanitation
and harborage reduction alongside it, and with the kitchen treated as a system
rather than as a set of surfaces. WSU's own guidance is that baits work best
when competing food is removed and that control can take a week or more to
become visible. The full approach is on
[cockroach control](/services/cockroach-control/).

And if what you have turns out to be a wood cockroach that came in under the
garage door, we would rather tell you that and leave you with a door sweep to
buy than sell you a treatment program for an animal that was never going to
stay.
