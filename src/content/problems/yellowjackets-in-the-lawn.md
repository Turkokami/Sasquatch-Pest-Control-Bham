## The nest you cannot see

Every wasp situation on a residential property has its own character. A paper
wasp nest under an eave is visible, small and usually manageable. A hornet nest
in a tree is unmissable. A yellowjacket colony in the ground is neither, and
that is precisely what makes it the one we take most seriously.

There is nothing to see. The entrance is a hole in the ground, frequently under
an inch across, in a lawn, a bank, a border, a compost area or the untended
margin between mown grass and rough ground. The colony itself may be a foot
below, in a cavity that started as a rodent burrow and has been expanded through
the season.

Which means nobody knows it is there until something disturbs it. Usually a
mower. Sometimes a string trimmer, a rake, a wheelbarrow wheel, a dog, or a child
running across a lawn.

And unlike most stinging insects, ground-nesting yellowjackets respond to
disturbance at the entrance with a coordinated defense in numbers. They are
capable of stinging repeatedly, they release alarm pheromone that recruits more
of the colony, and they will follow a retreating person a considerable distance.

That combination — invisible, defended, and reached by accident — is why this is
the wasp problem most likely to produce a serious injury on a domestic property
in this county.

## Where the nests are

Yellowjackets choose sites with a particular set of qualities, and once you know
them you can predict where a nest will be on most properties.

**Abandoned rodent burrows.** By far the most common. The cavity already exists,
it is the right size, it drains, and it has a defensible entrance. Any property
with vole or mouse activity in the ground has candidate sites everywhere.

**Banks and slopes.** Sloped ground drains well and offers a horizontal entrance
that stays dry. The bank at the edge of a lawn, the side of a ditch, the
embankment along a driveway, and the cut behind a retaining wall are all
classic.

**Under structures at ground level.** Sheds, decks, steps, porches and
outbuildings on skids or blocks create a protected space with soil beneath.

**In root plates and beneath stumps.** The void left as a root system decays is
ideal.

**Under stored material.** Timber piles, pallets, tarps, stacked pots and
anything that has been sitting on the ground for a season.

**In the untended margin.** The strip between a mown lawn and a fence line,
hedge or treeline is disturbed rarely and offers cover, which is why so many
nests are found there in the week somebody finally decides to tidy it.

**In retaining walls and rockeries**, using the voids between stones.

## Reading the year

The seasonal curve is the single most useful thing to understand about
yellowjackets, because it explains almost everything people notice.

In spring a single overwintered queen starts the nest alone. Through early
summer she raises the first workers, and the colony is genuinely small — a few
dozen insects. It is present and it is easy to miss entirely.

Through July and August the colony grows steeply. Workers forage for protein to
feed developing larvae, which is when they take an interest in barbecues, pet
food and anything meat-based.

By late August and September the colony reaches maximum size — depending on
species and conditions, that can be several thousand individuals — and the
dietary preference shifts toward sugar, which is when they arrive at fallen
fruit, sweet drinks, bins and outdoor eating in numbers.

This peak coincides exactly with the weeks people are outside most and doing the
most yard work. That is not a coincidence in any meaningful sense, but it does
explain why almost every serious sting incident we hear about happens in a
four-week window at the end of summer.

With hard frosts the colony dies. Only newly mated queens survive, dispersing to
overwinter elsewhere, and the nest is not reused.

## Finding the entrance safely

If you suspect a ground nest, the way to confirm it is to watch, not to look.

Stand well back — twenty feet is comfortable — with the sun behind you or the
light across the area rather than in your eyes. Mid-morning through afternoon on
a warm day gives the most traffic.

Watch for a consistent flight line. Foraging yellowjackets do not wander toward
a nest; they come in on a repeated path and drop into a single point. Two
minutes of watching will usually resolve the entrance precisely.

Mark it from a distance. A garden cane pushed into the ground several feet away,
or a bucket set down as a landmark, is enough. Do not put a marker at the hole
itself.

Then keep everybody away from it. Rope off the area if children or pets use that
part of the garden, and tell anybody who does yard work exactly where it is.

What not to do: never poke, dig, probe, uncover or shine a flashlight directly into a
suspected entrance to check. That is the action that converts a manageable
situation into an emergency.

## Why the home remedies are a bad idea

There is a well-established folk repertoire for ground nests and every item in
it puts a person directly over an angry colony.

**Pouring gasoline or fuel down the hole.** Genuinely dangerous, environmentally
serious, contaminates soil, and does not reliably kill the colony. It also
requires you to stand at the entrance for several seconds.

**Boiling water.** Rarely reaches the nest chamber, definitely reaches the
wasps at the entrance, and leaves you at close range holding a full kettle.

**Aerosols from the hardware store.** These work on a visible nest at a
distance. On a ground nest they deliver product to the top few inches of a
tunnel while the colony below is unaffected and now alerted.

**Blocking the hole.** Yellowjackets will excavate a new exit, and in a nest
under a structure that new exit sometimes opens into a building rather than
outside.

**Doing it at night with a flashlight.** The reasoning is sound — the colony is home
and less active — but a light held at the entrance gives an agitated colony a
target to fly at, and you are working in the dark next to an obstacle course.

We have been called to enough badly stung people after each of these to say
plainly that none of them is worth it.

## What removal involves

Done properly, a ground nest is treated rather than excavated, and the equipment
matters more than the technique.

Protective gear first: a proper suit, veil and gloves, not a jacket and
sunglasses. Yellowjackets find gaps.

Treatment is generally a dust or an appropriately formulated product delivered
into the entrance and the tunnel so that returning foragers carry it into the
nest chamber. Depth and tunnel geometry decide how straightforward that is; a
shallow nest in an open lawn is quick, and a colony a foot down behind a
retaining wall is not.

Timing is deliberate — early morning or dusk, when the largest proportion of the
colony is in the nest rather than out foraging.

Then the site is left alone for a period rather than immediately disturbed,
because returning foragers continue to arrive for a day or two. Digging the nest
out immediately is unnecessary and provocative.

And afterwards, where the site was a rodent burrow or a persistent structural
void, closing or filling it reduces the chance of the same spot being chosen
next year.

## When leaving it alone is the right answer

We will say this on site and we will say it here: not every ground nest needs
treating.

If the nest is well away from where anybody walks, works or plays — in a
woodland margin, a far field boundary, a bank nobody crosses — and there is no
realistic prospect of accidental disturbance, then leaving it is legitimate. The
colony dies with the frosts and does not return to that hole.

If it is late in the season and the nest is marginal in position, the same
calculation applies with more force, because you are only waiting a few weeks.

Yellowjackets are also, for what it is worth, substantial predators of other
insects through the summer, and a colony in a far corner is doing more good than
harm.

What changes the answer is proximity and traffic. A nest within range of a
mowing line, a path, a play area, a shed people use, a dog run, or anywhere
somebody works with power equipment is a different proposition, and so is any
nest where a member of the household has a known sting allergy. In those cases
it comes out.

## If a nest has already been disturbed

This happens most often mid-mow, and the response matters.

Move away immediately and keep moving. Do not swat, and do not stand still to
assess. Yellowjackets will pursue, and alarm pheromone marks whoever disturbed
the nest.

Get indoors, or into a vehicle, and stay there. Get children and animals in
first.

Leave the equipment where it is. A mower parked over an entrance can be
retrieved later; nobody should go back for it that day.

Expect the area to stay dangerous for several hours, and possibly through the
following day. The colony does not settle quickly.

If somebody has been stung multiple times, or shows any sign of a reaction
beyond local pain and swelling — difficulty breathing, swelling away from the
sting site, dizziness, widespread hives — that is a medical emergency and needs
emergency care immediately rather than a phone call to us.

## Telling them apart from the things they get confused with

Several insects nest in or near the ground here and only some of them are worth
treating, so identification is worth a moment.

**Yellowjackets** are the ones this page is about: bright yellow and black,
smooth-bodied with very little hair, fast, and defensive at the nest entrance.
Traffic in and out of a single small hole in numbers is the signature.

**Bumble bees** also nest in the ground and in cavities, frequently in old rodent
burrows, under sheds and in compost. They are large, round and densely furry,
and the colony is small — dozens rather than thousands. They are important
pollinators, they are not aggressive away from the nest, and the colony dies out
naturally in late summer. We will not treat a bumble bee nest that can
reasonably be left, and we will say so.

**Mining and solitary bees** produce small mounds of fine soil with a central
hole across a lawn in spring, often many of them in one area. They look alarming
because of the number, they are almost entirely harmless, they do not defend a
colony, and they are gone within weeks. Treating them is a waste of money and a
loss to the garden.

**Honey bees** occasionally occupy a ground cavity or a wall void. These are not
a pest control job; a swarm or an established colony is a beekeeper's business,
and we will point you to one rather than treat.

**Paper wasps** build the small open-celled nests under eaves and rails rather
than in the ground, and they are markedly less defensive.

**Bald-faced hornets** build the large enclosed gray nests in trees and shrubs.
Different insect, different job, same family.

If you can photograph the insect from a safe distance, or describe the size,
shape and how furry it is, we can usually tell you on the phone which of these
you have and whether it needs anything doing at all.

## Reducing the odds next year

A few practical measures genuinely lower the number of ground nests on a
property.

Walk the ground in late spring, when colonies are small and a queen founding a
nest is much easier and safer to deal with than a mature colony in August. This
is the single most effective habit and almost nobody does it.

Deal with rodent burrows, since they are the preferred site. A property with a
managed rodent situation has fewer ready-made cavities.

Keep the margins tidier — the strip along a fence line or treeline that never
gets attention is where nests go undetected longest.

Remove or lift stored material off the ground, and turn or move anything that
has been sitting in the same place since spring.

Manage the food sources that concentrate foragers near living areas: lidded
bins, fallen fruit picked up, pet food not left out, and drinks covered at
outdoor tables in late summer.

And know where last year's nest was, because the site conditions that suited a
colony once will suit another.

## Farms, acreage and working properties

A ground nest on a working property is a different risk calculation from one in
a suburban back garden, and a good share of this county is working property.

The exposure is higher. Field margins, ditch banks, fence lines and equipment
yards are exactly the ground yellowjackets prefer, and they are also where
somebody is operating machinery, clearing brush or handling stock. A nest struck
by a tractor or a brush cutter puts a person in a worse position than a mower
does, because getting clear takes longer.

Livestock and working dogs complicate it further. Animals cannot be told where
the nest is, and a horse or a dog that disturbs a colony can be stung
repeatedly before anybody can intervene.

Outbuildings add sites. Barns, equipment sheds, feed stores and anything on
blocks or skids create protected ground voids in numbers, and the margin around
them is rarely walked.

The practical approach on acreage is different from a single-nest call. It is
worth walking the working areas in early summer rather than waiting for an
incident, marking what is found, and dealing with the nests that sit on routes
people and animals actually use — while leaving the ones in far field corners
alone. That is a smaller bill than treating everything and a better outcome than
treating nothing.

If you have staff, seasonal help or contractors on the property, telling them
where known nests are is worth as much as any treatment.

## Do the traps help?

Hanging traps are the most common thing people buy for this, and the honest
answer is that they have a narrow use.

A trap draws wasps toward it. That is the mechanism. Placed close to a patio or
a door it therefore concentrates foraging insects exactly where you did not want
them, which is why some households find traps make their late summer worse
rather than better.

Placed well away from where people sit — at the far edge of a property, downwind,
between the nest and the house — a trap can pull some traffic away from a
seating area. That is a genuine but modest benefit.

What a trap will not do is reduce the colony. A mature nest produces workers far
faster than a trap removes them, and catching a few hundred foragers does not
change what is happening underground.

The spring-queen argument, that trapping in April removes founding queens before
they establish, has some logic to it and thin evidence behind it. It is cheap,
it does no harm, and we would not promise you a result from it.

Our view: traps are a comfort measure for outdoor eating, positioned away from
people, and nothing more. They are not a substitute for dealing with a nest that
sits on a mowing line.
