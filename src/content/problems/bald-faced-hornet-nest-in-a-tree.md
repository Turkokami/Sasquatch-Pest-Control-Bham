## The nest everyone recognizes

Of all the stinging insect situations on a residential property, this is the one
that produces the most immediate alarm, and it is easy to see why. A mature
bald-faced hornet nest is a gray, layered, papery structure the size of a
football or larger, hanging visibly in a tree, a hedge, a shrub or occasionally
against a building.

It looks like a serious problem. It looks like something that must be dealt with
today.

Quite often it is not, and the reason is worth understanding before anybody
spends money on it.

## What they actually are

Despite the name, bald-faced hornets are not true hornets. They are a large
black and white species of yellowjacket, closely related to the ones that nest
in the ground, and their behavior sits between the two.

They are markedly larger than a common yellowjacket, patterned in black and
ivory white rather than yellow, with the pale face that gives them their name.

The nest is built from chewed wood fiber mixed with saliva, layered into a
distinctive gray papery shell around several tiers of comb, with a single
entrance near the bottom. A mature nest may contain several hundred workers,
occasionally more.

They are aerial nesters by preference — trees, shrubs, dense hedges, and
sometimes the eaves, soffits or wall face of a building. Height varies from
around head level up to well into a canopy.

## The annual cycle, which decides everything

Almost every practical question about these nests is answered by the life cycle,
so it is worth setting out clearly.

In spring, a single queen that mated the previous autumn and overwintered
somewhere sheltered emerges and begins a nest alone. At this stage it is the
size of a golf ball and contains one insect.

Through early summer she raises the first workers, who take over construction
and foraging. The nest grows steadily as they add layers to the outside and comb
within.

Through July and August growth accelerates sharply. This is when the nest
becomes the recognizable large structure, and when the colony is at its most
populous and most defensive.

In late summer the colony produces new queens and males, which leave to mate.

With the first hard frosts the colony dies. The workers, the old queen, the
brood — all of it. Only the newly mated queens survive, dispersing to overwinter
in leaf litter, under bark or in a sheltered cavity.

And critically: **the nest is never reused.** Next spring's queens each build
somewhere new. An old nest hanging in a tree in January is an empty paper shell
that weather will break up over the winter.

## Why the leaf-fall discovery is usually a non-event

The single most common version of this call goes like this: the leaves came down
in October, and there is an enormous hornet nest fifteen feet up in the maple
that nobody knew about.

Think about what that means. The nest has been there since spring. It reached
full size in August. The household has been living, gardening and eating in that
yard all summer, and nothing happened. The nest was invisible because the foliage
hid it, and the colony was going about its business without any conflict.

Now it is late October, the colony is already in decline or finished, the frosts
will complete it within weeks, and the structure will not be occupied again.

In that situation the honest recommendation is usually to leave it alone. You
can knock the empty nest down in winter if you want the tree tidy, at which
point it is just paper.

We say this knowing perfectly well it is a job we are declining. It is still the
right answer, and a company that treats every visible nest as an emergency is
telling you more about its sales process than about hornets.

## When it genuinely should come out

Position and timing decide it, not size.

**Proximity to a doorway, a path, a driveway or steps.** A nest within a few feet
of somewhere people pass every day is a hazard, because eventually somebody will
brush the branch, bang a door, or walk into the flight line.

**Low nests.** A nest at head height in a hedge or a shrub is far more likely to
be disturbed than one twenty feet up in a canopy, and hedges get trimmed.

**Play areas, patios and outdoor seating.** Anywhere children or guests are
regularly present within the defended radius.

**Working areas.** Near a shed, a workshop, a woodpile, a vegetable garden, a
fence line that needs maintenance, or anywhere power tools get used.

**Where somebody in the household has a sting allergy.** This changes the
calculation entirely and we treat it as urgent.

**Mid-season discovery.** A nest found in June or July has months of growth
ahead of it. Dealing with it then is a much smaller job than dealing with it in
September.

**Against a building.** A nest on a wall, soffit or eave is closer to occupied
space and more likely to interact with people and windows.

If none of those apply, leaving it is a legitimate choice and often the better
one.

## How defensive are they, honestly

Somewhere between the reputation and the reassurance.

Bald-faced hornets defend the nest at a greater distance than most stinging
insects here — commonly cited as roughly a ten-foot radius, though it varies
with the colony, the weather and the time of season. Within that, disturbance
provokes a response. Beyond it, they are largely indifferent to people.

They can sting repeatedly, unlike a honey bee. Some individuals will also spray
defensive fluid toward the eyes of a perceived threat at the nest, which is
unpleasant and worth knowing about.

They will pursue an intruder some distance from the nest, and alarm signaling
recruits others.

Away from the nest, foraging workers are not aggressive. They are predators of
other insects — flies, caterpillars and other soft-bodied prey — and a hornet
working a shrub in your garden is not a threat to anyone.

The practical translation: respect the radius, do not disturb the nest, and the
colony will not seek you out.

## What not to do

**Do not knock it down.** A dislodged nest releases the whole colony at once, on
the ground, next to you. This is the classic way people end up with dozens of
stings.

**Do not spray it from below with a hardware store aerosol.** The jet reaches ten
to twenty feet at best, the shell absorbs a great deal of it, and you are
standing directly beneath an entrance you have just provoked, in the open, with
nowhere to go.

**Do not shoot it.** People do. It produces the same outcome as knocking it down
with the added benefit of holes in something behind it.

**Do not burn it.** A paper nest in a living tree is an obvious fire risk, it
rarely kills the colony cleanly, and it can damage or kill the tree.

**Do not seal it or wrap it.** Whatever you have wrapped it in will not hold.

**Do not do any of it from a ladder.** A ladder removes the one thing that keeps
people safe in this situation, which is the ability to move away quickly.

## What removal actually involves

Where a nest genuinely needs treating, the work is straightforward for somebody
properly equipped and genuinely unwise for somebody who is not.

Full protective equipment — a suit, veil and gloves rated for it, not a jacket
and a hat.

Treatment at dusk or before dawn, when nearly the whole colony is in the nest
rather than out foraging. This single decision changes the job more than
anything else.

Application through the entrance and into the nest structure with equipment that
can reach the height involved, rather than from directly underneath at close
range.

A period of settling before anything is removed. Returning foragers arrive for a
day or so, and the nest is left in place until traffic has stopped.

Then, where the nest is to be taken down, removal of the structure itself — a
separate visit, usually, and one that is genuinely uneventful once the colony is
gone.

Height and access are the variables that decide cost. A nest at eight feet in an
open shrub is quick. A nest twenty-five feet into a canopy over a neighbor's
fence is a different proposition, and occasionally the honest answer is that it
cannot be reached safely and should be left to the frosts.

## If somebody has been stung

Most stings from this species are a local event and resolve on their own, but it
is worth knowing what normal looks like and what does not.

A normal reaction is immediate sharp pain, redness, and swelling around the sting
site that develops over a few hours and subsides over a day or two. Cold applied
to the area, an antihistamine and a simple painkiller cover it. Bald-faced
hornet stings are, by most accounts, more painful than a common yellowjacket's,
which is a function of size rather than of anything more serious.

A large local reaction — swelling extending well beyond the site, sometimes
across a whole limb, developing over a day or two — is uncomfortable and
alarming but is not usually dangerous. It is worth mentioning to a doctor,
particularly if it recurs.

What needs emergency care immediately is any sign of a systemic reaction:
difficulty breathing, tightness in the throat or chest, swelling of the face,
lips or tongue, hives spreading away from the sting, dizziness, faintness, or
sudden nausea and vomiting. That is anaphylaxis and it is a call to emergency
services, not a call to us.

Multiple stings from a disturbed colony also warrant medical advice regardless of
allergy status, simply because of the total venom load.

If anyone in the household carries an epinephrine autoinjector for insect
stings, a nest anywhere near living space is not a judgment call. It comes out,
and we will prioritize it.

## What we tend to find on a property

When we come out for one nest, we look at the whole property, because these
rarely occur in isolation and the other findings change the advice.

Paper wasp nests under eaves, deck rails, in open sheds and under garden
furniture — usually small, usually leaveable, worth knowing about.

Yellowjacket ground nests in banks, borders, under structures and along the
untended margin, which are the genuinely hazardous ones because nobody sees them
before a mower does.

A second aerial nest, which is common enough to be worth checking for once the
first one has been found.

The food and habitat features that concentrate foraging insects near living
areas: open bins, unpicked fruit, exposed compost, and outdoor eating areas
positioned under fruit trees.

And, since we are walking the grounds anyway, the things that matter for
everything else we do — timber on soil, harborage against the foundation,
vegetation touching the building, and where water is going.

None of that is upselling. It is what a proper look at a property produces, and
you are free to do nothing with any of it.

## Telling it apart from other nests

**Paper wasps** build small, open, umbrella-shaped nests with visible cells and
no outer shell, usually under eaves, rails and in sheds. Much smaller colonies
and considerably less defensive.

**Yellowjackets** in the ground or in a wall void are the same family but a
different job entirely, with no visible structure.

**Honey bee swarms** are sometimes mistaken for a nest — a large clustered mass
of bees hanging from a branch, with no paper structure at all. That is a
temporary resting swarm looking for a home, it is not aggressive, and it belongs
to a beekeeper rather than to us. Call one; most are pleased to collect it.

**Mud dauber nests** are small tubes of dried mud on walls and under eaves, made
by a solitary wasp that is essentially harmless and does not defend anything.

**An old nest from a previous year**, which is weathered, faded, often torn open
by birds, and empty. If it is winter and there is no traffic, that is what you
are looking at.

## Living alongside one

For the nests that do not need removing, a few sensible measures make the rest of
the season straightforward.

Know where the flight line runs and stay off it. Mark the tree so visiting
trades, gardeners and family know.

Avoid running loud vibrating equipment directly beneath it — a mower passing is
usually fine, a chainsaw in the same tree is not.

Keep the food sources that draw foragers close to seating under control:
covered bins, fallen fruit picked up, drinks lidded in late summer.

And accept the trade. A hornet colony spends the summer removing a substantial
volume of flies, caterpillars and other insects from your garden. That is worth
something, and for a nest in a far corner of a property it is worth more than
the cost of removing it.

## Nests on buildings rather than in trees

A proportion of these end up on a house rather than in a tree, and the
calculation shifts when they do.

Under a soffit or a deep eave is the most common building position, usually
tucked where the overhang gives shelter from rain. Against a gable end, above a
porch, under a deck, in a carport and inside an open shed or barn all occur.

Three things change. Proximity is greater, so the defended radius overlaps with
doors, windows and walkways that people use daily. Access is often easier, which
makes the work more straightforward. And the nest is attached to something you
own, which raises the question of what it leaves behind — a paper nest removed
from siding leaves a stained patch that generally washes off, and one left in
place will shed material through the winter.

A nest on a building near an entrance is one of the clearest cases for removal.
A nest under a barn eave forty feet from anywhere anybody goes is not, and the
same seasonal logic applies as anywhere else.

One position deserves particular caution: a nest inside a shed, garage or barn
that people enter. An enclosed space removes the ability to back away, and it
is worth simply not using that building until the nest has been dealt with.

## Trees, pruning and next year

Two practical points about the tree itself, since that is where the nest is.

Treating a nest does not require damaging the tree, and it should not. Anybody
proposing to cut a limb to reach a nest is solving the wrong problem — the
colony ends in weeks either way and the limb does not grow back.

Pruning is worth thinking about for the season after, though. Dense low
branching, unmanaged hedging and shrubs that have grown into a solid mass are
exactly the sheltered structure a founding queen looks for in April. Ordinary
maintenance pruning, done in winter when nothing is nesting, reduces the number
of suitable sites without anybody treating anything.

The same goes for the general habit of walking the property in late spring.
A golf-ball nest with one insect in it in May is trivially dealt with. The same
colony in September is the thing people call about.
