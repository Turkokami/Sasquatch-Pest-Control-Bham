## Watch the hole for two minutes

Almost every question on this page answers itself if you stand back and look
properly before deciding anything.

Something is going in and out of the ground in your lawn, and the internet calls
all of it ground bees. Three quite different situations produce that, and they
need three different responses — one of which is urgent, two of which are
nothing at all.

The two-minute test:

**Many separate small holes, each with one insect, unhurried.** Mining bees.
Harmless, seasonal, and finished within weeks.

**One hole, with insects streaming in and out continuously and flying fast and
straight.** A yellowjacket nest. This is the one that matters.

**One hole, modest traffic, large furry insects.** Bumble bees. Small colony,
not aggressive, leave it.

## Mining bees, which are what ground bees usually means

Solitary, spring, and among the best pollinators in the garden.

A mining bee female digs her own burrow in bare, well-drained, sparsely
vegetated soil — the thin patch under a tree, the dry edge of a lawn, a slope
that never quite grew grass. She provisions it with pollen, lays, and leaves.
Hundreds of females may use the same good patch of ground, which from a distance
looks exactly like a colony and is not one. There is no shared nest, no workers,
and nothing to defend.

Which is why they do not sting. The females have a sting and almost never use
it, having nothing to protect; the males, which hover low over the ground in
spring looking for females and account for most of the alarming activity people
notice, cannot sting at all.

The season is short. Adults are above ground for a few weeks in spring, and by
early summer the holes are quiet and the lawn has grown over them. The usual
best response is to do nothing and let it happen.

If you want fewer next year, the answer is grass rather than insecticide.
Mining bees need thin, dry, bare soil. Overseeding a patch, improving the
drainage that made it bare in the first place, or simply watering it more
removes the habitat. That is also the only approach that lasts, because killing
this year's adults does not change what the ground looks like next March.

## Bumble bees in a hole, which are also fine

Different animal, same answer.

Bumble bees frequently nest underground, typically in an abandoned rodent
burrow, under a shed, in a compost heap or inside an old mouse nest in long
grass. The colony is small — tens of bees rather than the thousands in a
yellowjacket nest — and they are not inclined to defend it aggressively.

We do not treat them. That is set out on
[bee removal](/services/bee-removal/) and on
[bumble bee](/pest-library/bumble-bee/), and it is policy rather than a favor:
bumble bee populations are under real pressure and a nest under a shed in July
is dead of natural causes by October. If a nest is somewhere genuinely
unworkable — a doorway, a child's sandpit — call us and we will talk about what
is actually possible, which is usually less than people hope.

## Yellowjackets, which are the ones worth acting on

Not bees at all, and the reason this page exists.

A yellowjacket nest in the ground is a colony of thousands by late summer, built
in an old rodent burrow or a cavity under a bank, with a single entrance. The
insects are smooth rather than furry, boldly banded yellow and black, narrow at
the waist, and they fly fast and directly in and out of the hole in a continuous
stream.

The danger is specific and it is vibration. Mowing over a ground nest, running a
strimmer along a bank or wheeling a barrow across one in August produces an
immediate defensive response from a large colony, and that is the scenario
behind nearly every serious stinging incident we attend. Unlike a honey bee, a
yellowjacket can sting repeatedly.

The full treatment of that problem — how to find the entrance, why dusk matters,
what not to pour down it, and when it is reasonable to leave it alone — is on
[yellowjackets nesting in the lawn](/services/wasp-control/yellowjackets-in-the-lawn/),
and the species itself is at
[western yellowjacket](/pest-library/western-yellowjacket/).

## Telling them apart at a glance

If you can get a photograph, these are the things to look for.

- **Hair.** Bees are visibly furry. Yellowjackets are smooth and shiny.
- **Waist.** A yellowjacket's waist is sharply narrow. A bee's body is
  continuous.
- **Markings.** Yellowjacket banding is bright, crisp and high-contrast. Mining
  bees are mostly brown, tawny or black, sometimes with pale bands of hair that
  look softer.
- **Legs in flight.** Yellowjackets let their legs hang. Bees tuck them.
- **Pollen.** A bee carrying visible pollen on its legs or underside is a bee.
  Yellowjackets never do.
- **Month.** Mining bees peak in April and May. Yellowjacket ground nests peak
  in August and September.

That last one is worth weighting heavily. A hole full of activity in April is
almost certainly bees. The same lawn in late August is a different question.

## What to do, by situation

**Mining bees, any month.** Nothing. Let the few weeks pass, then thicken the
grass if you would rather not have them next year.

**Bumble bees, anywhere reasonable.** Nothing, and the colony is gone by
autumn.

**Yellowjackets away from paths and doorways.** A legitimate judgment call.
The colony dies at the first hard frost and nothing overwinters in the nest. If
nobody has to go near it, leaving it is a real option.

**Yellowjackets near a path, a gate, a door, a play area or anywhere a mower
goes.** Deal with it. This is the configuration that injures people.

**Anyone in the household with a sting allergy.** Deal with it regardless of
where the nest is.

And if you are not sure which of the three you have, send a photograph of the
hole and of the insect if you can get one. We will tell you which it is, and
most of the time the answer will be that it is a bee and you should leave it
exactly where it is.
