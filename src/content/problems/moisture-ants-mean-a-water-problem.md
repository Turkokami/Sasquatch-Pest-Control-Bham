## An ant that works as a diagnosis

Most pest findings tell you that something got in. Moisture ants tell you
something quite specific about the building itself, and that makes them unusual
and, in an odd way, useful.

They will not nest in sound dry timber. It is not a preference; the biology
requires wood that is genuinely wet and has already begun to break down through
decay. Where they are, that condition exists. Where that condition does not
exist, they are not.

So a moisture ant finding is a marker. It says: at this location, in this piece
of wood, there is enough persistent water for rot to have taken hold. It does
this with far more precision than a damp meter reading and considerably more
reliably than looking at a wall.

People ask how wet is wet, and the honest answer involves two numbers rather
than one. Framing lumber in a dry building sits somewhere around twelve percent
moisture content. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory places serious decay above
the fiber saturation point of wood, which averages about thirty percent, and
treats air-dry timber held under roughly twenty percent as carrying a sensible
margin against fungal damage.

Twenty is the level you want to stay beneath. Thirty is the level at which
decay is genuinely running. Those two get transposed in a great deal of pest
writing, and the transposition changes what a meter reading means: twenty-two
percent in a sill plate is not proof of rot, it is a sill plate that is wetter
than it should be and moving the wrong way. Moisture ants follow the fungi
rather than the water directly, so a colony marks the far end of that range and
not the near edge of it.

The unwelcome part is what it implies about how long the water has been there.
Decay does not develop over a wet weekend. By the time timber is soft enough to
support a colony, the moisture has generally been present for a matter of years.

## What they look like

They are easy to mistake for other small ants until you know the tells.

Small — noticeably smaller than a carpenter ant, and smaller than most of the
ants that come into a kitchen. Yellowish, light amber or pale brown rather than
black or dark red, which is the most reliable field mark. Slow-moving and not
especially defensive. Frequently found in dense concentration in one place
rather than trailing across a room.

The swarming stage is often the first sighting. Winged reproductives emerge
indoors, typically in late summer or autumn, and produce a sudden cloud of small
flying ants in a bathroom, a basement, a utility room or around a window. This
is what triggers most calls, and it is alarming out of proportion to the ants
themselves. What it is telling you is that a mature colony is established inside
the structure, which is the part worth attending to.

A second sign, less known and quite distinctive, is the material they build.
Moisture ants produce a carton-like construction from chewed wood and soil, and
where they have been established in a wall or a sill for some time you can find
a spongy, layered, papery mass in the timber cavity that looks nothing like the
clean sanded galleries a carpenter ant leaves.

## Where the water usually is

In this county, the places that produce moisture ant findings are consistent
enough to list.

**The sill plate and rim joist in a crawlspace.** By some margin the most common.
This is the timber that sits directly on the foundation, and it takes water from
splash-back, from poor grading, from a foundation that wicks, and from
persistent crawlspace humidity. It is also structural, which is why we look at
it on every visit.

**Under a shower, a bath or a toilet.** A slow failure at a pan, a waste
connection or a wax ring can run for years without ever appearing as a visible
leak, keeping the subfloor and joists wet the whole time.

**Below a window or a door that has taken driven rain.** Failed sealant,
missing flashing or a sill without slope. The same conditions that produce
carpenter ants in a frame produce moisture ants when the water is heavier and
longer-standing.

**Around a roof penetration.** A chimney flashing, a plumbing vent boot, a
skylight curb or a poorly sealed fan outlet. Roof water tracks along framing
before it shows on a ceiling, so the wet timber is frequently some distance from
any stain.

**Deck ledgers and posts at ground contact.** Where a deck is attached to a
house without proper flashing, the ledger and the rim behind it stay wet
permanently. Posts set directly into soil rot from the bottom.

**Under a leaking exterior faucet or a hose bib**, which is a small volume of
water in exactly the wrong place, arriving every time somebody waters the
garden.

**In an unventilated or wet crawlspace generally**, where no single leak exists
but the humidity is high enough, for long enough, to produce decay across a
broad area.

## The condensation cases

Not every water problem is a leak, and the ones that are not tend to go
undiagnosed longest.

Crawlspaces without a functioning vapor barrier take moisture continuously out
of the ground. In this climate that is a large volume of water over a year, and
it is enough on its own to keep framing at a moisture content that supports
decay.

Extract fans discharging into an attic rather than through the roof or wall push
warm wet air directly into a cold space, where it condenses on the underside of
the sheathing and runs down onto the framing. We find this regularly, and the
householder is usually surprised because there is no leak to find.

Cold metal in contact with timber — ductwork, pipework, some window assemblies —
condenses in winter and keeps the adjacent wood damp through the season.

Air leakage from a heated interior into an unheated cavity does the same thing
in wall assemblies, particularly around unsealed ceiling and wall penetrations.

All of these are water problems. None of them involves a drip you could point
at.

## Why treating them first is the wrong order

We treat moisture ants where it is warranted. We do not lead with it, and the
reason is simple arithmetic.

The colony is there because the wood is wet. If the wood stays wet, the site
stays suitable, and it will be recolonized — by the same species, from the same
sources, generally within a season or two. Treating without repairing buys you a
quiet year and a repeat call.

Worse, treating alone removes the indicator. The ants were pointing at a
structural problem. Silence them and the decay carries on unobserved, which in
a sill plate or a rim joist is a genuinely expensive way to save a small amount
of money.

The order that works is: find the water, understand its extent, repair it,
replace what has been lost, and then treat if anything remains. Frequently
nothing remains, because a colony in timber that has been cut out and replaced
has gone with it.

## What the repair usually involves

We are not builders and we do not quote building work. What we do is identify
precisely what is wet and why, so that the right trade is fixing the right
thing.

The common repairs, in rough order of frequency: correcting grade so soil falls
away from the foundation rather than toward it; extending downspouts so roof
water discharges well clear; installing or repairing a crawlspace vapor barrier;
clearing and, where needed, adding crawl ventilation; replacing failed sealant
and installing missing flashing at windows, doors and roof penetrations;
rerouting a bathroom or kitchen fan to discharge outside; repairing the plumbing
failure behind a wet subfloor; and cutting out and replacing timber that has
lost structural capacity.

That last one is the item people most want to avoid and most often need. Decayed
sill plate, rim joist or post material does not recover when it dries. It has
lost strength permanently and the only remedy is replacement.

Where we can, we will tell you the difference between wood that is wet and
sound, wood that is superficially decayed, and wood that has gone. The blunt
probe test is most of it — sound timber resists a firm push, decayed timber
gives, and material that has gone will let a probe sink in without resistance.

## What else you tend to find alongside them

A wet crawlspace or a wet wall cavity supports a whole community, and moisture
ants are usually not traveling alone.

Springtails, in very large numbers, which are harmless and are themselves a
reliable damp indicator. Sowbugs and pillbugs, which need moisture to survive
and cannot persist in a dry space. Millipedes and centipedes. Silverfish.
Various fungus-feeding beetles.

Above that layer, everything that eats those things: spiders in quantity, and
the general elevation of insect activity that makes a damp building feel busier
than a dry one.

And in the timber itself, the decay fungi that started the whole process, which
in a crawlspace are frequently visible as discoloration, a white or brown
growth, or a cubical cracking pattern in the wood.

So a single moisture ant finding often opens into a broader damp-building
conversation. That is not us expanding the scope. It is what the finding
genuinely means.

## Telling them apart from the alternatives

Three things get confused with moisture ants and each changes the answer.

**Carpenter ants.** Much larger, dark, and able to work timber that is damp
rather than saturated. They are the more structurally significant of the two and
the treatment involves an outdoor search for a parent colony, which moisture
ants do not require in the same way.

**Pavement ants and other small household species.** Similar in size, but darker,
faster, and found trailing to food rather than concentrated in wet timber. These
are a kitchen problem, not a building problem.

**Termites.** Dampwood termites occupy overlapping conditions and are found in
similar places. The distinguishing signs are the debris — hard six-sided pellets
rather than carton material — and the damage pattern, with galleries running
across the grain in a way ant work does not. Where a wood-destroying organism has
been identified in somebody's report, treating it is squarely within what we are
certified to carry out, and we will tell you plainly which of these you have.

Getting this right on site matters more here than in most situations, because
the three carry very different implications for the building.

## If they have just swarmed indoors

A sudden emergence of small winged ants inside a room is the most common way
this problem introduces itself, and it is worth knowing what to do in the hour
it is happening.

Vacuum them up. They are not dangerous, they will not bite, and the winged
reproductives that emerge indoors almost never establish a new colony inside a
house — they are trying to get outside. Removing them is cosmetic and it is
fine.

Before you clear them all, keep a dozen in a sealed bag or a jar. Identification
from a specimen is straightforward and it removes all the guesswork from the
visit.

Note exactly where they came from. A swarm exits through a specific point — a
gap at a baseboard, a crack at a window return, a light fitting, a bathroom
fan, the edge of a floor. That point is directly connected to the nest, and
therefore to the wet timber, which is what we actually need to find. People
frequently clean up thoroughly and then cannot remember which side of the room
it started on.

Resist the urge to seal the exit point immediately. It is the most useful thing
in the building right now.

And do not read the size of the swarm as the size of the problem. A mature
colony releases reproductives in one concentrated event, so a dramatic emergence
and a modest one can come from very similar situations.

## Why this is a common finding here

Whatcom County produces more of this than most places, and the reasons are
climatic and structural rather than anything to do with housekeeping.

The rainfall pattern matters more than the total. Long, mild, persistently wet
autumns and winters mean that timber which gets wet stays wet, because there is
little in the way of a hot dry season to drive the moisture back out. A wall
that would dry out in a fortnight elsewhere may not dry out here between October
and April.

Crawlspace construction is close to universal in the older housing stock, and a
crawlspace is a moisture management system whether or not anybody has treated it
as one. Where the vapor barrier is missing, torn or absent from the start, the
ground gives up water into the space continuously.

Grade is the third factor. A great many lots here fall toward the building
rather than away, either by original siting or because landscaping has been
built up over decades, and the result is water sitting against a foundation for
months at a time.

And a substantial share of the housing predates modern flashing practice
entirely, so the details at windows, decks and roof penetrations were never
built to the standard that would keep water out of the assembly for fifty years.

None of that is anybody's fault. It is the reason we look at the same handful of
places on every single visit, and it is why the fix is nearly always about water
movement rather than chemistry.

## When a sale is involved

Moisture ants come up in property transactions more often than any other insect
we deal with here, because the places they occupy are exactly the places a
buyer's inspector looks.

Where a buyer's report has identified moisture ant activity and decayed
structural timber, what is usually being asked for is a repair and a treatment,
in that order. The repair belongs to a builder. The treatment side of a
wood-destroying organism finding is within what we are certified to carry out,
and we are glad to handle that part.

What we will not do is give you a document that reads like something it is not.
We are not structural pest inspectors and we do not issue those reports. If the
transaction needs one, that is a separate professional and we will say so
plainly rather than blur the line to keep the work.

The practical advice for sellers is the same as for anybody else, only earlier:
the water problem behind a moisture ant colony does not improve while a house is
on the market, and it is considerably cheaper to address in your own time than
during a negotiation.

## What we will not sell you

**A treatment as a substitute for a repair.** It does not work and it hides the
thing that matters.

**A recurring plan for an ant that is only present because of a leak.**

**A structural opinion we are not qualified to give.** We will tell you what the
timber feels like under a probe and where the water is coming from. Load paths
and repairs are an engineer's or a builder's call.

**Alarm.** Moisture ants are not aggressive, they do not damage sound wood, and
the underlying problem is usually fixable. The seriousness lies in the decay,
and the decay is generally addressable if it is found.

## What happens when you call

We look for water before we look for ants.

That means the crawlspace in full with a light, the sill plate and rim joist
probed rather than glanced at, the plumbing runs and the areas below wet rooms,
the window and door openings, the roof penetrations from below where they can be
seen, and the exterior — grade, downspouts, hose bibs, deck attachments and
anywhere the ground is holding water against the building.

Then we look at the timber and tell you what condition it is actually in.

You get a plain account: where the water is coming from, how far the damage
extends, what needs replacing, which trade should do it, and whether any
treatment from us is warranted at all. On a fair number of these visits the
honest answer is that you need a plumber, a roofer or a drainage contractor and
not a pest control company, and we will say so.

Assessment is free, there is no term agreement, and we are not going to sell you
a recurring plan for an ant that is only there because your sill plate is wet.
