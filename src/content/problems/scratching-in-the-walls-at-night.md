## The sound is information

Almost every call that starts with scratching in a wall begins the same way: it
is dark, the house is quiet, somebody is lying in bed, and there is a small
persistent noise on the other side of the plaster.

It is an unsettling thing to listen to and it is also useful data. Before
anybody comes out, the sound itself narrows the field considerably — when it
happens, where in the structure it is, and what kind of noise it is. Those three
things tell an experienced person most of what they need to know.

So it is worth going through them properly, because a homeowner who pays
attention for two nights can usually describe the problem better than a
technician can guess it.

## When it happens

Rodents are crepuscular and nocturnal, which in practice means two activity
peaks: shortly after dark and again in the hours before dawn.

If the scratching starts within an hour of sunset, runs in bursts, quiets in the
middle of the night and picks up again around four or five in the morning, that
is a textbook rodent pattern. It is what we hear described most often and it is
the one we handle.

Noise that is loudest in the middle of the day, particularly in the roof space,
usually is not a rodent. Daytime activity overhead points toward animals that
are outside what we do, and we would rather tell you that on the phone than
arrive, take a fee and refer you on afterwards.

Noise that is constant, unvarying and mechanical is worth ruling out before you
call anybody. Ductwork, a loose vent damper, expansion in a flue and water
movement in pipes have all been mistaken for animals, and they do not follow the
dusk-and-dawn pattern.

## Where in the wall

Height matters more than people expect.

**Low in the wall, near the floor.** Most often mice or rats moving up from a
crawlspace. The wall cavity connects to the floor structure at the bottom plate,
and any penetration through that plate — a pipe, a wire, a duct boot — is an
open door between the two spaces. Low noise almost always means the underfloor
space is worth looking at first.

**High in the wall, or at the ceiling line.** Usually something moving between a
wall cavity and the attic, using the top plate the same way. Roof rats in
particular are comfortable climbing and will happily work the vertical cavities
of a house.

**Behind a kitchen or bathroom wall specifically.** Those walls carry the most
plumbing and therefore the most holes, and they also carry the warmth and the
food smell. A disproportionate share of the calls we take involve one of those
two rooms.

**Moving.** If the noise travels along a wall over a few seconds, you are
hearing an animal use a run. That is the most helpful description you can give
us, because it tells us the cavity is a corridor rather than a nest, and it
points at where the corridor goes.

## What the noise sounds like

**Scratching and scrabbling.** Claws on framing or on the back of the drywall.
Generic to all rodents, and the most commonly reported.

**Gnawing.** A harder, more rhythmic, more grinding sound. Rodent incisors grow
continuously and get worn down on whatever is available, which regrettably
includes timber, plastic and cable sheathing. Gnawing is the sound that should
move a job up your list, because it is the one that ends in damage.

**Fast scurrying.** Light, quick and brief — typically mice. A house mouse
weighs less than an ounce and sounds like it.

**Heavier, slower movement, sometimes with a thump.** More consistent with rats.
People often describe it as sounding "too big to be a mouse," and they are
usually right.

**Squeaking, or many small sounds at once.** A nest with young rather than a
single animal passing through, which changes both the urgency and the amount of
trapping involved.

## Why the wall is a hallway, not a home

Here is the thing most homeowners have backwards, and it changes what a sensible
response looks like.

Rodents very rarely nest inside a wall cavity by choice. A stud bay is narrow,
it has poor access to food, and it is a difficult place to raise young. What it
is, is an excellent protected route between the places they do want to be.

The nest is generally somewhere with more room: the crawlspace, the attic
insulation, a soffit, a boxed-in section of ceiling, the back of a cabinet run,
or an unused corner of a garage. The wall is how the animal gets from there to
your kitchen and back.

This matters practically. Cutting into the wall you can hear is nearly always
the wrong move — you get a hole in your plaster and no animal, because the
animal is not there during the day. The work that resolves the problem happens
at the ends of the corridor and at the point where something got into the
building in the first place.

## What is actually in there

In Whatcom County the realistic list is short.

**Norway rats.** Ground-oriented, heavy-bodied, strong burrowers. They favor
crawlspaces, the underside of decks, and the ground floor of a building, and
they move up into walls from below. Most of the rat work in the county is this
species.

**Roof rats.** Present locally, more of a climber, more likely to be operating
in the upper structure and coming in at the roofline rather than at ground
level. If the noise is high and there are branches touching the roof, this is
the candidate.

**House mice.** The most common thing in a wall by a wide margin. They will use
an opening a quarter of an inch across, they breed quickly, and a "single mouse"
is usually not a single mouse.

**Deer mice.** More common where a property backs onto woodland or open ground,
and more of a seasonal problem — they push into buildings when the weather
turns rather than living inside year-round. Their droppings and nesting material
warrant more careful cleanup than a house mouse's, which we cover in the work
rather than leaving to you.

And a fourth possibility we will name plainly: something we do not handle. If
what is in your roof turns out to be wildlife, that is not our department. We do
insects and rodents. We will tell you what you appear to have, and point you
toward somebody equipped for it, at no charge and with no attempt to sell you a
rodent program on the way past.

## Why it started now

Rodent calls in this county are strongly seasonal, and the pattern is worth
knowing.

The push begins as nights cool in September, peaks through October and November,
and stays elevated all winter. What changes is not the number of animals outside
but their motivation — cover dies back, natural food declines, and a heated
building with a quarter-inch gap becomes considerably more attractive than it
was in July.

Which is why the scratching that starts in mid-October so often follows a
completely uneventful summer. Nothing about your house changed. The weather did.

A secondary trigger is disturbance. Site clearing, demolition, a neighbor's
drainage work, a hedge coming out — anything that removes established harborage
nearby will push animals to find new accommodation, and buildings within a
couple of hundred feet get the traffic.

## What not to do

**Do not put poison into a wall cavity.** This is the single most common
self-inflicted problem we get called to fix. The animal dies where you cannot
reach it, and a decomposing rodent inside a closed cavity produces a smell that
lasts for weeks and attracts flies on top of it. Sometimes the only remedy is
opening the wall to find it.

**Do not rely on ultrasonic devices.** They are widely sold and there is no
credible evidence they work. Animals habituate to sound quickly, and a rodent
with an established food source is not deterred by a noise.

**Do not seal the building while animals are inside it.** Exclusion is the right
answer, but the sequence matters. Close everything with a population still
indoors and you have trapped them in with your insulation and wiring rather than
outside where they belong.

**Do not cut an exploratory hole.** As above, the animal is not home during the
day, and you will have made a hole for nothing.

**Do not wait for it to stop.** Silence is not resolution. It usually means the
activity moved to a part of the structure you cannot hear from your bedroom.

## What we actually do about it

The work has three parts and they run in a deliberate order.

**First, find the way in.** This is the whole job, in the sense that everything
else is temporary without it. We work the exterior systematically at ground
level and up: foundation and grade, crawlspace vents and their screens, the
crawl access hatch, utility penetrations where pipes and cables enter, the gap
under a poorly hung garage door, where a deck ledger meets the wall, the joint
between an addition and the original building, and along the roofline where
soffits, fascia and roof-wall junctions frequently have openings nobody has ever
looked at.

Inside we look at the routes rather than the wall you can hear — the crawlspace
in full rather than from the hatch, the attic at the hatch and further where it
is safe, the plumbing chases, and the underside of cabinet runs.

**Second, remove what is inside.** Trapping, not poison, for anything within the
building envelope. Trapping means we know how many animals came out, we take
them away, and nothing dies in a place you cannot reach. Placement matters
enormously here: traps set on the actual runs, in the actual voids, checked and
adjusted rather than laid out and forgotten. Rats in particular are wary of new
objects and often need a few days before they will commit.

**Third, close the building.** Sealing done in materials that hold — metal mesh,
proper sheet metal, mortar where appropriate, correctly fitted vent covers and
crawl access — not expanding foam on its own, which a rodent goes through in an
evening. This is the part that decides whether you are calling somebody again
next October.

Where insulation has been contaminated, we will tell you honestly whether it
needs to come out or whether it can be left. That is a real cost and we do not
recommend it lightly, but there are situations where leaving fouled material in
a roof space is the wrong call, and we would rather have that conversation
directly.

## Two things you can check tonight

Neither of these requires tools, and both make the visit shorter.

**Put flour or talc down.** A light dusting on a hard surface where you suspect
traffic — the crawlspace hatch, the back of a cabinet under a sink, along a
baseboard in the room where the noise is loudest — will hold prints by morning.
Tail drag between the prints suggests a rat. Small, closely spaced prints
suggest a mouse. Nothing at all in three nights is itself useful, because it
moves attention to a different part of the building.

**Note the exact time twice.** Not "some time in the night" but the actual clock
time the noise starts and stops, on two separate nights. A consistent onset
tracking sunset is a rodent. Sound that begins whenever the heating comes on is
probably not an animal at all.

## Why the houses here leak where they do

Whatcom County housing has a few recurring weaknesses that account for a large
share of what we find, and knowing them helps you understand why we go where we
go.

Crawlspace vents are the standout. Older buildings were fitted with vents whose
screens have rusted or been broken by yard equipment, and a failed vent screen
is not a small gap. It is an open doorway sized for the animal we are looking
for. Replacing them in metal is one of the highest-value pieces of work on a
typical property here.

Crawl access hatches come second. A great many of them close approximately
rather than properly, and a hatch that has been off and back on a dozen times
for utility work rarely seals afterwards.

Then the penetrations. Every building has a set of holes drilled for water,
waste, gas, power, cable and heat pump lines, and the great majority of them
were never sealed to any standard beyond keeping the weather out. A gap that
looks tidy from the outside frequently opens into a floor cavity.

Additions are the fourth. Wherever a later structure meets an older one, there
is a junction, and junctions built at different times to different standards are
where openings survive undetected for decades.

Garage doors round it out. A door whose bottom seal has hardened, split or worn
at the corners leaves a genuine entry, and the garage is very often connected to
the rest of the house by more openings than anybody realizes.

None of that is a criticism of the housing stock. It is simply a list of where
to look, and it is why an hour spent on the outside of a building is worth more
than an afternoon spent listening at the wall.

## What to expect on timing

For a mouse situation in a reasonably sound building, the usual shape is a
couple of weeks: trapping running while the sealing work is done, activity
dropping off through it, then a quiet check afterwards.

Rats take longer, sometimes considerably. They are neophobic — genuinely
suspicious of anything new in their environment — and a rat that has decided a
trap is a threat will step around it for days. Anyone quoting you a same-evening
resolution on a rat in a wall is telling you what you want to hear.

Buildings with structural difficulties — an inaccessible crawlspace, a complex
roofline, an addition with a poorly closed junction — take longer again, and we
would rather say that at the start.

## A note on the noise itself

People ask us fairly often whether they should be worried, or just annoyed.

The honest answer is that the risk from the noise is low and the risk from what
is causing it is not nothing. Rodents gnaw, and cable sheathing is one of the
materials they gnaw. They contaminate insulation and stored goods. They carry
parasites and pathogens, and they are prolific enough that a small problem
becomes a larger one over a winter rather than staying still.

None of which is a reason to panic at two in the morning. It is a reason to have
somebody look while the problem is still one animal and one gap.
