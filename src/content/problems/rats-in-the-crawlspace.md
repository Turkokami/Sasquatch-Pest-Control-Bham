## Why the crawlspace and not somewhere else

If you were a rat choosing accommodation in Whatcom County, you would pick the
crawlspace.

It is dark all day. Nobody goes in it, sometimes for years at a stretch. It sits
directly beneath a heated floor, so it is warmer than the ground outside. It is
usually lined with fiberglass insulation, which is close to ideal nesting
material. It has a vapor barrier to burrow beneath. It has water in the form of
condensation or a damp corner. And it opens to the outside world through a row
of vents that were installed for airflow rather than security.

That combination is why rats under the floor is the single most common
substantial rodent job we do here, and why it is different in kind from finding
one in a wall. A wall cavity is a corridor. A crawlspace is a home, and the
population living in it is often established rather than passing through.

## How you find out

Very few people discover this by going down and looking. It usually announces
itself another way.

Sounds from below the floor, particularly in the evening and again toward
morning, and particularly in a room over the lowest part of the crawl. Rats are
heavy enough to be audible through a floor in a way mice generally are not.

A smell that develops slowly and is hard to place. Established rat activity
produces a distinctive musty ammonia odor, and it often reaches the living space
through the same gaps the animals use.

Droppings appearing in a garage, a utility room, or the back of a low cabinet —
the places where the underfloor space connects upward.

A dog or cat that has become very interested in one particular part of the
floor and will not let it go.

Or the honest version: a contractor, a plumber or a heat pump technician went
under the house for something unrelated and came back up with news.

## What they are doing down there

Understanding the pattern makes the response make sense.

Norway rats are the usual species in this situation. They are burrowers by
preference and they operate at ground level, which is exactly what a crawlspace
offers on the inside of a building.

They establish runs — consistent routes worn along the same lines, typically
following the edges of the space, the tops of foundation walls, the ducting and
the perimeter rather than crossing open ground. Those runs are visible as smear
marks on timber and concrete, as flattened tracks in the dust on a vapor
barrier, and as concentrations of droppings.

They nest in the insulation, pulling it down out of the joist bays to build with
and leaving the bays bare. A crawlspace with insulation hanging in swathes and
piled in corners is not a fitting failure, whatever anyone has told you.

They gnaw, because they must. Timber, plastic pipe, cable sheathing, the vapor
barrier itself, and occasionally ducting all get worked on. The wiring is the
one that matters most and it is the one nobody sees.

And they move upward wherever the floor structure allows. Every pipe and cable
that passes through a bottom plate is a potential route into a wall, which is
why a crawlspace problem so often becomes a kitchen problem a few weeks later.

## How they got in

There is nearly always a specific answer, and in this county it is usually one
of five.

**Vent screens.** The most common by a distance. Older crawl vents were screened
in materials that corrode, and yard equipment, pressure washing and general age
finish the job. A rusted-through vent is not a small defect — it is an opening
sized for the animal.

**The crawl access hatch.** Frequently a sheet of plywood or a light door that
has been removed and replaced repeatedly for utility work and no longer sits
tight. Sometimes it is simply missing.

**Foundation penetrations.** Water supply, waste, gas, electrical, cable and
heat pump lines all pass through the wall somewhere, and the great majority were
sealed to keep weather out rather than animals.

**Burrowing under the foundation line.** Where a foundation is shallow, where a
slab-on-grade addition meets an older wall, or where landscaping has built the
grade up against a building, a Norway rat is entirely capable of digging its own
entrance.

**Deck and porch skirting.** Structures built against a house create a protected
void that connects to the crawl area, and skirting is very often decorative
rather than sealed.

We look at all five on every visit, because a building usually has more than one
and closing four of them is not a resolution.

## What is outside feeding it

A crawlspace population is supported by something above ground, and it is worth
being blunt about the usual suspects.

Bird feeders, which spill seed continuously. Pet food left out or stored in
permeable packaging in a garage. Compost that is open rather than contained.
Fallen fruit under an unpicked tree. Bins with hardened or missing lids.
Chicken feed, which in this county is a very frequent contributor. Stored
material — timber, pallets, tarps, unused equipment — sitting directly on the
ground within a short run of the building.

None of these cause a rat problem on their own. All of them make a marginal
property attractive, and dealing with them is the cheapest part of the whole
exercise.

## Telling rats from mice under a floor

The two need different responses, and the crawlspace usually makes the answer
obvious once you know what to look at.

**Droppings.** Mouse droppings are small, roughly the size of a grain of rice,
pointed at the ends and produced in very large numbers. Rat droppings are
substantially bigger — around half an inch or more — with blunter ends, and
appear in smaller numbers because there are fewer animals producing them.

**Runs.** Rats leave heavy, obvious sign: greasy smear marks where they pass
repeatedly against timber and concrete, worn tracks in dust, and clear
consistent routes. Mice are light enough that their traces are subtle and often
only visible in a dusty vapor barrier.

**Gnaw damage.** Rat gnawing is coarse, with visible individual tooth marks and
real material removal. Mouse gnawing is fine and shallow.

**Openings used.** A mouse gets through a quarter-inch gap. Extension guidance
puts a rat at slightly more than half an inch to pass through, though state
wildlife guidance is clear that a quarter inch is enough for one to gnaw an
opening up to size, and a rat is far more capable of enlarging what it finds.
So the size of the holes narrows the diagnosis without settling it: if quarter-
inch gaps are the only defect on an otherwise tight building, mice are the more
likely answer, but the sealing standard stays the same either way.

**Nesting.** Mice make discrete nests, roughly fist-sized, tucked into a
protected spot. Rats work at a different scale, pulling insulation down across
several joist bays.

It matters because the trap types differ, the number of traps differs
enormously, and the exclusion specification differs. Treating a mouse problem
like a rat problem leaves gaps open. Treating a rat problem like a mouse problem
leaves you badly under-trapped.

## What it does to the rest of the house

People are usually more concerned about the crawlspace than about the building
above it, and it is worth reversing that emphasis for a moment.

**Wiring.** Cable sheathing is a material rodents work on, and a crawlspace is
full of it — power runs, low-voltage cable, thermostat wiring, sometimes
communications. This is the damage that carries real consequence and it is
entirely invisible from inside the house.

**Ducting.** Flexible duct is easily breached. A hole in a supply run means you
are heating the crawlspace, and it also means air that has passed through a
contaminated space can reach living areas.

**Plumbing.** PEX and other plastic supply lines are chewable. A pinhole in a
supply line under a floor can run for a long time before anybody notices,
producing a wet crawl area and a substantial bill.

**Insulation performance.** Batts pulled down from joist bays are not insulating
anything. A crawlspace that has been worked over for a couple of winters is
measurably more expensive to heat above.

**The upward routes.** Every penetration through the bottom plate connects the
crawl area to a wall cavity, and every wall cavity connects to somewhere you
live. A crawlspace population that has been there a while very often produces a
kitchen sighting eventually, and by then two problems need solving.

## Coops, feed and the neighboring-property problem

Two things come up so often on properties in this county that they deserve their
own note.

Backyard poultry is common here and it is one of the most reliable rat
attractants there is — not the birds themselves but the feed, which is high
value, often available continuously, and frequently stored in bags rather than
metal. Almost every rural and semi-rural rat job we do has a feed component. The
fixes are practical: feed in metal containers with fitted lids, feeders lifted
and emptied at night rather than left full, spillage cleaned rather than
allowed to accumulate, and coop floors and skirting closed properly. You do not
have to give up the chickens. You do have to stop feeding everything else.

The second is that rats do not respect property lines. On smaller lots,
particularly in older parts of Bellingham and Ferndale, a population is often
supported by something two doors down — a neglected outbuilding, an unmanaged
compost heap, a vacant property, a feed source nobody has connected to the
problem. We can close your building completely, and we will, but where the
pressure is coming from next door the honest framing is that we are keeping
animals out rather than removing them from the area. Where a neighbor is willing
to have a conversation, that conversation is worth more than another visit from
us.

## The cleanup question, answered honestly

This is where the industry has a reputation problem, so we will be direct about
where the line sits.

Full crawlspace restoration — removing all insulation, replacing the vapor
barrier, sanitizing the space and reinsulating — is a genuinely large piece of
work. It is sometimes exactly right. It is also the single easiest thing to
oversell to somebody who has just been told there are rats under their bedroom.

Our position is that restoration is warranted when the contamination is
widespread rather than localized: insulation pulled down across a large
proportion of the joist bays, nesting material distributed through the space,
saturation rather than scattered droppings, a vapor barrier that has been
extensively burrowed under and torn, and an odor that persists after the animals
are gone.

It is not warranted for a modest number of droppings on otherwise sound
material in a space that is dry, and we will tell you that even standing in a
crawlspace that has just given somebody a fright.

Where the honest answer sits in between, we say so and let you decide. Sometimes
the right call is to remove and replace the worst two or three bays, clean up
the rest, and reassess in a year.

## Doing it in the right order

The sequence is not arbitrary and getting it wrong costs money.

**Trapping first, or at least alongside.** Snap traps set on the actual runs and
at the actual entry points, in numbers, checked and repositioned. Rats are
neophobic: they notice new objects in a familiar space and avoid them, sometimes
for several days. Placement, patience and enough traps are what make this work.
We do not use bait inside a building envelope, because a rat that dies in a
floor structure is a problem we then have to solve twice.

One practical wrinkle that catches people out: where a vapor barrier is in place,
rats frequently travel underneath it rather than on top, and a trap set on the
plastic sits above a runway it never touches. The tell is a raised, meandering
ridge in the sheeting. We cut in, set below, and patch the barrier afterward.

**Sealing second, once we know activity is down.** Metal mesh and sheet
material, mortar where appropriate, new vent covers in a specification that
lasts, a properly fitted crawl hatch, penetrations closed with something a
rodent cannot chew. Expanding foam alone is not a seal and anybody using it as
one is buying themselves a return visit.

**Restoration last, if it is needed at all.** There is no sense in installing new
insulation while an unresolved population is living under the floor, and no
sense in doing it before the building is closed. Where the work is needed, doing
it last means doing it once.

## Moisture, which is usually part of the story

A crawlspace with a rat problem very often has a water problem too, and the two
reinforce each other.

Damp ground supports the insects that other things eat. Damp insulation loses
its function and becomes more attractive as nesting material rather than less.
A wet space corrodes vent screens faster. And moisture in a crawlspace produces
its own separate list of problems — moisture ants, springtails, sowbugs and
decay in the framing, which in this climate takes structural capacity faster
than rodents do.

So a fair proportion of what we recommend under a house is not pest work at all.
Getting roof water discharged well away from the building. Correcting grade that
falls toward the foundation. Clearing vents that landscaping has buried.
Repairing a vapor barrier that is in pieces. Fixing the slow supply leak that
has kept one corner wet for three years.

We say it knowing it is not work we bill for, because a dry crawlspace is
harder to live in for everything we have just been discussing.

## Safety if you are going down yourself

Plenty of people want to look before they call, which is reasonable. A few
points worth having first.

The main concern with rodent droppings and nesting material is airborne
particulate from dry material, and the local species that matters for that is
the deer mouse rather than the rat. Either way the handling is the same:
ventilate the space before you go in, do not sweep or dry-vacuum, wet material
down with a disinfectant solution before disturbing it, use a fitted respirator
rather than a paper mask, wear gloves, and bag material rather than piling it.

Beyond that, crawlspaces have their own hazards that have nothing to do with
animals — protruding fasteners, exposed wiring, standing water near electrical
work, low headroom and no easy exit. If any of that describes yours, it is a
reasonable moment to let somebody else do it.

## What it takes and how long

For a typical single-family house here with an established but not extreme
crawlspace population, the shape of it is a few weeks: trapping running while
the exterior gets closed, activity dropping through the second and third week,
then a check to confirm.

Buildings that take longer are the ones with structural complications — a crawl
that cannot be fully accessed, a foundation that a rat is genuinely burrowing
under, several connected structures, or a neighboring property that is
supplying animals faster than we can exclude them.

We would rather set that expectation at the beginning than let you believe a
single visit ends it. It occasionally does. More often it does not, and a
company that pretends otherwise is one you will be calling again.
