## Not really a pest problem

Small dark flies drifting up when you water, sitting on the soil surface,
appearing at windows, and generally being present in a way they were not a month
ago. Fungus gnats are one of the most common indoor insect complaints there is
and one of the least serious.

They are a symptom of a growing habit rather than an invasion of a building.
Their larvae live in the top layer of potting medium and feed on fungi, algae
and decaying organic matter, all of which flourish when soil is kept
persistently wet. Where soil stays damp, they thrive. Where it dries out
regularly, they cannot complete a generation.

That is the whole mechanism, and it means the solution is a watering change
rather than a treatment. We are aware this is a page from a pest control company
telling you that you probably do not need a pest control company. It is still
the accurate answer, and the useful thing we can do here is give you the method
that works.

## What they are

Adults are small — a few millimeters — dark gray or black, with long legs and
long antennae relative to their body, and a weak fluttering flight. They are
poor fliers, which is why they tend to stay near the plants rather than
distributing through a house, though they are drawn to windows and screens.

Adults live only about a week and do not feed meaningfully. Their entire
function is reproduction, and a female lays somewhere in the region of a hundred
to two hundred eggs in damp soil.

Larvae are the stage that matters: small, translucent or whitish, with a
distinct black head capsule, living in the top inch or so of the growing medium.
They feed on fungi, algae, root hairs and organic debris.

The full cycle from egg to adult runs roughly three to four weeks at normal
indoor temperatures, which is why patience is required and why results appear
in the second or third week rather than the second or third day.

## Do they damage plants

Mostly no, with specific exceptions worth knowing.

On established houseplants with developed root systems, fungus gnat larvae are
essentially a nuisance. They are feeding on the fungal and organic component of
the soil rather than on the plant.

Where they genuinely cause harm is with seedlings, fresh cuttings, and plants
with very fine or delicate root systems. Larvae will feed on root hairs and can
damage or kill young plants outright. Anyone propagating from seed or striking
cuttings has a real reason to control them.

Heavy populations can also spread soil-borne pathogens between pots, which
matters more in a greenhouse or a serious collection than on a windowsill.

For most households the honest assessment is that the plants are fine and the
flies are annoying, which changes how much effort is worth spending.

## The method that works

In order of importance.

**Let the soil dry out.** The top inch to two inches should become genuinely dry
between waterings. Not damp — dry. This is the whole intervention. Eggs and young
larvae in a dry surface layer do not survive, and the cycle breaks.

Push a finger in to check rather than judging by the surface, which looks drier
than it is. For most houseplants this simply means watering less often than
people do; overwatering is the most common houseplant error by a wide margin and
fungus gnats are its most visible consequence.

**Water from below where you can.** Standing a pot in a tray to take up water
leaves the surface layer drier while the roots still get what they need. Empty
the tray afterwards rather than leaving the pot standing in water.

**Empty the saucers.** Standing water under a pot keeps the base of the medium
saturated indefinitely.

**Improve drainage.** A pot with no drainage hole, a compacted medium, or a
saucer full of accumulated fines will hold water no matter how carefully you
water. Repotting into a free-draining medium fixes several problems at once.

**Cover the surface.** A half-inch layer of horticultural grit, coarse sand or
fine gravel on top of the soil is a physical barrier: adults cannot easily reach
the medium to lay, and emerging adults struggle to get out. It also keeps the
visible surface dry. This is genuinely effective and it looks better than soil.

**Yellow sticky traps.** Adults are strongly attracted to yellow. Traps set at
soil level catch large numbers, reduce the number of eggs being laid, and — most
usefully — give you a measure. Change them weekly and count. Falling numbers
means it is working.

**Bti, if you want to accelerate it.** A biological larvicide, sold as mosquito
bits or dunks, based on a bacterium specific to fly larvae. Steep it in the
watering can and apply as normal watering. It is target-specific, harmless to
plants, people and pets, and it deals with the larval stage while the drying
regime takes effect.

**Repot the worst offenders.** Where a plant has been in saturated medium for a
long time, removing as much of the old medium as practical and repotting into
fresh, free-draining material resets it. Check for root rot while you are there,
since the conditions that produce gnats also produce that.

## What does not work

**Spraying the adults.** They live a week and die anyway. Killing the ones flying
around does nothing about the several hundred larvae in the pot, and it puts
insecticide on plants and surfaces indoors for no benefit.

**Fogging or bug bombs.** Same reasoning, considerably more product, considerably
less point.

**Cinnamon, vinegar, dish soap and the rest of the internet remedies.** Some have
a marginal effect on fungal growth at the surface. None reliably clears a
population, and several risk the plant.

**Hydrogen peroxide drenches.** These are widely recommended and they do kill
larvae on contact. They also disrupt beneficial soil biology, they need
repeating, and they do not address the wetness that caused the problem. If the
watering does not change, the gnats come back. It is a treatment for a symptom.

**Letting a plant wilt.** Drying the top layer is the aim; drought-stressing the
plant is not, and people occasionally overcorrect and lose the plant to the
cure.

## Where they came from

Almost always with new material rather than through a window.

**New plants.** By far the most common route. Nursery and greenhouse growing
conditions are ideal for fungus gnats, and a proportion of plants arrive with
eggs or larvae in the medium. Quarantining new plants away from the collection
for a few weeks is a habit worth having if you buy often.

**New potting medium.** Bagged compost and potting soil, particularly if stored
damp or opened and left in a garage or a shed, can carry them.

**Repotting into old medium**, or topping up with material from an open bag that
has been sitting around.

**Outdoors in summer**, where plants moved onto a patio pick them up and bring
them back in.

Occasionally there is no plant involved at all, and that possibility is worth
checking before you spend three weeks adjusting your watering.

## When the plants are not the source

A few situations produce the same small flies from somewhere structural, and
these are the ones where calling somebody makes sense.

**A leak keeping something wet.** Under a sink, behind a dishwasher, at a
supply line, or a slow failure in a wall. Damp organic material anywhere will
support them.

**A wet crawlspace.** A saturated underfloor space with a failed or absent vapor
barrier produces fungus gnats and a whole community of other moisture-driven
insects — springtails, sowbugs, millipedes, silverfish. If you have all of those
along with the gnats, the crawlspace is worth looking at rather than the plants.

**A blocked condensate drain** on a heat pump or an air handler, which produces a
small permanently wet reservoir of exactly the right kind.

**Compost or organic material stored indoors** — a bin, a sack, a bag of
potting soil in a utility room.

The diagnostic is simple: if the flies persist after four weeks of genuinely dry
surface soil, or if they are appearing in rooms with no plants in them, the
source is somewhere else.

## The flies that are not fungus gnats

Two lookalikes matter, and one of them is a real finding.

**Drain flies.** Small, fuzzy, distinctly moth-like, with broad rounded wings
held over the body, and a habit of sitting still on walls near sinks and showers
rather than flying around plants. They breed in the organic film lining drains
and traps. The fix is mechanical cleaning of the drain, not a product poured
down it, and they will keep coming back until that film is removed.

**Phorid flies.** Small, humpbacked in profile, and with a very characteristic
habit of running rapidly across a surface rather than flying. They breed in
decaying organic matter, and indoors, in numbers, they can indicate something
significant — a broken waste pipe under a slab, sewage in a crawlspace, or a dead
animal in a void. Persistent phorid flies with no obvious source are worth
investigating properly rather than treating.

**Fruit flies.** Rounder, tan or brownish, often with red eyes, hovering around
fruit, bins and drains rather than soil. Different problem, different fix.

If what you have is sitting on walls rather than around plants, or running rather
than flying, it is worth a second look before you change your watering routine.

## What we would actually come out for

We are not going to pretend a windowsill of houseplants needs a professional, so
here is where it does.

**A greenhouse or a large collection**, where the population is self-sustaining
across dozens of pots and the economics of doing it plant by plant no longer
work.

**Propagation and seedlings**, where the larvae are genuinely damaging young
stock and losses are mounting.

**A commercial growing or retail setting**, where there is a reputational
dimension and a scale problem.

**Suspicion that the source is structural** — a crawlspace, a leak, a drainage
issue — which is squarely what we do, and which usually turns up several other
things at the same time.

**Persistent flies that are not fungus gnats**, particularly phorid flies, where
the finding may be a plumbing failure rather than an insect problem.

In every other case, the drying regime plus sticky traps plus a grit layer will
get you there in a month, and it will cost about eight dollars.

## A four-week plan

If you want this as a schedule rather than a set of principles, here it is.

**Week one.** Stop watering on a routine and start checking with a finger. Empty
every saucer. Put a yellow sticky trap at soil level in each affected pot, and
note the date. Top-dress the worst pots with a half-inch of grit. Move any pot
that is standing in a permanently damp spot — a windowsill that collects
condensation, a corner with no air movement.

**Week two.** Water only the pots whose top two inches have gone genuinely dry,
and water those from below where the pot allows it. Change the sticky traps and
compare the catch against week one. Expect adult numbers to be similar or
slightly higher at this stage, because larvae that were already in the soil are
still emerging.

**Week three.** Numbers on the traps should be visibly falling. Continue.
Repot anything that has never dried out despite the change, because that pot has
a drainage problem rather than a watering problem. If you want to speed it up,
this is the point to add a Bti drench.

**Week four.** Most households find it effectively over. Change the traps once
more and keep them for another fortnight as a check.

**After that.** Keep the watering habit. The single reason this recurs is
returning to a schedule instead of checking the soil.

If the trap counts have not fallen at all by the end of week three, stop
adjusting the plants and start looking for a wet place elsewhere in the
building.

## Why this is worse in winter here

Households in this county often notice fungus gnats appearing between November
and March, and there are two reasons that have nothing to do with the plants
themselves.

Plants use far less water in low light. The same watering routine that suited a
plant in July delivers roughly twice what it needs in December, so soil that
used to dry out in four days now stays damp for ten. Almost every winter fungus
gnat problem is really this.

And plants that spent the summer outside come indoors in autumn, frequently
bringing eggs and larvae with them, into a warm room where the cycle continues
year round rather than pausing.

The general indoor humidity of a wet Whatcom County winter contributes at the
margins too, particularly in a house with a damp crawlspace pushing moisture
upward.

The practical adjustment is simply to water considerably less from October
through February, and to check rather than assume. It is the same advice as
above, applied at the time of year when people are least likely to think of it.

## Growing media and why some are worse

Not all potting material behaves the same way, and the choice makes a
measurable difference to whether this recurs.

**Peat-based composts** hold a great deal of water and, once they have dried out
completely, can be difficult to rewet evenly — which leads people to overwater to
compensate. They are the standard bagged medium and they are the most common
substrate for this problem.

**Coir-based media** hold water similarly but tend to rewet more readily.

**Bark, perlite and grit additions** open the structure up, let water pass
through, and dry the surface faster. Adding a third by volume of perlite or fine
bark to a standard bagged compost changes the drying behavior noticeably and is
the simplest structural fix available.

**Inorganic media** — leca, pumice, and the semi-hydroponic setups some people
use — contain very little for larvae to feed on and are largely immune to this.

**Very old medium** is the worst case. Potting compost breaks down over a few
years into a fine, dense, water-retentive material with a lot of decomposed
organic content, which is precisely what fungus gnat larvae want. A plant that
has been in the same pot for five years and now produces gnats every winter is
usually telling you it is time to repot rather than time to buy a product.

Storage matters too. An opened bag of compost left damp in a garage or a shed
over a season becomes a breeding site in its own right, and every pot filled from
it starts with a population. Keep opened bags sealed and somewhere dry.

## If you keep plants and pets

One practical note, since a lot of the advice circulating online ignores it.

Everything recommended on this page is safe around animals. Drying the soil,
grit top-dressing, sticky traps placed where a curious cat cannot reach them,
and Bti — which acts on fly larvae specifically and has no effect on mammals — are
all fine.

What we would avoid in a house with pets is systemic insecticide granules sold
for houseplants, and any broad-spectrum spray applied to foliage that an animal
may chew. Several popular houseplants are already a problem for cats and dogs
without adding anything to them.

If you have a pet that eats soil or chews leaves, the grit layer earns its place
twice over: it deals with the gnats and it discourages digging.
