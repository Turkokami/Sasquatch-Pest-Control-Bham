## Where the farmland stops and the timber starts

Deming sits at the point on the Mount Baker Highway where the valley floor gives
way to the foothills. West of it is farm ground. East and above it is standing
timber running up toward the mountain. Most properties here have some of each
within sight.

That boundary is the whole story of pest control in this area, and it is what
makes Deming different from the towns further down the valley. Nooksack and
Everson are surrounded by fields. Custer is open country. Deming is both at once,
and it gets the pest pressure of both at once.

The practical result is that a Deming property has two separate sources of
trouble working on it from opposite directions, on different schedules, with
different fixes. The farm ground sends rodents. The forest sends carpenter ants.
The two problems overlap in the calendar but almost never in the solution, and
treating them as one job is how people end up disappointed.

## Two kinds of rodent, and this area has both

Most residential rodent work in this county is mice. Out here it is frequently
rats as well, and the difference is worth understanding before anybody sets a
trap.

Farm ground supports both. Grain, feed, silage, fruit, standing water and dense
ground cover produce populations that live outdoors quite happily for most of the
year and only approach buildings when something changes — the ground is worked,
the cover is cut, the weather turns, or a food source moves indoors for storage.

**Mice** live in small spaces, breed quickly, and do not range far. A mouse
problem is usually local to one building and one entry point, and it grows fast
once it starts.

**Rats** range much further, are far more cautious about anything new in their
environment, and cause a different kind of damage. Chewed wiring in a shop, holes
gnawed through a crawlspace vent, heavy noise in a floor or a wall at night —
those are more often rats than mice. They are also considerably harder to trap
carelessly, because a rat will avoid a new object in a familiar run for days.

Which one it is changes the placement, the timing and the expectation, so we work
that out before we do anything. Droppings, the size of the gnawing, the runs and
the smell all say which animal you have, and getting it wrong wastes a fortnight.

## What the farm ground does to a building

The route in is nearly always ordinary. A crawlspace vent with perished or
missing screening. A gap where a plumbing or electrical line passes through a
rim joist. A garage door with a worn seal. The base of a shop wall where the
siding stops short of the slab.

None of those look like damage. They look like a building that has been standing
for thirty years, which is exactly what they are. Rodents do not need a failure,
only an opening, and a quarter of an inch is enough for a mouse to use.

On a property with outbuildings — and nearly every property here has them — the
sequence matters. The shop, the barn and the woodshed take the pressure first,
because they are closest to the ground cover and least sealed. The house comes
second, once those buildings are full. Sealing the house alone concentrates the
population instead of removing it, which is why we quote the site rather than
the building.

## Carpenter ants are the spring and summer story

This is the pest that defines Deming, and the reason is the timber.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in it to nest, and their
natural habitat is exactly what surrounds this community: stumps, fallen logs,
standing dead trees, and the damp punky wood at the base of living ones. A mature
forest edge supports a great many colonies, and none of them have anything to do
with anybody's house until they expand.

The way they expand is the part most people have never been told. A mature colony
establishes a **parent nest** outdoors — in a stump, a log, a tree — and then
throws out **satellite colonies** nearby. Satellites contain workers and older
larvae but not the queen, and they will happily set up in a structure. A wall
void, a rim joist, a door frame, the wood around a skylight.

That is why the carpenter ant problem here is so much heavier than in town. In a
subdivision, a building generally has to generate its own problem through years
of unnoticed moisture. On the forest edge, the colony already exists forty feet
away and only has to move part of itself indoors.

## The season is genuinely a season

Activity starts as the ground warms in spring and builds through summer, which is
why this is a spring and summer job here rather than a year-round one.

The first sign for most households is not damage. It is large black ants indoors
in March or April, often near a window or a sink, often one or two at a time.
People kill them and assume that is the end of it. What those individuals
actually indicate is that something has become active nearby and has begun
foraging.

Winged ants indoors are a different and more serious signal. They mean a mature,
reproducing colony, and if they are emerging inside the building rather than
arriving through a door, the colony is in the structure rather than outside it.

The other sign is **frass** — the material they push out of the galleries as they
excavate. It looks like sawdust and is not: it is fibrous and shredded, it
frequently contains insect fragments, and it accumulates directly below the
gallery that produced it. Finding a small pile on a sill, a stair tread or the
top of a baseboard is worth more than finding the ants, because it tells you
where to look.

## Following the moisture, and the cedar

Carpenter ants prefer wood that has already been softened. That is why the damage
we find here concentrates in predictable places: a sill plate under a leaking
hose bib, framing around a poorly flashed window, a rim joist over a crawlspace
that never dries out, the base of a post where a deck meets the house.

Cedar is worth a specific mention because there is a great deal of it in this
area, in siding, trim, decking and outbuildings. Cedar's reputation for
durability is about decay resistance, not insect resistance, and once a cedar
member has taken on moisture it is perfectly workable for an ant. A cedar shake
wall on the shaded north side of a house at the forest edge is a common place to
find galleries.

Treating without addressing the water buys a season. The colony goes, the damp
wood stays, and something occupies it again. On a wooded property, where the
building dries slowly and the ground stays damp under the canopy, that turnaround
can be quick.

## The autumn arrivals come together

Then the season changes and an entirely different set of insects shows up.

Multicolored Asian lady beetles and brown marmorated stink bugs are both
relatively recent arrivals in this state, and both do the same thing every
autumn: they look for somewhere sheltered to spend the winter and they choose
buildings. Spiders appear at the same time for their own unrelated reasons, which
is why the three get reported together in a single phone call.

The scale surprises people. This is not a handful of insects. On a warm afternoon
in late September a sunlit wall can carry hundreds at once, and a proportion of
them will find a way in — under siding, around a window frame, through an
unscreened attic vent, into a soffit.

Once they are inside a wall void they are dormant. They do not feed, they do not
breed indoors, they do not bite, and they do no damage to the building. The
problem is entirely that they are there, and that on the first warm days of late
winter some of them wake up on the wrong side of the wall and end up in a
bedroom.

## Why the timing of that work matters so much

Because the useful window is short and most people call after it has closed.

Sealing can be done at any time and is the part that lasts. Exterior treatment of
the walls these insects gather on only works in the few weeks when they are
massing on the outside and have not yet moved through, which in this part of the
county is usually late August into September. Too early and the residual has
degraded. Too late and the wall being treated is one they have already left.

Once they are in the void, honest options narrow considerably. What emerges can
be removed, and the sealing can be done properly for the following year, but
nobody should be treating the inside of a house repeatedly through winter for
insects that cannot reproduce there.

## Spiders on a wooded property

Spiders are the most reported and least dangerous item on this list. Nothing
with a medically significant bite has established itself this far north, and the
two species behind nearly every call — the giant house spider and the cross
orbweaver — are harmless to people.

A forest-edge property gets more of them for a simple reason: there are more
insects around it to eat. The autumn increase indoors is mostly males wandering
to find females rather than anything moving in, and it coincides with the beetle
and stink bug wave, which is why it feels like one event.

Reducing exterior lighting that concentrates flying insects, knocking down webs
where they matter, and closing the same gaps that keep everything else out does
more than repeated spraying.

## Wasps at the forest edge

Wasps do well here for the same reasons everything else does — plenty of forage
and plenty of sheltered structure across a house, a shop, a barn and a woodshed.

Ground-nesting yellowjackets are the genuine hazard rather than the nuisance. A
nest in a bank, a ditch edge or an old rodent burrow gives no warning until a
mower or a brush cutter goes over it, and on wooded acreage there is a great deal
of ground where that can happen. If there is regular clearing on the property, it
is worth knowing where the activity is in July rather than meeting it in August.

We do not treat honey bees and will say so. A swarm is a beekeeper's job and
there are people locally who will collect one.

## Crawlspaces under a canopy

Crawlspaces here dry slowly, and some barely dry at all.

Shade from mature trees, ground that stays damp long after rain, and airflow
broken up by surrounding vegetation all mean a crawlspace at the forest edge runs
wetter than one on open ground a few miles west. Wet crawlspaces are the common
root of several problems at once: softened framing for carpenter ants, moisture
that draws sowbugs and pillbugs, and material a rodent will nest in.

The vapor barrier over the soil is the only thing between wet ground and the
underside of the floor, and when it is torn, displaced or lapped badly it is not
doing that job. Getting that right is frequently the single most useful thing
done on a property out here, and it fixes several complaints at once rather than
one.

## Woodpiles, stumps and what is stacked against the house

There is one item on a Deming property that does more damage than its size
suggests, and it is the woodpile.

Split firewood stacked against a wall is a colony's ideal position: it is dry
under a cover, it is already partly decayed, it is undisturbed for months at a
time, and it is touching the building. Carpenter ants found in a wall are very
often carpenter ants that arrived from the stack six feet away, and the same
stack shelters rodents, spiders and overwintering beetles.

Moving it is free and it works. Off the wall, up on a rack, and where practical
away from the house entirely. Bring in what will be burned in a few days rather
than storing a season's worth against the siding.

Stumps and buried wood are the other half of it. A stump left in the ground
within thirty or forty feet of a building is a likely parent colony site, and so
is any buried construction debris — offcuts, form boards, a stack of scrap left
under a deck when the house was built. Where we find galleries in a structure
here, there is very often a source outdoors that nobody had thought about
because it is not visible from the house.

## Vegetation touching the building

The other thing worth doing before spending money on treatment is breaking the
bridges.

A branch resting on a roof is a road. Ivy on a wall is cover and a ladder at the
same time. Shrubs grown tight against siding hold moisture against the paint,
stop that wall drying after rain, and give everything a sheltered route to the
eaves. None of this requires clearing the property, which is neither possible
nor the point out here — it means a foot of daylight between the planting and
the building, and nothing resting on the roof.

It is the cheapest work on this page and it reduces four separate problems at
once: ant access, rodent access, spider harborage and the damp that starts the
whole sequence.

## The Deming year

**January to March.** Overwintering beetles and stink bugs waking in wall voids
and appearing indoors on mild days. Rodents already established and breeding.
Repair and exclusion season.

**April to June.** Carpenter ants become active and this is when most people
first see them. Wasp queens start nests, which is the cheapest possible moment to
deal with one.

**July and August.** Carpenter ant activity at its peak, wasp colonies at the
size that gets noticed, and ground-nest risk highest during mowing and clearing.
The window opens for treating walls ahead of the autumn arrivals.

**September and October.** The heaviest weeks of the year. Lady beetles, stink
bugs and spiders together on warm walls, and farm ground being cut so rodents
move toward buildings at the same time.

**November and December.** Whatever got in is in. Cold concentrates rodent
activity in the heated parts of a building, which is when the noise overhead
starts.

## What we will not sell you

**A carpenter ant treatment with no attempt to find the water.** We will do the
treatment, and we will tell you plainly that it is temporary if the damp wood
stays. We would rather quote the repair.

**Interior treatment through winter for overwintering insects.** They are dormant
in a void and cannot reproduce there. Spraying the room they emerged into does
nothing about the rest of them.

**A recurring plan for a property that does not need one.** A good deal of
acreage out here genuinely does, because the pressure never stops. Some
properties do not, and if sealing and a single treatment will hold, that is what
we will say.
