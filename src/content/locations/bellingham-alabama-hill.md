## A neighborhood that aged all at once

Alabama Hill went up in a concentrated period through the 1960s and 70s, on
sloping ground east of the city, and that shared birthday is the single most
useful fact about working here.

Houses built in one decade by similar builders using similar materials do not
just look alike. They fail alike, and they fail at the same time. The components
that define this housing stock — cedar and T1-11 siding, original foundation vent
screens, decks, flashing, single-glazed windows since replaced, the first
generation of continuous soffit venting — all have service lives, and on this
hill they are running out together.

That is why we so often find the same three or four things at address after
address, and it is why a problem at one house on a street is a reasonable prompt
to check your own.

## The cedar, and what fifty years does to it

Cedar was used generously here and it is the material that most defines our work
on this hill.

It weathers beautifully. It also softens, and softening is what matters. Carpenter
ants do not eat wood — they excavate galleries in it to nest — and what they want
is timber that has begun to break down. Half a century of Pacific Northwest
weather turns cedar trim, fascia, the bottom edge of siding, deck framing and
fence posts into precisely that.

The thing homeowners get wrong is looking at the face of the siding. Cedar
weathers from the exposed edges and the shaded, splashed and joint areas inward.
A wall that looks perfectly respectable from the driveway frequently has punky
fascia above it, a soft bottom course, and trim around a window that has been
taking water at a failed flashing for a decade.

The structural point that decides whether a treatment holds: a mature carpenter
ant population keeps its **main nest outdoors** and puts satellite nests inside
the building. On these lots the main nest is usually findable — a stump left from
the original clearing, a standing dead limb, an old landscape timber, a rotting
fence post, the woodpile. The satellites are what people see indoors.

Treat the satellites and leave the main colony sitting twenty feet away in the
garden, and you buy a quiet couple of months. That is the whole reason this work
gets a reputation for coming back, and it is why the first visit here is
investigative before it is anything else.

Frass tells you what you are dealing with. Carpenter ants push shavings out, so
the debris below a gallery is fibrous and often has insect parts in it — quite
different from the fine powder under a wood-boring beetle hole or the hard
pellets a dampwood termite leaves behind.

## Components at end of life

Beyond the cedar, this era left a specific inventory, and most of it is now on
borrowed time.

**Original foundation vent screens.** Chosen for airflow rather than longevity,
in light material, and now on their second half-century. Somewhere on nearly
every house of this age one has corroded through at a corner and has been open
for years. This is the most common single entry point on the hill and among the
cheapest to correct properly.

**Decks.** Frequently original or first-replacement, with two separate
consequences. The space beneath is dry, sheltered and undisturbed, which makes it
excellent harborage. And the ledger where the deck attaches to the house is a
chronic defect of this construction period — flashed badly or not at all, which
produces sustained wet framing at exactly the point where a lot of weight is
hanging.

**Continuous soffit venting.** One of the era's better ideas, and a route once
the screening degrades or a panel works loose. On a hillside house the soffit is
often reachable from a branch or a bank that was not there in 1972.

**Retrofit archaeology.** Fifty years of improvements leave marks. Windows
replaced, sometimes twice, each time with a gap at the trim that was filled with
whatever was in the van. Insulation blown in decades after construction into
cavities never designed for it. A wood stove installed and later removed, leaving
a thimble. Cable, satellite and internet each drilling their own entry. Heat
pumps added, with a line set through the wall.

None of that is bad work. It is simply accumulation, and it means a proper
exclusion job on a house of this vintage finds a dozen or more small things
rather than one obvious hole.

## What the slope does

Alabama Hill is built into a grade and the terrain shapes the building envelope
more than people expect.

A **daylight basement or split level** meets the earth at several elevations. The
line where buried foundation becomes exposed foundation is where water and
insects both find their way in, and it is rarely detailed as carefully as either
condition would be alone.

A **stepped or partly excavated crawlspace** does not drain evenly. Groundwater
travels downhill, arrives at the uphill wall, and collects at the low end of the
crawl. That end stays damp for months after the rest has dried, and the joists
and subfloor above it are what soften. Where we find moisture ants or decay in a
house on this hill, that is nearly always where they are.

Access compounds it. The crawl hatch is commonly at the high, dry, convenient
end, which means the interesting findings are at the far end from the door. A
report written from the hatch on a hillside house is close to worthless, and it
is the step most often skipped by whoever came before us.

**Retaining walls and terracing** hold moisture against structure and, where
they are the original timber, become nesting sites in their own right.

**Drainage** ties it all together. A downspout discharging onto a bank that
drains straight back at the foundation is a common finding here and an easy fix,
and correcting it usually does more than any product would.

## Landscaping that grew up

Everything on this hill was planted small in about 1970, and almost none of it
is small now.

A shrub set two feet from the wall is now pressed against the siding. A tree
planted for future shade now has branches on the roof. Foundation planting has
matured into a continuous screen that holds moisture against the building and
gives cover right up to the vents. Ground cover has crept over the grade line.
Old landscape timber has softened into ideal nesting material.

None of that was a mistake. It is what happens when a garden succeeds for fifty
years. But it means that on this hill the most valuable half-hour of a survey is
frequently spent with pruners rather than with anything from the truck: pulling
planting back off the walls, cutting the branch that bridges to the roof, and
re-establishing a dry band at the base of the siding.

Where homeowners do that work, treatments hold. Where they do not, we are back,
and we would rather say so than take the repeat booking quietly.

## The wooded edge

Parts of the hill back onto trees, and where they do the pressure is different in
degree rather than in kind.

Wooded ground supplies continuous cover right up to a building, so an animal
never has to cross open ground to reach it. Deer mice come off the margins along
with rats and house mice, finding vents, the gap under a deck, a service entry or
a soffit. Spider and centipede numbers are higher, because damp wooded ground
produces insects in quantity and everything that eats insects follows.

Where droppings need clearing in an enclosed space, ventilate it first and avoid
sweeping or vacuuming dry material — a sensible precaution regardless of what the
risk turns out to be in a given building, and free to observe.

The treeline is permanent and it is a large part of why people live here.
Nothing applied to a property changes it. What changes the outcome is the
envelope, which is finite work: vents screened in material that will outlast the
next fifty winters, the deck gap closed, branches cut back, and grade corrected.

## Rodents and the autumn

The seasonal logic here is the Bellingham one, sharpened by the hillside and the
housing age.

Rats and mice live outdoors comfortably through the summer — blackberry,
undergrowth, the wooded margins, decking with a gap beneath, woodpiles and
compost. Then the rain sets in through late September and October, the ground
saturates, burrows flood, and everything needs somewhere dry within a few weeks.

A fifty-year-old house on a slope with an open vent screen and a stepped
crawlspace is a good answer to that problem.

The prevention window is August: dry ground, dry crawlspaces, easy access to the
exterior, and the movement still weeks off. A house sealed then simply does not
have the winter problem. A house sealed in December has an interior problem plus
whatever is now shut inside with it, and the order of operations gets
considerably more awkward.

## Insulation, and what a winter costs

On a hill with this much rodent pressure it is worth being specific about what
happens inside a building over a season.

Rodents do not merely cross insulation, they tunnel and nest in it, and they are
incontinent as they travel. A single run becomes a contaminated channel rather
than a soiled patch, and compressed, fouled material has lost most of the value
somebody is paying to heat against.

In an attic that shows up as ceiling staining, a smell that is hard to place, and
a heating bill that has quietly climbed. In a crawlspace it shows up as batts
hanging loose, because they took on moisture and lost their grip — and that loose
material is then both nesting habitat and a moisture problem of its own.

Where remediation is needed the order is not negotiable: exclude first, remove
and replace second. New material installed into a building that is still open is
paying twice, and we will say so rather than quoting the part that is easier to
sell.

## The renovation moment

A great many houses on this hill are being updated, and renovation is the best
and worst time for this work.

Best, because an open wall is the only occasion anybody sees what fifty years
did to the framing: old galleries, moisture damage around a window that failed in
1994, a run of soft sill, a nest in a cavity nobody could otherwise reach.
Correcting any of it is never cheaper than while the finish is already off, and
sealing penetrations during a remodel is trivial where it is awkward forever
afterwards.

Worst, because a building under renovation is wide open — doors propped, sections
of wall off, materials stacked outside, and frequently nobody there overnight. We
regularly see houses acquire problems during a remodel that they did not have
before it began, especially across an autumn.

The useful sequence, if work is planned: have somebody look before the walls
close, make envelope sealing part of the project rather than an afterthought, and
deal with the crawlspace while access is easy.

Old damage deserves a specific note. Galleries in a joist and old exit holes are
history rather than a current problem, and damage persists for decades after the
insects have gone. We would far rather establish which you are looking at than
treat something that finished in 1988.

## Wasps on a hillside

Banked ground, terracing and retaining structures give yellowjackets the cavities
they want, and a ground nest beside a set of steps or a path is invisible until
somebody disturbs it. On a property where people are walking narrow paths between
levels that is a genuine hazard, and the colony peaks in late summer when
everybody is outdoors.

Bald-faced hornets build the gray paper nests people photograph, often in a tree
and frequently unnoticed on a wooded lot until the leaves come down. Paper wasps
take eaves, deck rails and the corners of sheds and are the mildest of the three
— where they are somewhere nobody goes, we will often suggest leaving them.

The cheap intervention is a spring walk of the eaves, decks and ground edges,
when a founding nest is the size of a golf ball. The same nest in August is a
different conversation entirely.

## Spiders, and the September phone call

Giant house spiders bring the autumn calls. They are harmless, they have been
established across western Washington for generations, and the big fast ones
found indoors are wandering males looking for mates rather than anything coming
in for warmth. A spider stranded in a bath is a male that got in and could not
climb out.

There is no established brown recluse population anywhere in Washington and none
has been recorded in this county.

What actually reduces the numbers is exterior work: clearing webbing, dealing
with harborage under decks and in crawl vents, moving firewood off the wall,
correcting outside lighting that concentrates the insects they feed on, and
sealing the door sweeps most of them use to get in.

## What we will not sell you

**A promise about the treeline.** That population is permanent. We seal the
building, which is the part that actually works.

**Recurring service over an unresolved water problem.** If the low end of your
crawl is wet, drainage comes first, and a quarterly plan laid on top of it is an
indefinite cost.

**Treatment for old, inactive damage.** Fifty-year-old galleries are evidence of
history, not of a live infestation.

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Wildlife is outside our scope, and on a
wooded hillside the question comes up often. We handle insects and rodents, and
will hand you a better number for the rest.

## How a visit works here

We start outside, and on this hill that walk takes a while. Every elevation where
the building meets earth, the drainage and where it discharges, the condition of
the cedar at its edges and joints, the deck and its ledger, the vent screens, the
soffit line, the branches reaching the roof, the stumps and dead wood on the lot,
the firewood, and the retaining structures.

Then underneath — properly, with a light, working to the low end of a stepped
crawl rather than reporting from the hatch. Then the attic. Then inside.

You get a plain account of what we found and what is causing it, what needs doing
now, what can wait, and which items are carpentry, drainage or tree work rather
than a pest treatment. On a fifty-year-old hillside house a real share of the
answer is not work we get paid for, and we say it anyway.

Looking is free for every pest we handle other than bed bug verification, which
is charged and then taken off the treatment. Nothing here is tied to a term
agreement.

## Buying or selling on the hill

A short note, because the housing stock here turns over steadily and the same
findings come up in transaction after transaction.

If you are buying, the items worth looking at yourself before anybody writes a
report are the ones this page has already named: the bottom edge of the cedar and
the fascia, the vent screens, the deck ledger, where the downspouts discharge,
and whether the crawl access is at the high end of a slope. None of that needs
training. All of it predicts what a professional evaluation is going to say.

If you are selling, the same list is worth attending to early rather than
negotiating over late. Vent screens and a downspout are inexpensive. A soft
fascia board and a badly flashed ledger are carpentry rather than pest work, and
they read very differently on a report when they have already been corrected.

We are not structural pest inspectors and we do not issue the wood-destroying
organism report a lender relies on — that is a separate credential and it should
come from an independent inspector. What we do is treat what such a report
identifies, and on this hill that is most often carpenter ants in softened cedar
with the colony sitting out in the garden.
