## A creek running through a settled town

Fishtrap Creek runs through the east side of Lynden with a greenbelt and a
public trail along much of it, and the streets that back onto that corridor are
established: mature trees, settled gardens, schools and parks nearby.

The corridor is genuinely good to have. It is also, from where we stand, a
continuous strip of vegetated, undisturbed ground with water in it, running
behind a long line of houses.

That is the whole story here. Not a defect in the neighborhood — the opposite,
in fact — but it explains why houses on these streets get pressure that houses
four blocks west do not, and why the useful work is at the boundary and on the
building rather than anywhere near the creek.

## What a greenbelt corridor actually provides

Four things, all of them permanently.

Cover, in the form of continuous ground vegetation and undergrowth that is
maintained lightly if at all. Water, year round. Food, from the vegetation
itself and from what a corridor through a town collects. And connectivity — a
route running for a considerable distance, linking populations that would
otherwise be isolated in separate gardens.

That last one is the part people miss. An isolated patch of cover supports a
small local population. A corridor lets animals move, recolonize, and reach a
property from a source that is nowhere near it.

The houses immediately backing onto it take the most pressure, and that is
visible in the pattern of calls from these streets. It falls off quickly with
distance, which is itself useful information: if you are three streets away and
having a problem, the source is probably closer to home than the creek.

## The rodents that use it, and how they get in

It is rats as well as mice along a corridor like this, and telling them apart
before anything is set matters.

Mice live in small spaces, breed quickly and range very little, so a mouse
problem is usually local to one building and one route. Rats range far further,
are markedly cautious about anything new in a familiar run, and produce a
different signature — heavy movement in a floor or wall at night, gnawing
through something structural, chewed wiring, a vent opening enlarged rather
than merely used.

That caution is the practical difference: a rat will avoid an unfamiliar object
in its run for days, so an approach that works on mice within a night can read
as complete failure on rats for a week.

The routes in are ordinary. Corroded crawlspace vent screens. Service
penetrations. The bottom corners of a garage door. A crawl hatch resting
against an opening. The unscreened space under a deck or porch. And on
established lots, the planting against the wall that lets something reach all of
them without crossing open ground.

The one people under-rate on a street of mature trees is the roof. A branch
touching a roofline is a bridge over everything sealed below, and rats climb
well.

## Where nothing gets applied

Work at a creek boundary has real limits and they are the correct ones.

Anything applied to ground that drains to the creek reaches the creek. So the
perimeter treatment that would be routine around a foundation elsewhere is not
the approach at that edge of a property. What replaces it is enclosed, secured
stations, trapping, and physical exclusion of the building.

For rodents that is the more durable method regardless — it deals with the
route rather than with the individuals crossing a surface — so the constraint
pushes the work toward the better answer. What it means practically is that
creek-side work is slower and more mechanical than a spray, and a quote that
looks cheaper because it is a spray is not comparing like with like.

## The trail, and who else is back there

A public trail behind a line of gardens changes placement more than it changes
diagnosis.

Anything used near that boundary has to be sited where a member of the public,
a child or a dog cannot reach it. That means enclosed and secured stations,
placed deliberately, and it means nothing is left out between visits. We would
rather you told us where people actually walk and where the fence is easy to
get around than work it out from the property line.

It also means the boundary is not entirely yours to manage, and where a problem
sits in the greenbelt itself rather than on your lot, that is a matter for the
city rather than for a homeowner. We will say which it is.

## Mature trees, and what actually matters about them

These streets have big trees, and people arrive at the conversation braced to
be told they are the problem. They are usually not.

A living, healthy mature tree is rarely a carpenter ant nest. What supports
colonies is standing deadwood, old stumps, fallen limbs left at the back of a
lot, punky wood at the base of an ageing tree, and the landscape timber that
accumulates on a settled property — ties, raised beds, old deck framing, fence
posts, firewood that has gone soft at the bottom of the stack.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in it, and they take
softened damp timber far more readily than sound dry timber. Every item on that
list is ideal, and every item is close to a building.

The two things worth doing are getting damp wood off the ground and away from
the house, and trimming branch contact with the roof. Neither involves removing
a tree anybody cares about.

## Damp ground and what it permits

Ground near a creek has a water table closer to the surface than ground on
higher land, and it moves seasonally. A vented crawlspace over bare soil in
that situation stays damp regardless of how good the roof and gutters are,
which is why people check the wrong things first.

Damp matters because of what it permits. Carpenter ants excavate softened wood
far more readily than sound wood. Wood-boring beetles and decay fungi need
moisture content that dry framing never reaches. And moisture ants — which we
find regularly on ground like this — are not really a pest problem at all: they
nest in wood that is already wet and decaying, so finding them is a moisture
report rather than an infestation.

A ground-contact vapor barrier and sorting out the obvious drainage is the
highest-value work available on a lot like this, and a decent share of our jobs
along here involve no product whatever.

## High water on a small creek

A creek through a town rises and falls, and what that does to the population
along it is worth understanding because it produces a call every few years that
nobody expects.

When water comes up, it floods burrows and low harborage along the bank.
Whatever was living in them does not drown; it moves laterally and upward onto
whatever ground stays dry, which is the gardens and then the buildings.

The arrival is sudden and it has nothing whatever to do with how a property is
kept. Immaculate places get it as readily as neglected ones, and the timing
follows the weather rather than the calendar.

Two things follow. The first is that this is an event with an end rather than a
permanent condition to be managed indefinitely on a contract — the sensible
response is to deal with the animals that arrived and close the openings they
used, so the next rise has nowhere to go on your property.

The second is that anything stored low along a back fence is in the path of it.
Timber, boxes, garden materials and firewood at the bottom of a lot that backs
onto the corridor are exactly what a displaced population moves into first.

## What a public trail brings with it

A trail behind a line of gardens is an amenity and it is also a source of
something specific: food waste.

Litter along a public path — wrappers, drink containers, dog waste bags left
beside a bin rather than in it, and whatever comes out of an overflowing
receptacle at the end of a warm weekend — is a supplementary food supply
running the length of the corridor. It is modest in any one place and
continuous overall.

That is not an argument against trails and it is not something a homeowner can
fix. It is the reason the pressure along here is steadier than the vegetation
alone would explain, and it is worth reporting an overflowing bin to the city
rather than assuming somebody else has.

The part you can control is your own boundary. A compost heap, a bird feeder,
fallen fruit left on the ground, or pet food bowls outside are each a
concentrated food source at the near end of a corridor that already has traffic
on it, and any of them will make your lot the attractive end of the strip.

## Wildlife that is not our trade

A greenbelt corridor through a town brings animals into gardens that a
subdivision two miles away does not see, and it is worth being clear about
where our work stops.

Raccoons, opossums, skunks and beavers are not pest control in the sense this

What we can do is the part that overlaps: identifying what is inviting them —

Bats are a separate matter again and a legally protected one. If there is a

## The autumn arrivals

From late September the sunny elevations collect the seasonal crowd, and a
street with a vegetated corridor behind it gets more of them than an open one.

Cluster flies are the Lynden specialty. They breed outdoors in soil and have
nothing to do with hygiene or waste, and they gather on warm walls before
working into gaps and overwintering in voids — then appear indoors on mild
winter days in numbers that seem impossible relative to any opening anyone can
find.

Multicolored Asian lady beetles and brown marmorated stink bugs do the same
thing on the same walls. Spiders come in for their own reasons and get noticed
at the same time; most of what turns up is giant house spiders, and the autumn
activity is males moving to find females rather than an infestation arriving.

No member of that group bites, breeds under your roof, or damages anything
structural. Because they do not
reproduce inside, the number in the house is entirely a function of how many
got in — so sealing plus correctly timed exterior work in the narrow window
before the wave is the answer, and spraying rooms all winter is not.

## Wasps along a corridor

Ground nests along the greenbelt edge, banks and rough boundaries are the ones
that cause injuries, because they are found by mower or by somebody clearing a
fence line rather than by eye.

On a lot backing onto a corridor there is usually more rough ground than on an
interior lot, and knowing where the activity is before August is worth having.
It costs nothing to have somebody look while they are on the property anyway.

Aerial nests in eaves, sheds and under deck rails are more visible and easier.
Bald-faced hornets build the large gray paper nests; European paper wasps build
small open combs under railings and are far less defensive.

Honey bees we do not treat. If there is a colony in a wall we will identify it
and refer you.

## Deciding whether the creek is really your problem

Because the corridor is the obvious feature, it gets blamed for everything, and
on a fair number of jobs along here it is not the answer.

The test is distance and direction. Pressure from a corridor falls off sharply
with distance from it, and it arrives at the boundary nearest the creek. If the
activity is concentrated on the far side of the house, or you are several
streets away, something closer to home is more likely — a neighbor's woodpile,
an open shed, a bird feeder, a compost heap, or a gap somebody made and did not
close.

That distinction is worth making before anybody spends money, because the work
is different. A corridor problem is answered with exclusion and a boundary
strategy. A local problem is usually answered by finding one thing and removing
it.

We will tell you which one you have rather than defaulting to the explanation
that happens to be visible from the back fence.
