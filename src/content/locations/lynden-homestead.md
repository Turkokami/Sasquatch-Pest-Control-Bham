## Houses built around a course

Homestead is residential development on Lynden's west side arranged around a
golf course that opened in the early nineties, with Fishtrap Creek and the park
beside it a short distance away.

Three features shape the work and all three are about ground rather than
buildings. There is a large area of irrigated, fertilized turf. There are water
features and drainage associated with it. And there is a creek corridor and
park margin nearby, which is continuous undisturbed habitat running past the
whole area.

The housing itself is the easy part: consistent, of a known period, with a
predictable set of openings. Most of what we deal with here arrives from
outside it.

## Irrigated turf is a habitat, not a lawn

A golf course, common lawns and irrigated garden planting produce one of the
most reliable pest patterns in the county, and it starts underground.

Watered, fertilized turf supports a heavy population of soil invertebrates —
worms, larvae, grubs — and dense grass provides continuous cover at ground
level. That is a food supply and a shelter layer over a large contiguous area,
maintained deliberately, year after year.

Nothing about that is a fault of the course. It is what a course is, and it is
also what a well-kept lawn is on a smaller scale. But it is the reason the
animals below turn up here rather than three streets away.

## Moles and voles, which are not the same animal

These two get blamed for each other constantly and the confusion costs people a
season, so it is worth being precise.

**Moles** work below ground. They tunnel in pursuit of worms and larvae and
push the spoil to the surface as conical mounds. They eat essentially no plant
material at all. What a homeowner sees is mounds and a lawn that feels spongy
or lifts underfoot, and what they conclude is that something is eating the
grass. Nothing is.

**Voles** work at the surface. They cut shallow runways through the thatch and
ground cover, leave small clean openings, and do eat plant material — roots,
bulbs, and crucially the bark at the base of young trees and shrubs over
winter. A ring of bark stripped at ground level will kill a young ornamental
outright, and it happens under long grass or snow where nobody sees it.

The tell is in the workings. Winding surface trails with clipped vegetation,
appearing when long grass is cut short, means voles. Pushed-up soil means
moles. A property can have both.

Neither is much of a building problem — voles occasionally enter a crawlspace
or a garage, moles essentially never — which is worth saying because the alarm
is usually out of proportion. The damage is to the ground.

## What lives in the turf underneath

Behind the mole question sits a third one that only gets asked when the lawn
starts dying in patches.

European chafer and crane fly larvae are the two that matter in this county.
The first feeds on roots, the second on roots and crowns, and both do their
damage in spring when the grass is trying to grow and cannot. What people see
is turf lifting like a loose carpet, irregular yellowing, and birds working the
lawn hard in the mornings.

The connection to moles runs the opposite way from what most people assume. A
lawn with a heavy larval population is a lawn worth tunneling under, so the
grub problem usually arrives first and the mole arrives because of it. Removing
the animal while the food source stays put simply invites the next one.

The adult crane flies in autumn — long-legged, conspicuous, frequently indoors
— are harmless. They do not bite, they do not damage anything, and they are
not the part worth treating.

Most of this is turf management rather than pest control, and where that is the
honest answer we will say so and tell you what to ask a groundskeeper.

## The creek corridor and the park margin

A vegetated creek corridor is among the best rodent habitat there is:
continuous cover, food and water running for a considerable distance, with
almost no disturbance. The park margin adds to it.

The houses closest to that corridor take materially more pressure than the ones
in the middle of the development, and that difference shows up plainly in the
pattern of calls.

It cannot be fixed at the source and should not be — a riparian corridor is
worth having. It is entirely fixable at the building, which is where all the
useful work is.

The seasonal note is that margins get cut and maintained, usually once or twice
a year, and when they are, whatever was living in them relocates within days.
Activity spikes in the week the maintenance happens. That is predictable, so
the sealing work is best done ahead of it.

## Water features and where product does not go

Ponds and drainage associated with a course change the toolkit near them, for
good reasons.

Anything applied to ground that drains to open water reaches the water. So work
near a pond margin, a ditch or the creek moves to enclosed, secured stations,
to trapping, and to physical exclusion of the building itself.

That is not a reduced service. Closing the route and removing the individuals
is more durable for rodents than treating a surface they walk across, and the
water constraint pushes the job toward the better method rather than away from
it. What it does mean is that boundary work here is slower and more mechanical
than a perimeter spray, and that is worth understanding before comparing
quotes.

Open water also produces its own nuisance insects — midges and other non-biting
flies emerging in numbers on still evenings — which are genuinely unpleasant
and are not a treatable structural problem. Where the honest answer is
screening and lighting, we will give it.

## Geese, and the birds a course attracts

Managed turf beside water attracts waterfowl, and while geese are not a pest
control matter in themselves, what they leave is relevant.

Droppings on paths, lawns and around water margins support fly breeding in
warm weather, and accumulations near a house become a nuisance in their own
right. The management of that is a grounds question rather than ours.

Where it does become our work is when nesting on or around buildings leaves
material in gutters and voids, and the insects living in an abandoned nest move
inward. Bird mites and carpet beetles turning up indoors weeks later, with no
apparent connection to anything, are frequently traceable to a nest nobody
thought about.

## Nineties housing, and its particular openings

Housing of this period is consistent enough that we can predict most of what we
will find before arriving.

Crawlspaces are typically vented, with original screening now around thirty
years old, corroding from the ground side where nobody inspects. Service
penetrations were sealed with expanding foam where they were sealed at all —
which handles air and water and does nothing about a mouse. Garage doors have
generally been replaced once, and the bottom corners are where daylight shows
from inside. Siding often runs close to grade.

None of that is a defect. It is a building of its period, and the repairs are
small, mechanical and permanent.

The other period feature is the landscaping. Planting put in when these houses
were new is now thirty years mature, and on many lots it is against the siding
over a deep bed of accumulated material. That combination — permanent cover
touching the wall, over damp organic matter — is the most productive single
thing to change on a mature lot, and it costs an afternoon rather than an
invoice.

## Rodents, and the routes they actually use

Nothing exotic gets into these houses.

Corroded crawlspace vent screens. Foamed service penetrations. The bottom
corners of the garage door. A crawl hatch that is a board against an opening.
The unscreened space under a deck or patio built against the house. And where
planting or stored material sits against siding, the corridor that lets
something reach all of the above unseen.

The one that gets under-rated here is the roof. Trees planted when the
development was new now reach rooflines, and a branch touching a roof bypasses
everything sealed at ground level. Rats climb well and will use it. Trimming
vegetation contact is worth as much as most foundation work.

## Carpenter ants and the landscape timber

Carpenter ants here mostly come out of wood on the ground rather than out of a
forest.

They do not eat wood — they excavate galleries in it to nest, and they take
softened, damp timber far more readily than sound dry timber. Thirty-year-old
landscape ties, raised beds, old deck framing, fence posts, a stump left when
something came down, and firewood that has been in the same place long enough
to go soft at the base are all exactly what they want, and all of it is close
to a building.

The pattern is a parent colony outdoors sending satellite colonies outward,
with a rim joist or a wall void serving a satellite as well as a log does. The
signals are large black ants indoors in spring, winged ants emerging indoors
later — which mean a mature reproducing colony — and frass, the sawdust-like
material pushed out of galleries, which means an active nest directly above
where it lands.

The work is finding the parent colony, not treating what crosses a kitchen
floor.

## Wasps, and the ground nests on amenity ground

Yellowjackets here are a late-summer problem and the ground nests are the ones
that cause injuries, because they are found by mower rather than by eye.

On a development with a lot of maintained and semi-maintained ground, that is a
hazard for grounds staff as much as for residents, and a nest along a bank, a
verge or a rough edge is worth reporting rather than stepping around.

Aerial nests in soffits, sheds and under deck rails are more visible and easier
to deal with. Bald-faced hornets build the large gray paper nests people
photograph; European paper wasps build small open combs under railings and are
markedly less inclined to defend them.

Honey bees are not our work, and near berry country they are worth more than we
are. A colony in a wall gets identified and referred to somebody who removes
them properly.

## Who acts, and where the line falls

Housing arranged around shared amenity almost always has an association behind
it, and the first question on any job here is which side of the line the source
is on.

Work on your lot — the building envelope, the garage, the crawlspace, planting
against the walls, what is stored outside, drainage near the foundation — is
ours to do directly and it is where the durable improvement is.

Pressure arriving from common ground is a different conversation. Shared
landscaping, course margins, water features, road verges and greenbelt strips
support populations, and those populations do not recognize a line they cannot
see.

We raise this at the start rather than at the end for one reason: somebody who
pays for thorough work on their own property, next to an untouched source
thirty feet the other side of a boundary, will get a partial result and will
reasonably feel they were sold something. Where the answer involves the
association or the course, we will say so and put it in writing so it can be
handed over.

## Winter, when the ground is quiet

The season people ignore here is the one where the useful work happens.

Course maintenance eases off, the turf stops growing, the margins get cut back,
and the animals that spent the summer in cover start looking for somewhere
warmer. That combination is exactly why rodent activity indoors peaks well
after the weather turns rather than during it.

It is also when a building can be worked on properly. Planting is cut back and
the foundation line is visible, the ground is not being irrigated, and a
crawlspace inspection in January tells you what the wet months are actually
doing under the floor rather than what a dry July suggests.

If a job here is going to be done once and done properly, the quiet months are
when to do it.

## Placement on a course boundary

One thing about working here differs from anywhere else, and it is worth
stating plainly.

People and dogs go onto course margins to retrieve things. That is normal, it
is not going to stop, and it decides where anything can be placed. Everything
near a boundary goes in enclosed, secured stations, sited where somebody
stepping off a fairway edge will not encounter it.

Tell us where people actually walk rather than where the boundary line is. The
two are rarely the same, and we would much rather be told than guess.

We will also walk you through the label before we treat, name what is being
applied and where it is not being applied, and give the re-entry directions
unprompted. Every product this company carries is published on this site with
its full label and safety data sheet.

## What we would rather you did not pay for

If the lawn damage is moles, the honest answer is that this is a grounds
problem, it does not threaten the building, and it does not justify a treatment
program.

If the nuisance insects on a summer evening are coming off open water, nothing
applied to your house will change that.

And if the rodent route is a thirty-year-old vent screen and a garage door
seal, the answer is a morning of hardware rather than a schedule of visits. It
ends the route, and it does not repeat.

## Talking to the course before assuming

Where a problem genuinely originates on course ground, the useful first step is
a conversation rather than a complaint.

Course maintenance teams generally know their own ground well, they have their
own turf programs, and they are usually aware of a wet corner, a rough margin or
a stretch of bank that is causing trouble. A specific, factual report — where,
what, and when it was observed — gets a better response than a general one.

We will give you that in writing if it helps.

## How a visit works here

We start at the boundary: what your lot backs onto, how close the turf, the
water and the creek margin get, and what has accumulated along the fence line.

Then the building at ground level — foundation line, vent screening and how it
is fixed, sweeps, penetrations, garage door corners from the inside with the
door shut, and any deck perimeter.

Then the roof line for vegetation contact and the soffit run for wasps.

Then the crawlspace, entered rather than peered into, with a light, end to
end.

You get a plain account of what is active, what is historic, what is driving
it, which items are turf or grounds work rather than pest work, and what is
worth doing before the margins are next cut. The assessment costs nothing
unless it is bed bug verification, which is billed and credited back against
treatment, and no term agreement is required.
