## A turnover neighborhood

Happy Valley sits below the Western campus and it is defined less by how its
buildings were built than by how often they change hands.

A large share of this housing is rented to students, and a large share of that
turns over on the same short calendar — emptying in June, refilling in September,
with a second churn as leases shift mid-year. Houses divided into units, purpose
built apartments, and single-family homes let to four or five people share the
same rhythm.

That rhythm is the whole story. It produces a pest profile you will not find in
Sunnyland or Edgemoor, and it means the useful advice here is about timing and
scope far more than it is about products.

## Turnover is the transport mechanism

Bed bugs and, to a lesser extent, German cockroaches move with belongings. They
do not fly, they do not travel far under their own power, and they do not appear
spontaneously in a clean building. They arrive in things people carry.

So consider what June looks like in this neighborhood: several hundred
households packing up at once, secondhand furniture changing hands, mattresses
and sofas moving between houses in the back of pickup trucks, storage units
loaded and unloaded, and a good deal of it arriving in a different building three
streets away. September repeats it in reverse.

That is the single most effective bed bug distribution system in Whatcom County,
and it runs twice a year on schedule.

It is worth saying plainly to landlords and to tenants: this has nothing to do
with cleanliness. A bed bug problem is a transport problem. Any property with
frequent turnover is exposed regardless of how well it is kept, and the
operators who handle it best accept that and build detection into the routine
rather than treating each incident as an anomaly or a failing.

That framing matters practically, not just morally. Tenants who expect to be
blamed report late, and late reporting is exactly what turns a contained problem
in one room into a building-wide treatment.

## The scope problem in shared housing

This is the thing that most often goes wrong here, and it is not a treatment
failure.

In a building with shared structure — a divided house, a duplex, an apartment
block — the pests are a **building-level population**. Wall voids, plumbing
chases, shared attics and common utility runs connect units, and insects move
through them without any regard to who pays which lease.

Treat one unit thoroughly and the population in the adjacent units is untouched.
It moves back. The tenant reasonably concludes the treatment did not work; the
landlord reasonably concludes the tenant reinfested it. Neither is right. The
scope was wrong.

What actually resolves it is a building plan: map which units are affected,
identify the likely source, treat in a coordinated pass rather than as separate
call-outs, and follow up on an interval matched to the insect's life cycle
rather than to the billing cycle.

Landlords who resist that generally pay for the same problem three times, and by
the third round it is larger than it was. We say this before taking the work
rather than after, and where somebody wants single-unit treatment anyway we will
do good work on that unit and be clear about what it can and cannot achieve.

## Bed bugs specifically

Early detection is the entire game, and the economics are stark.

A problem caught while it is still one room is a contained job with a
predictable cost. The same problem three months later, after occupants have
moved between bedrooms and soft furnishings have shifted, is a property-wide
treatment at several times the price, with units out of service during the
letting season.

What makes early detection realistic in a rental:

**Inspect between tenancies.** The gap between one tenant leaving and the next
arriving is the cheapest and easiest moment to look properly, and it is the one
most often skipped.

**Use mattress and box spring encasements as standard.** They make establishment
harder and, more usefully, they make evidence obvious rather than hidden.

**Brief whoever cleans or turns units on what to look for**, because they are in
those rooms more often than anyone else.

**Make it safe to report.** A tenant who thinks they will be blamed or charged
waits. That delay costs far more than the treatment would have.

Our assessment for bed bugs carries a verification fee, credited against
treatment if you go ahead. We charge it because verification is slow, detailed
work — genuinely going through a room rather than glancing at it — and because
free bed bug callouts attract a volume of speculative visits that pushes out real
appointments. Everything else we handle here is free to look at.

## German cockroaches

The other signature of this neighborhood, and routinely confused with a
completely different insect.

German cockroaches are indoor and human-associated. They do not live outside in
this climate. They arrive inside things — appliances, cardboard, kitchen
equipment, boxes from a previous flat — and once established they breed faster
than an unplanned treatment schedule can knock them back.

They persist in voids: behind and beneath kitchen equipment, in the cavity around
a dishwasher, inside the frame of a fridge, in the gaps behind cabinetry. That is
why a kitchen can look clean and still have a problem, and why surface treatment
alone achieves very little.

Getting on top of them takes a coordinated pass across the affected units,
attention to the harborage rather than the visible surfaces, and follow-up
timed to the life cycle. Shared kitchens and high occupancy make this harder,
and student housing has both.

They should not be confused with **Oriental cockroaches**, which are a
damp-crawlspace insect, live outdoors and under buildings, come up through
drains and crawl vents, and are reporting a moisture condition rather than a
hygiene one. Older houses in this neighborhood get those too, and the treatments
are not interchangeable. Identification comes first and it is the cheapest thing
we do.

## The buildings themselves

Underneath the turnover, Happy Valley's housing is a mix, and the stock does
matter.

There is older single-family housing, much of it now let to groups, sitting on
shallow crawlspaces over bare earth with the familiar consequences: moisture
ants in framing that has softened, decay fungus where it stays wet, and the
damp-crawl group of sowbugs, millipedes, springtails and Oriental cockroaches
underneath.

There are houses divided into units at various points over the decades, where
the division created new junctions between old framing and new, and those
junctions are rarely sealed as well as either section on its own.

And there is purpose-built rental stock of several eras, where the issues are
more conventional — service penetrations closed with expanding foam, which stops
drafts and does not stop a rodent, and shared refuse areas that concentrate
pressure for the whole building.

High occupancy amplifies everything. More people means more food, more waste,
more comings and goings, and more doors held open. A house occupied by five
adults with different schedules generates conditions a family home does not,
and that is a factor rather than a criticism.

## Waste, and the thing landlords can actually change

If there is one physical intervention that changes outcomes in this
neighborhood, it is refuse.

Shared bins that will not close, overflowing collection points, and rubbish
staged outside between collections will support a rodent population that no
service schedule overcomes. In a street of multi-occupancy houses this compounds
quickly, and by autumn it is feeding a substantial population that then looks
for somewhere dry.

The interventions are unglamorous: enough bin capacity for the actual occupancy,
lids that close, a hard surface rather than ground contact, and a collection
frequency matched to the number of people. Landlords who fix that see a
difference. Those who do not are buying quarterly service against a food supply
they are providing.

## Rodents and the autumn

The seasonal pattern is the Bellingham one, sharpened by the housing.

Through summer, rats live outdoors perfectly well — in blackberry, under
decking, in the greenbelt margins and along the creek corridors. Then the rain
sets in through late September and October, burrows flood, and that whole
population needs somewhere dry within a few weeks.

Student housing at that exact moment is newly reoccupied, full of food, warm,
and frequently has a refuse situation that has not yet settled. It is a good
target.

The work that prevents it is exclusion, and the right time is August — which in
this neighborhood is precisely when the buildings are empty and access is
easiest. That is a genuine scheduling advantage and almost nobody uses it. A
landlord who seals a house in August between tenancies avoids the entire winter
problem; one who waits until a tenant reports scratching in November is dealing
with an interior problem in an occupied building.

## Spiders, and the reassuring answer

Every September the calls start, and in a neighborhood that has just refilled
with people who have not lived through a Pacific Northwest autumn before, the
volume is high.

These are giant house spiders. They are harmless, they are long established
across western Washington, and the large fast ones people find are wandering
males looking for mates rather than an invasion moving indoors for warmth. The
ones stranded in a bath are males that got in and could not climb out.

Brown recluse has never been recorded in this part of Washington and the species
is not established in the state. Anyone telling a nervous first-year tenant
otherwise while holding a quote is selling fear.

The useful measures are exterior: clearing webbing, dealing with harborage under
porches and decks, correcting outside lighting that concentrates the insects
they eat, and sealing door sweeps and thresholds.

## Between tenancies, which is the whole opportunity

Worth its own section for landlords and managers, because the single most
valuable window in this neighborhood is one that most owners spend doing
something else.

When a unit is empty it can be inspected properly, treated without coordinating
around anybody's schedule, and sealed without working around furniture. Nothing
has to be moved, nobody has to be out of the property for a few hours, and there
is no tenant relationship to manage around the work.

In Happy Valley that window falls in late summer — exactly when exclusion work
should be done anyway, because the ground and crawlspaces are dry and the autumn
rodent movement has not started.

So the two calendars line up almost perfectly, and almost nobody uses it. A
practical sequence for a rental here looks like: inspect at handover, deal with
anything found while the unit is empty, seal the building envelope in the same
visit, and go into the letting season with the property in known condition.

The alternative — waiting for a tenant to report something in November — means
treating an occupied unit, coordinating access with several people, and doing
exterior work in weather that limits what is possible.

Owners with several properties benefit most, because the work can be batched
across a turnover week rather than scattered across a year of call-outs.

## What tenants can do, and what they cannot

It is worth being straight with renters, because a lot of the advice aimed at
them is either useless or unfair.

**Worth doing.** Report early and in writing, because a dated report protects
you and because early problems are small problems. Keep bedding off the floor
and away from walls where you reasonably can. Be careful with secondhand
furniture, particularly upholstered items and bed frames, which is where a great
deal of this neighborhood's bed bug movement originates. Keep food in sealed
containers, which does more against cockroaches than any spray a shop will sell
you. Put refuse in the bin rather than beside it.

**Not worth doing.** Buying a retail spray for bed bugs. It does not resolve an
established population, and worse, it scatters them into adjacent rooms and
units, which turns a contained problem into a distributed one and makes the
eventual professional treatment harder and more expensive. Foggers and bombs are
the same story, only more so.

**Not your fault.** Bed bugs and cockroaches in shared housing are not evidence
of anything about you. They travel with belongings and they move between
connected units. Any framing that treats them as a hygiene failure makes tenants
hide problems, and hidden problems become expensive ones.

**Not fixable alone.** If the building is shared, your unit is one part of a
larger picture. You can and should report; the scope decision belongs to whoever
manages the property.

## Fleas, and the pet question

Worth covering because shared houses in this neighborhood turn over pets as
often as they turn over people.

Fleas arriving with a new household member are the obvious route, but the one
that catches people out is a house that has been empty over the summer and then
reoccupied. Flea pupae can sit dormant in carpet and floor seams for months and
emerge when vibration and warmth tell them a host has arrived. So a tenant moves
into a property that had a pet two occupancies ago and gets bitten in week one,
with no animal anywhere in the building.

That is not a mystery and it is not the new tenant's doing. It is the previous
population completing its life cycle on schedule.

Treatment for it is straightforward but it needs the follow-up interval
respected, because the pupal stage is protected and a single visit leaves the
next emergence untouched. Vacuuming between visits genuinely helps and is the
one thing an occupant can do that measurably changes the outcome.

Where there is no pet history at all, a flea problem usually means an animal
under the floor or in a void rather than in the rooms. That is a different job,
and where the animal is wildlife rather than a rodent it belongs to a specialist
rather than to us.

## Noise, damage and what a landlord actually pays for

The costs in this neighborhood are rarely the treatment.

They are a unit out of service during letting season. A tenancy that ends early.
A review that follows a household through a rental listing. A deposit dispute
that turns on who introduced what. Insulation and ducting damaged over a winter
by rodents nobody reported. And the compounding cost of treating the same
problem across three separate call-outs because the scope was never widened.

Set against that, inspecting an empty unit at handover and sealing the envelope
in the same visit is inexpensive. That is the argument we make to owners here,
and it is not a sales argument so much as arithmetic.

Where a property is genuinely at the end of its useful envelope — skirting gone,
crawl open, refuse situation unfixable — we will say that too, rather than
selling a recurring program against a building that needs work we do not do.
