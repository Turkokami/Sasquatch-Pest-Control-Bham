## A district with two halves

Fairhaven is the most architecturally distinct part of Bellingham and, from our
side of the work, it is really two different jobs that happen to share a name.

There is the historic commercial core — brick and timber buildings from the boom
years, built shoulder to shoulder, now holding restaurants, retail, offices and
apartments above. And there is the residential district around it: old houses on
old foundations, on streets that run down toward the water and back up against
the Padden Creek corridor and the wooded greenbelt.

The commercial half is a shared-structure problem. The residential half is a
crawlspace-and-greenbelt problem. Treating either as though it were the other is
the most common mistake we see here.

## The commercial core, and why the block is the unit

The single most useful thing to understand about doing pest work in a historic
commercial block is that the buildings are connected, and almost nobody who
leases a unit in one knows it.

Party walls. Shared basements, or basements that were once shared and have been
partially divided since. Utility chases and service runs that pass from one
building into the next. Drainage extended and re-extended as tenants changed over
a century. Voids above suspended ceilings that continue past a wall you assume is
solid.

A rodent population in a block like this is a single population using the whole
structure. It does not recognize a lease line, and it moves in response to
whatever pressure it encounters. Treat one tenant thoroughly and the animals
relocate to the neighboring unit, wait, and come back when the pressure eases.

So a tenant can spend years paying for monthly service against a population that
was never theirs alone, seeing modest improvement and never a resolution. We
have watched it happen, and it is the reason we say this at the first visit
rather than the fourth.

Where a building owner, a property manager or several tenants will work to one
plan, the result is different in kind rather than incrementally better. That is
a harder conversation than selling a single contract and it is the honest one.

Where that coordination is not available — and often it is not — we will still do
good work on your unit, seal what can be sealed, and be clear that what you are
buying is control of your space rather than elimination of the block's
population.

## Old buildings, old openings

The commercial stock here is well over a century old in places, and age brings
its own inventory of ways in.

Mortar joints that have weathered and opened, particularly low on a wall or
where a downspout has been discharging against it for decades. Old ventilation
openings from a use nobody remembers — a coal chute, a boiler intake, a service
entry for a utility that no longer exists. Basement window wells that have
filled with debris and no longer close properly. Pipe penetrations from three
generations of plumbing, each one drilled generously and none of them sealed.

The masonry itself is not the vulnerability. What matters is that behind and
above the brick, the floor structure, the roof structure and much of the interior
framing are timber, and that timber is subject to exactly the same moisture and
carpenter ant problems as a wooden house. A brick wall does not protect a wet
joist.

Roof level deserves specific mention. Flat and low-slope roofs on these
buildings carry every mechanical service, and each penetration is sealed on a
much shorter cycle than the roof itself. Once something is above a suspended
ceiling it has access along the full length of the building, which is how a
problem that started at a roof unit turns up as droppings above a shop floor at
the far end.

## Food service in a hundred-year-old building

A good share of the district is restaurants and cafés, and it is a genuinely
specific job.

**The building is the first constraint.** Older drainage, floors resurfaced
repeatedly over decades, equipment fitted into rooms not designed for it, and
voids behind and beneath fixtures that cannot easily be reached or cleaned.
Those voids are where German cockroaches persist, and they are the reason a
kitchen can be immaculate and still have a problem. A spotless surface tells you
nothing about what is behind the equipment.

**Waste is the second and it is where the real progress sits.** A dumpster with
a broken lid, an enclosure that will not close against the ground, or bins
staged in an alley overnight will support a rodent population that no service
schedule overcomes. In a shared block this is doubly true, because one tenant's
waste practice feeds the population that turns up in the business next door.

We put that in the report rather than quietly servicing around it. Billing
monthly while working around an open enclosure would be dishonest, and it also
does not work.

**Deliveries are the third.** Cardboard and pallets arrive from elsewhere with
whatever traveled in them, and stored-product insects in dry goods come in
inside the product rather than through a door. Treating a perimeter for those
accomplishes nothing; the work is finding the source lot.

**Drains and grease** are the fourth. Drain flies breed in the organic film
lining floor drains, sinks and the underside of covers. Surface treatment does
nothing to them, and mechanical cleaning ends the problem within a fortnight.
Grease interceptors and the ground around them are their own reservoir.

Scheduling matters as much as technique in this district. We work around service
hours rather than turning up mid-rush, and for most Fairhaven accounts that
means early or between services.

## The residential streets

Away from the commercial core, Fairhaven's housing is old, individual and often
substantial — a mix of turn-of-the-century houses, later infill, and a good
number of properties that have been divided into units at some point.

The underlying condition is the one that runs through all of Bellingham's older
stock: a shallow crawlspace over bare earth, frequently with a vapor barrier
that is absent or has been pushed aside by whoever last ran a pipe. Ground
moisture goes straight into the space and into the joists above it, continuously,
in a climate that supplies plenty.

Everything moisture-driven follows from that. Moisture ants colonize framing
that has softened, and finding them is useful information rather than merely a
problem — the water is the disease and the ants are the symptom. Decay fungus
runs where the wood stays wet, and decay rather than insects is what actually
costs structural capacity here. Sowbugs, millipedes, springtails and Oriental
cockroaches live in the damp and occasionally come up through a gap around a
pipe.

Carpenter ants are the expensive one. They prefer wood that has begun to soften
and keep a parent colony outdoors — a stump, a fence post, a landscape timber, a
woodpile — with satellite nests inside the structure. On these lots, with mature
planting and old trees, the parent is nearly always findable if somebody looks,
and finding it is the difference between a result and a repeat visit.

Steep lots are a Fairhaven feature worth naming. A house built into a slope has
part of its foundation buried and part exposed, and the transition between them
is where water and insects both find their way in. Sloped crawlspaces hold water
at the low end long after the rest has dried, and that low end is where the
decay and the ants will be.

## The creek corridor and the greenbelt

Padden Creek and the wooded corridor running through the district are among the
best things about living here, and they are a permanent source of pressure.

A greenbelt is continuous cover. It runs from one end of a neighborhood to the
other with no break, and it delivers rodents right up to the foundations of the
houses that back onto it. On an open block an animal has to cross exposed ground
to reach a building. Here it does not.

That pressure is not going away and it should not. Nothing applied to your
property will reduce the population living in the corridor, and any company
suggesting otherwise is selling something.

What is achievable is the building envelope, and it is genuinely achievable. The
crawl vents screened with material that will still be there in ten years. The
gap where a deck meets the house closed. The branch that bridges to the roof
trimmed. Grade corrected where landscaping runs straight up against the siding.
Woodpiles moved off the wall. That work is finite and it holds.

Trapping alone on a greenbelt-adjacent property is a recurring cost rather than
a solution, and we would rather tell you that at the first visit than bill you
quarterly for it.

## Apartments and divided houses

Fairhaven has a substantial rental stock, including older houses divided into
units and apartments above the commercial spaces, and that changes how some work
has to be scoped.

In a building with shared structure the pests are a building-level population.
Wall voids, plumbing chases and framing cavities connect units, and insects move
through them without regard to who pays which lease. Treating one unit while the
ones either side go untreated relocates the problem — usually next door, often
back again a month later. Tenants reasonably conclude the treatment failed. It
did not fail; it was scoped wrong.

What works is a building plan: map the affected units, identify the source,
treat in a coordinated pass, and follow up on an interval matched to the life
cycle rather than the billing cycle. Landlords who resist that generally pay for
the same problem three times.

Bed bugs and German cockroaches are the two that most reward this approach and
punish the alternative hardest. Both travel readily between units, and both are
far cheaper to resolve early and thoroughly than late and piecemeal.

Bed bug verification is the single exception to our free assessment, and the fee comes off the treatment if you go ahead. Everything else costs nothing to have us look
at.

## Visitors, and what the season does

Fairhaven is a destination, and the seasonal swing shows up in our work.

Food service volume rises sharply through the summer and falls away after, which
means a program built on identical monthly visits year-round is simultaneously
overserving the winter and underserving the peak. We would rather scale it to
the season than bill a flat schedule.

Outdoor seating is the other seasonal factor. Wasps and yellowjackets converge
on outdoor food from late July onward, when colonies are at their largest and
the workers have switched from protein to sugar. By August a patio can be a
genuine customer-experience problem rather than a hygiene one. Some of the fix
is physical, some is waste timing, and very little of it is something applied.

Lodging in the district brings the bed bug exposure that comes with any guest
turnover. It is a transport problem rather than a hygiene one, and the operators
who handle it best build detection into the routine rather than treating each
incident as an anomaly.

## Spiders, and the autumn calls

Every September the phone starts about large fast spiders indoors, and in a
district with this much greenbelt, this much mature planting and this much old
crawlspace, the volume is high.

They are giant house spiders — harmless, long established across western
Washington, and the large ones people find are wandering males looking for mates
rather than an invasion. Brown recluse has never been recorded in this part of
the state and the species is not established anywhere in Washington.

What reduces the population is unglamorous and effective: clearing exterior
webbing, dealing with harborage under porches, decks and in crawl vents,
correcting exterior lighting that concentrates the insects they feed on, and
sealing door sweeps and thresholds. For a business with a lit sign and a patio,
the lighting point matters more than most owners expect.

## What we will not sell you

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Birds on a parapet, an animal in a ceiling
void — none of that is ours. We do insects and rodents, and we will tell you
which you have and hand you a better number for the rest.

**A resolved block from a single-unit contract.** If your building is connected
to its neighbors and only you are treating, we will say what that can and
cannot achieve rather than implying more.

**A recurring program over a wet crawlspace.** Drying, removal and drainage come
first. Quarterly service laid over an unresolved moisture problem is an
indefinite cost.

**Treatment for old damage with no current activity.** Galleries and exit holes
persist for decades after the insects have gone.

## How a visit here works

For a residential property we start outside and walk the whole perimeter,
including the greenbelt side, which is the elevation people rarely look at and
where a good share of the findings are. Grade and drainage, the
siding-to-foundation transition, vents, penetrations, vegetation contact, and
the state of any previous exclusion work. Then underneath properly with a light,
which on this housing stock is where the answer is. Then the attic and the
interior.

For a commercial property we start with your operating constraints — hours,
service times, access, what may be placed where — because those shape everything
after them. Then the exterior and the waste situation, the receiving area, the
basement and any shared structure, the ceiling void, and the roof.

Either way you get a plain account: what we found, what is driving it, what
needs doing now, what can wait, and which items belong to a builder, a roofer, a
drainage contractor or a waste provider rather than to us. In a district of
buildings this old, a real share of the answer is not work we get paid for, and
we say it anyway.

Inspections are free for every pest we handle, bed bugs excepted, and there is
no long-term contract.

## Buildings on the water side

The properties running down toward the bay and the marine edge pick up a factor
the rest of the district does not.

Salt in the air works on every metal component of a building envelope — vent
screens, flashing, fasteners, door hardware, and any hardware cloth from a
previous exclusion attempt. It corrodes faster here than it does half a mile
inland, which means an exclusion specified for a sheltered site is a temporary
result. Anything we install on this side is chosen for the exposure, because a
job that fails in three winters was not worth the money.

Wind-driven rain is the companion problem. It goes into assemblies designed for
rain that falls downward, and it works trim, siding and flashing loose over
time. The consequence is a wall on a weather elevation that stays damp and
supports moisture ants in framing that looks perfectly sound from inside.

Marine bank, riprap and driftwood along the shoreline edge are good rodent
harborage sitting close to housing, in the same way the greenbelt is on the
other side of the district. It is part of the shoreline, it belongs there, and
the work is at the building rather than the bank.

## Renovation, and the moment to act

A great deal of this district is being restored, and renovation is
simultaneously the best and the worst time for pest work.

Best, because an open wall is the only time anyone sees what a century did to
the framing — old carpenter ant galleries, moisture damage around a window that
failed decades ago, a run of soft sill plate, a nest in a cavity nobody could
otherwise reach. Correcting any of it is never cheaper than while the finish is
already off, and sealing penetrations is trivial during a remodel and awkward
forever afterwards.

Worst, because a building under renovation is wide open. Doors propped, sections
of wall off, temporary covers, materials stacked outside, and frequently nobody
there overnight to notice anything. We regularly see buildings acquire problems
during a remodel that they did not have before it started, particularly across
an autumn.

If you are planning work here, the useful sequence is to have somebody look
before the walls close, make envelope sealing part of the project rather than an
afterthought, and be deliberate about the crawlspace or basement while access is
easy.
