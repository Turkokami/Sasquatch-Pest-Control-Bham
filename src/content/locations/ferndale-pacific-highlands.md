## A subdivision built around green space

Pacific Highlands is modern subdivision housing with sidewalks, parks, planted
verges and stormwater management designed into the layout rather than added
afterward.

That design is genuinely good, and it is also the pest story. A development
built around green space has green space threaded through it — buffer strips,
pond margins, park edges, planted corridors between blocks — and every one of
those is permanent, vegetated, undisturbed ground running directly past the
backs of houses.

Combine that with lots close enough that neighbors share fence lines and a
common construction era, and you get the two features that define the work
here: continuous habitat, and problems that behave as a street rather than as
an address.

## The green corridors are the point

It is worth being clear that none of this is a defect. Retaining green space
and managing stormwater on site is better planning than the alternative, and
nobody should read this as an argument against it.

But from where we stand, a vegetated corridor is a vegetated corridor. What
makes it good habitat is exactly what makes it good amenity: it is left rough
rather than manicured, it holds moisture, it carries continuous ground cover,
and almost nobody walks into the middle of it.

The consequence is a standing population living permanently a few yards from
the buildings, rather than arriving seasonally from somewhere distant. The
houses that back onto a buffer, a pond margin or a park edge get materially more
pressure than the houses in the middle of a block, and that difference is
visible in the pattern of calls we get from any street here.

That is not solvable at the source, and it should not be. It is entirely
solvable at the building.

## Stormwater ponds and their margins

Detention and retention ponds are a specific case worth separating out.

The water itself supports very little that troubles a house. What matters is
the margin: a deliberately rough, vegetated buffer that holds moisture
year-round, is maintained rarely and lightly, and often carries dense grass and
scrub. That is high-quality rodent habitat and it is designed to be
high-quality habitat for other reasons.

It also constrains what can be done nearby. Anything applied to ground that
drains to a pond reaches the pond, so work at that boundary moves to enclosed
stations, trapping and exclusion rather than to anything sprayed. That is the
better method for rodents anyway, and it is not a compromise.

The seasonal note is that pond margins get cut, usually once or twice a year,
and when they do the population living in them relocates within days. A house
backing onto a buffer will see activity spike in the week the maintenance
happens. That is predictable, which means the sealing work is best done before
it rather than after.

## Close lots, shared problems

On lots this size the buildings are close enough that a population works
several of them.

A rat living under a deck two doors down is not two doors down in any
meaningful sense; it is thirty yards away on a route that includes your
foundation. A wasp nest in one soffit is in a construction detail that exists
identically on eight other houses. An ant colony under a shared driveway
forages into both.

This is why the calls here come in clusters, and why the most economical work
on a street is often a run of houses at once rather than one at a time. Per
household it is cheaper, it removes the source rather than pushing it next
door, and it is the version that finishes.

Where that is not practical, work on a single house still matters and it is
worth saying why. The route into your building is on your building. Closing it
limits what reaches you regardless of what a neighbor does or does not do. It
is not as good as doing the street. It is much better than nothing.

## Fence lines, and what accumulates along them

The boundary between two close lots is one of the more productive things to
look at here and almost nobody does.

A fence line collects. Leaf litter builds up along it. Shrubs get planted
against it and grow into it. Bins, firewood, garden furniture, bikes, cuttings
and building materials get stored beside it because it is out of the way. On
both sides at once.

The result is a continuous strip of sheltered, undisturbed material running the
whole depth of the lot, connecting the green corridor at the back to the
buildings at the front. It is a route and a harborage in the same feature, and
it exists on nearly every lot in the development.

Clearing a foot either side of a fence line, and keeping stored material off
the ground and away from it, is free, takes an afternoon, and does more than
most treatments.

## One construction era, one set of details

Everything in a development like this went up over a few years to a common
specification, which is unhelpful for character and extremely helpful for
diagnosis.

Every house has the same vent type, the same soffit detail, the same garage
door and slab relationship, the same way the services were run through the rim
joist, and the same trim transitions. So when we find a route on one house, we
know where to look on the rest.

That works in the resident's favor in two ways. A first visit is faster, because
we are checking known details rather than searching. And where several
households want the same work, one specification covers all of them.

The failure points on housing of this age are consistent: service penetrations
sealed with expanding foam, which stops air and water and does not stop a
mouse; crawlspace vent screening stapled over an opening rather than
mechanically fixed into it; the bottom corners of the garage door; and the
voids behind trim at material transitions.

## Rodents, and what a route actually looks like

There is nothing exotic about the way anything gets into these houses, which is
good news.

Look for daylight at the garage door corners from the inside with the door
closed. Check every service penetration — water, waste, gas, electrical, data,
the dryer vent, the heat pump line set — for foam rather than a mechanical
closure. Check crawlspace vents by pulling gently at a corner of the screen.
Check where a deck or patio was built against the house for an unscreened space
underneath.

The one people miss is the roof. Where the green corridor carries mature trees
and a branch reaches a roofline, that is a bridge over everything sealed at
ground level, and rats climb well.

Closing those is a morning of hardware per house and it does not repeat. It is
the highest-value work available on this housing.

## Ants along the irrigated side

The most common non-rodent call here is ants along a foundation, and it is
usually a nuisance rather than damage.

Odorous house ants and pavement ants forage along warm, watered foundation
lines and come indoors along them, typically on the side of the house that gets
both irrigation and afternoon sun. Baiting along the trail resolves it. Spraying
the trail frequently splits the colony and makes the following month worse,
which is why a visible immediate result is not the measure to judge it by.

Carpenter ants are a different animal with a different meaning — they excavate
galleries in wood — and moisture ants are a report about wet timber rather than
a pest problem in their own right. Three insects that look similar to most
people and mean three different things, which is why identification comes
before treatment.

Reducing irrigation against the wall and keeping a gap between planting and
siding does more here than most of what can be applied.

## Wasps, and why the whole street gets them

Wasps in new-construction soffits are on the Ferndale list and this development
demonstrates it.

Modern soffit and fascia detailing produces long continuous voids with small
openings at intervals — vents, joints, the end of a run at a gable — which is
precisely what a queen looks for in spring. They are high up, invisible from the
ground, and nobody inspects them until there is traffic.

Because every house shares the detail, a good nesting year produces nests up
and down a street rather than at one address, which residents reasonably find
alarming and which is entirely normal.

Ground nests along the green corridors and pond margins are the ones that cause
injuries, because they are found by mower rather than by eye. On amenity ground
that is a maintenance hazard as much as a resident one and is worth reporting.

Honey bees we do not treat. If a colony is in a wall we will identify it and
point you at somebody who removes them properly.

## Parks, play equipment and where nothing gets applied

A development designed around parks has play equipment, sports courts and open
grass that children use daily, and that has a straightforward consequence for
how work near them is done.

Nothing gets applied to amenity ground where children play. Where a problem
genuinely sits on that ground, such as a wasp nest in a bank beside a path or
rodent activity around a shelter or a bin, the work is physical and enclosed:
trapping, secured stations, removal of the nest, and closing whatever is
providing the harborage.

Timing matters as much as method. Work near a play area is done when it is not
in use, and where an area needs to stay clear for a period afterward we will
say so plainly and in writing rather than leaving a small sign nobody reads.

This is management ground rather than resident ground, so most of it runs
through the association or the city. If you have found something on a shared
space, reporting it is a great deal more useful than working around it.

## When somebody builds next door

Developments like this fill in over years, and an active construction site
beside occupied houses reliably produces a wave of calls.

Two things are happening at once. Ground is being cleared and disturbed, which
displaces whatever was living in it into the nearest buildings within days. And
a site generates precisely what attracts rodents: stored material, waste,
cover, and quiet at night.

It is temporary, and it helps to know that rather than to assume something has
permanently changed. The work is the same as always, which is to close the
routes into your building, and doing it while the ground next door is being
worked is far better than doing it afterward.

## The autumn crowd on the sunny side

From late September the seasonal visitors arrive on whichever elevations hold
the afternoon sun.

Multicolored Asian lady beetles gather on warm siding and then push into gaps
around trim, laps and vents. Brown marmorated stink bugs do much the same.
Cluster flies accumulate in upstairs windows. Spiders come in for their own
reasons and get noticed at the same time.

Apart from the occasional nip from a lady beetle, none of them bites, and not
one of them breeds indoors or damages the building — which is what decides the
approach rather than a reason to dismiss them. Because they do not
reproduce inside, the number indoors depends entirely on how many got in.
Sealing, plus correctly timed exterior work in the narrow window before the
wave, addresses the supply. Repeated interior spraying through the winter
addresses what is already in the room and nothing else.

This is family housing with small yards, parks and sidewalks, and a great deal

Placement is planned around that. Anything placed goes in enclosed, secured

## What we would rather you did not pay for

A recurring perimeter treatment on a house whose real problem is a foamed
penetration and a green corridor at the back will produce a visible result
indefinitely and will never finish.

A treatment for wasps you have not found yet, sold as prevention, is not worth
what it costs. Looking in June is.

And where the honest answer is a fence line cleared, a bin lid, less water
against the wall and a branch cut back from the roof, none of that is billable

## Sidewalks, streetlights and the insects they gather

A development with continuous street lighting and sidewalks running past every
house has a feature that nobody thinks of as a pest factor, and it accounts for
a noticeable share of the spider calls we get here.

Lighting concentrates flying insects. Insects concentrate the things that eat
them. A house with a bright entry light, a lit garage apron and a streetlight
at the property line is providing a reliable food supply on its own walls every
night from spring through autumn, and the webbing that appears around the eaves
and the porch is the consequence rather than the problem.

Changing to warmer, lower and more directed fittings genuinely reduces it, and
it does so by removing the supply rather than the individuals. It also has the
advantage of being a one-off change rather than something anybody has to keep
paying for, and on a street where the houses share a lighting specification it
is a change the whole row benefits from at once.
