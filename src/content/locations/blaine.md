## Three towns wearing one name

Blaine is the most varied small place we work. Within a few square miles there is
an international land border with its commercial traffic, a working harbor with
commercial and recreational moorage, a resort spit, an older residential town,
and a large stock of houses that stand empty for much of the year. Those are not
variations on a theme. They are four different jobs.

What ties them together is the water and the exposure. Everything here sits
close to salt and close to wind, and both work on a building continuously.

## The marina

Blaine Harbor is a genuine working facility, and marinas have a rodent ecology
of their own.

Rats move along dock lines, through utility runs and between vessels. Storage
lockers, gear sheds and the sheds where people keep bait, line and equipment are
the reservoir. Live-aboard and long-term moored boats add food. The result is a
population that belongs to the facility rather than to any one owner, which is
the single most important thing to understand about it — treating one boat or
one locker while everything around it goes untouched achieves nothing that
lasts.

Our work here is on the shore-side structures and the fixed facilities, and it
is program work rather than visit work: mapped placements, protected from
weather and from non-target animals, on a schedule, with a record. Rodents in a marina are a permanent management problem rather than something you solve once.

Boat owners get a short, useful list. Do not store food aboard over winter. Bait
and pet food belong in hard containers, not in a locker. Shore power cords and
lines are highways, and a rat will use one without hesitation. Anything soft and
fibrous left in a cabin becomes nesting material by February.

## Semiahmoo and the spit

Semiahmoo is its own environment: a narrow sandspit with water on both sides,
serious wind exposure, resort and condominium structures, and residential
property with some of the highest salt exposure in the county.

Wind and salt matter more than anything biological out there. Vents work loose.
Screens corrode. Sealant fails early. Trim and flashing move. Every one of those
is an entry point, and on the spit they open faster than they do a mile inland.

That drives our material choices. Ordinary galvanized hardware cloth is not
adequate in this exposure — it corrodes, and an exclusion that fails in three
winters was money wasted. Marine-grade stainless mesh, appropriate fasteners and
sealants specified for coastal use cost more at the outset and are the only
version of the job worth doing here. We would rather quote the durable version
and explain why than win the work with a cheaper specification that will not
survive.

## Driftwood on the beaches

One local source worth naming, because it does not apply anywhere else we work.
The beaches here accumulate driftwood, and driftwood that has sat wet on a shore
for years is carpenter ant habitat by the time it is picked up. A load carried
home for a firepit, a border, a bit of landscaping or a woodshed brings the
colony with it and sets it down against a building. We find carpenter ants
started this way in Blaine more than anywhere else in the county, and the tell
is that the nest is outdoors, recent, and nowhere near the water damage that
usually explains one.

## Houses that stand empty

A large share of Blaine and Semiahmoo property is seasonal, second homes, or
owned by people who are elsewhere much of the year. Many owners are Canadian and
cross regularly rather than living here.

There is a persistent and completely understandable assumption that an empty
house has fewer pest problems. The opposite is true, and it is one of the most
useful things we can tell a seasonal owner.

An unoccupied house is quiet. Nothing disturbs anything. Lights do not go on,
doors do not open, floors are not walked on. It is dry inside while the whole
county is wet outside. Nobody notices a scratching sound in November, nobody
sees the droppings in December, and nobody spots the wasp nest in the eave
growing through July. So a problem that would be caught in its first week at an
occupied house runs undisturbed for four or five months.

Then the owner arrives in spring, and what would have been a straightforward
call has become soiled insulation, chewed ducting, a stained ceiling or a
substantial nest.

The answer is timing. Get exclusion work done in late summer before you leave —
that is when the crawlspaces are dry, the wasp season is over, and the autumn rodent movement has not started. Have somebody look
at the place mid-winter if you can. And where an owner cannot easily be here, we
can do the assessment while they are in town and schedule the work around their
travel rather than around ours.

Wasps deserve a specific mention for empty houses. A queen founds a nest in
April or May in a sheltered eave or a soffit, and with nobody there to notice, it
grows all summer without interruption. The nests we find at vacant Blaine
properties in August are routinely larger than anything we see at an occupied
house, for exactly that reason.

There is also a practical matter worth settling before you drive north. Anything
inside the building needs somebody able to let us in and authorize the work — an
owner who is in town that week, a neighbor holding a key, a property manager, or
the office at a gated development. We will not enter a house on a verbal
say-so from somebody we cannot confirm has the authority to give it, and a job
that stalls at the door on a Blaine route day usually waits until the next one
rather than being refitted the same afternoon. Ten minutes arranging access in
September saves that entirely.

## The border and the commercial corridor

The Peace Arch and truck crossings put a genuine commercial and logistics
economy into a small town: freight staging, warehousing, brokerage offices, duty
free, fuel and food service, and the parking and yard space that goes with them.

That is commercial pest work and it behaves like commercial pest work anywhere
freight moves. Goods arrive from elsewhere with whatever traveled with them.
Staging yards and open ground support rodent populations that then work into
adjacent buildings. Food service near a crossing runs at odd hours with heavy
turnover. Warehousing has high ceilings, big openings and doors that spend a lot
of the day open by necessity.

Programs here are built on documentation and on realistic goals. A warehouse
with a dock door open eight hours a day is never going to be sealed, so the work
is a defended perimeter, controlled interior placements, and attention to the
sanitation and housekeeping points that actually feed the population. We put
those in the report rather than quietly servicing around them.

## The residential town

Away from the water and the crossing, Blaine is an ordinary older small town,
and it has ordinary older small town problems.

The housing runs from prewar and postwar stock near the original core out
through midcentury development to newer building on the edges. The older houses
carry the familiar Whatcom County pattern: shallow crawlspaces, bare soil, vent
screens that rusted out years ago, and framing that has taken water somewhere
and now supports moisture ants.

Carpenter ants are the persistent one. There is a lot of cedar in this town, a
lot of it is now fifty years weathered, and softened cedar trim and fascia is
what a satellite colony wants. As everywhere in this county, the parent colony is
usually outdoors in a stump or a dead limb, and the job is to find it rather than
to keep treating the trail.

Rodents follow the same seasonal logic as the rest of the county, with the
addition that shoreline riprap, driftwood and the marine bank are good harborage
and sit immediately adjacent to housing in a way they do not inland.

## What the exposure does to a building

Worth setting out plainly, because it explains most of the difference between
working here and working in Bellingham.

Salt accelerates corrosion on every metal component of the building envelope —
vent screens, flashing, fasteners, door hardware, hardware cloth from a previous
exclusion attempt. Wind drives rain horizontally into assemblies designed for
rain that falls downward, and it works trim, siding and flashing loose over
time. Sun and salt together degrade sealants and gaskets faster than they
degrade inland.

Every one of those failures is an opening. So the honest description of pest
work on the Blaine waterfront is that it is largely building envelope work with
a biological motive, and that it needs redoing on a longer cycle than the same
work would inland — inspected periodically rather than assumed to be permanent.

## The Blaine year

The calendar out here runs differently from the inland county, mostly because
the water moderates the temperature and because so much of the housing is
occupied seasonally.

**Winter.** Rodent pressure indoors, concentrated in the empty houses where
nobody is there to hear it. Storm weather works at the building envelope through the winter, opening the gaps that get used later. Very few calls, and most of the damage
that gets discovered in April is happening now.

**Early spring.** Owners return, and this is our busiest stretch for Blaine.
People open a house that has been shut since October and find what moved in.
Ants become active, and in this housing stock they are usually reporting a water problem.

**Late spring.** Nesting is underway and the legal window has closed on anything
with eggs or young in it. Wasp queens are founding nests in eaves and soffits,
particularly at properties standing empty. This is the cheapest possible moment
to deal with a wasp problem and almost nobody is here to notice it.

**Summer.** Peak occupancy and peak wasp activity from July onward as colonies mature and workers switch
from protein to sugar. It is also the right window for exclusion: dry
crawlspaces, and easy access to the exterior.

**Early autumn.** The most important six weeks of the year here, and the most
commonly wasted. Owners are closing houses up and leaving. The rain is arriving
and the rodent movement is beginning. Work done in this window prevents the
winter problem entirely; work deferred to spring is remediation instead.

## Food service, and the small commercial accounts

Blaine has a lot of food handling for its size — the marina, the crossing, the
resort, and a downtown that swells in summer. Small commercial food accounts
here have a particular shape.

Seasonality is the first thing. A kitchen running flat out from June to
September and quietly the rest of the year has a pest profile that tracks that
curve, and a program built on identical monthly visits year-round is both
overservicing the winter and underserving the summer. We would rather scale it.

Outdoor seating and open-air service is the second. Wasps and yellowjackets converge on outdoor food, and by August a patio is a genuine
customer-experience problem rather than a hygiene one. Some of the fix is
physical, some of it is waste handling and timing, and very little of it is
something applied.

Waste is the third and it is where most of the real progress is available. A
dumpster with a broken lid, an enclosure that cannot be closed, or bins staged
outside a back door overnight will support a rodent population that no service schedule overcomes. We put that in the report, because quietly working
around it while billing monthly would be dishonest.

## The other household pests

Away from the water, Blaine gets the same domestic list as the
rest of the county, with local emphasis.

**Spiders** are heavy here, and the autumn wandering season brings the usual
run of calls about large fast spiders indoors. These are almost always giant
house spiders — harmless, established throughout western Washington, and moving
because males are looking for mates rather than because it is cold. Exterior
lighting matters more than people expect: a bright fixture concentrates the
insects that spiders eat, and the spiders follow the food.

**Cluster flies and overwintering insects** find the sunny elevations, which on
the waterfront means the south and west walls that also take the worst of the
weather. They emerge on the first mild afternoon and confuse people who assume
flies belong to summer.

**Carpenter ants and moisture ants** track the same water problems described
above, and in this exposure the water problems are common.

**Rodents** are the constant. Norway rats along the shoreline, the riprap and
the harbor; house mice everywhere; and deer mice in the more rural properties
toward the county line. Where droppings need cleaning up in an enclosed space we
will tell you to ventilate first and avoid sweeping or vacuuming dry material,
because that is the sensible precaution regardless of what the risk turns out to
be in any given building.

## Drayton Harbor and the tidal ground

The shallow water and tidal flats around Drayton Harbor shape the shoreline
properties in a way that is easy to miss from the road.

Tidal flats produce insects in quantity, and insect production feeds everything
above it, the spiders included. Properties backing onto that
ground get a heavier flying-insect load on summer evenings than anywhere inland,
and the exterior lighting question becomes correspondingly more important. A
bright white fixture on a shoreline house on a warm August night is doing more
to create a spider problem than anything else on the property.

Driftwood, riprap and the vegetated bank are excellent rodent harborage sitting
directly against housing. It is not something to be eliminated — it is the
shoreline, and it belongs there — so the work is about the building rather than
the bank: closing the crawl vents properly, keeping the crawl hatch sound, and
making sure the gap where a deck meets the house is not an open invitation.

## Ants and the damp-building list

Blaine's older stock and its exposure combine to produce a predictable set of
moisture-driven problems.

Moisture ants are the honest ones. They colonize framing that is already wet and
already softening, so finding them is a finding about water rather than about
ants. In this town the water is usually arriving horizontally — wind-driven rain
into an assembly designed for rain that falls straight down, or a failed sealant
joint on a weather elevation.

Carpenter ants prefer wood that has begun to soften and keep a parent colony
outdoors, commonly in a stump, a fence post or a woodpile. Treating the trail
indoors without locating that parent is why these jobs get a reputation for
returning.

Underneath both sits the damp-crawl company — sowbugs, millipedes, springtails,
silverfish and Oriental cockroaches — none of which are a serious problem in
themselves and all of which are reporting the same condition. Where a crawlspace
is genuinely wet, the durable answer is drainage and a sound vapor barrier
rather than repeated treatment, and we would rather say so.

## How we work in Blaine

Blaine is twenty-two miles from our shop, mostly freeway, and it sits on
scheduled route days rather than being fitted in between city jobs. We would
rather tell you which day that puts you on than promise a window we cannot hold.

A visit starts outside, with attention to the things this town does to
buildings: the vents, the flashing, the trim, the roof edge, the state of any
previous exclusion work, and where the wind and water actually hit. Then the
crawlspace and the attic. Then the interior.

You get told what we found, what is causing it, what should be done now, what
can wait, and which items are a building repair rather than a pest treatment.
For seasonal owners we will also tell you frankly what is worth doing before you
leave and what can safely wait until you are back.

Inspections are free for every pest we handle here, bed bugs excepted — those
carry a verification fee that is credited against treatment if you proceed.
There is no long-term contract and no multi-year agreement to escape from.
