## The block is the unit

There is one fact that governs pest work in downtown Bellingham, and most people
leasing a unit here have never been told it: **the buildings are joined.**

Party walls. Basements that were once continuous and have been partly divided
since. Service chases and utility runs passing from one building into the next.
Drainage extended and re-extended across a century of changing tenants. Voids
above suspended ceilings that continue straight past a wall you assume is solid.
Old openings from uses nobody remembers — a coal chute, a boiler intake, a
service entry for a utility that no longer exists.

A rodent population downtown is a single population using the whole block. It
has no interest in where one lease ends and the next begins, and it responds to
pressure by moving rather than by leaving.

That has a direct commercial consequence. Treat one tenancy thoroughly and the
animals relocate two doors along, wait, and return when the pressure eases. The
tenant sees a temporary improvement, concludes the treatment is not working, and
either changes provider or accepts monthly service as a permanent overhead. We
have watched businesses do that for years.

Where a building owner, a property manager, or several tenants together will
work to one plan, the result is different in kind rather than incrementally
better. That conversation is harder than selling a single contract and it is the
one worth having.

Where the coordination genuinely is not available — and downtown it often is not
— we will still do good work on your space, seal what can be sealed, and be
straight that what you are buying is control of your premises rather than
elimination of the block's population. Knowing which of those you have bought is
worth something on its own.

## Mixed use, and the vertical dimension

Downtown adds a complication that a purely commercial district does not have:
people live here, frequently directly above the businesses.

That vertical stacking matters because buildings move things upward. Heat rises
through chases. Food odors travel. Plumbing stacks and electrical runs connect
floors, and every one of them is a route. A restaurant at street level and
apartments on the two floors above are far more connected than either party
tends to assume.

So a resident with a cockroach problem in a flat above a kitchen is often
experiencing a building issue rather than a housekeeping one, and treating that
flat repeatedly is treating a symptom. Equally, a restaurant can be doing
everything right and still receive pressure from a neglected residential floor
above it.

Neither of those is an accusation. It is a description of how the building
works, and it is the reason we would rather write a finding that names the
route than quietly bill two separate tenants for the same problem.

For property managers, the practical implication is that a mixed-use building
benefits enormously from a single coordinated program rather than a patchwork of
tenant-arranged contracts, and it usually costs less in total.

## Restaurants and food service

Downtown has a high density of kitchens, and food service in older buildings is
a genuinely specific discipline.

**Voids are where the problem lives.** German cockroaches are indoor,
human-associated insects that persist inside equipment frames, behind and
beneath cabinetry, in the cavity around a dishwasher, in hollow legs and motor
housings. A kitchen can be immaculate at surface level and still hold a
substantial population, which is why owners are so often baffled by a recurring
issue in a clean operation. Surface treatment does very little; the work is at
the harborage.

**They arrive in things.** Cardboard, produce crates, appliances, secondhand
equipment and deliveries from a shared supplier are how German cockroaches move
between premises. In a connected block one operator's receiving practice becomes
everybody's exposure.

**Waste is the largest single lever.** A shared enclosure that will not close
against the ground, a compactor with a failed seal, or cardboard stacked in an
alley overnight will support a rodent population that no service frequency
overcomes. Downtown this is nearly always shared, which makes it both the
biggest opportunity and the hardest thing to change.

We put it in the report. Servicing around an open enclosure while billing
monthly would be dishonest, and it does not work either.

**Drains and grease.** Drain flies breed in the organic film lining floor
drains, sinks and the underside of covers. Spraying surfaces does nothing to
them; mechanical cleaning ends it within a fortnight. Grease interceptors and
the ground around them are their own reservoir and deserve specific attention.

**Timing.** We schedule downtown food service early or between services. A
kitchen we cannot properly access — where equipment cannot be pulled and voids
cannot be opened — is a visit that generates an invoice and very little else.

## The alleys

Downtown alleys are the service corridor for the entire district and they are
where a great deal of the population actually lives.

They concentrate waste from many premises into a short run, they provide
continuous cover and travel routes, they are poorly lit and rarely walked at
night, and they contain a century of accumulated structure — loading docks,
disused stairwells, roll-up doors that no longer seat, basement hatches, and
gaps behind and beneath every one of them.

An alley cannot be treated as though it belonged to one address, and no single
tenant can fix it. What individual businesses can control is their own
contribution: bins that close, cardboard broken down and inside, spillage
cleaned rather than hosed into a drain, and the back door not propped for hours
in warm weather.

Where several premises on one alley coordinate, the difference is visible within
a season. Where they do not, everybody pays for service against a shared food
supply.

## Basements, and what is under downtown

Downtown Bellingham has more below-grade space than most people realize, and it
is frequently the least-visited part of a building.

Old basements carry the district's oldest openings: original service entries,
areaways, window wells filled with debris, sump pits, and abandoned pipe runs
into ground that no longer connects to anything. They are dark, undisturbed and
often damp, which makes them both an entry route and a harborage.

They are also where the connections between buildings are most obvious to
anybody who goes and looks — and the reason a proper first survey downtown takes
longer than people expect. A great deal of the useful information is in a space
nobody has properly examined in years.

Damp basements bring their own list: silverfish, sowbugs, springtails and
Oriental cockroaches, all of which are reporting a moisture condition rather
than being problems in their own right. Where a basement is genuinely wet, the
durable answer is drainage rather than repeated treatment, and we would rather
say so.

## Parking structures

The multi-level parking downtown deserves a specific note because it is nobody's
priority and it affects everything near it.

Stair cores and lift lobbies connect levels vertically. Drainage runs the full
footprint and collects debris. Landscaped edges and planters sit against
structure. Waste from adjacent premises frequently accumulates at the lower
levels, and stored or long-parked vehicles provide sheltered, undisturbed
harborage — a vehicle left for a season is a warm, dry box full of insulation and
wiring.

Rodent damage to vehicle wiring is a real and expensive outcome that owners
rarely associate with the building they parked in.

None of this makes a parking structure treatable in the way a building is. What
it does mean is that a business adjacent to one should understand it as part of
the surrounding pressure, and that where the structure has an owner willing to
address drainage, waste and landscaping, the benefit reaches the buildings
around it.

## Apartments above the shops

Residential downtown is a substantial and growing share of the district, and
multi-unit work follows the same logic as the commercial side.

In a building with shared structure the pests are a building-level population.
Wall voids, plumbing chases and common runs connect units, and insects travel
them regardless of who holds which tenancy. Treating one flat while the ones
either side go untreated moves the problem next door and often back again a
month later. The resident concludes the treatment failed; it did not fail, it
was scoped to the wrong thing.

What resolves it is mapping which units are affected, identifying the likely
source, treating in one coordinated pass, and following up on an interval
matched to the insect rather than to the invoice.

Bed bugs and German cockroaches are the two that most reward this and punish the
alternative hardest. Both move readily between units and both are far cheaper to
resolve early and thoroughly than late and piecemeal.

Bed bug verification is the only assessment we charge for, and the fee comes off
the treatment when you proceed.

## Rodents, and the downtown autumn

The seasonal pattern is the city's, concentrated by the density.

Through the summer rats do well outdoors — the greenbelt edges, the creek
corridors, the vegetated margins and, downtown specifically, the alleys and
their waste. Then the rain sets in through late September and October, the
ground saturates, burrows flood, and the population needs somewhere dry.

Downtown also has a geographic driver that its density alone does not explain.
Whatcom Creek runs through the district on its way to the bay, and a vegetated
creek corridor is a continuous sheltered travel route with water along the whole
of it. Premises backing onto the ravine take pressure from that side as well as
from the alley.

Downtown offers a great deal of somewhere dry: basements, service voids,
suspended ceilings, roof plant, and the gap behind every roll-up door that no
longer seats properly.

The prevention window is late summer, when the exterior work is straightforward
and the movement has not started. For a commercial building that means the
service doors and their thresholds, the roof penetrations, the basement
openings, the enclosure, and the exterior of every pipe and conduit entry.

A building sealed in August has a quiet winter. One that waits until a tenant
reports something in November is dealing with an interior problem in an occupied
premises during trading hours.

## Roofs and service plant

Worth its own note because on downtown buildings this is where problems
originate and nobody looks.

Flat and low-slope roofs carry every mechanical service, and each penetration is
sealed on a much shorter cycle than the roof itself. Ponding where drainage is
imperfect keeps those areas wet and accelerates the failure. Parapets, curbs and
equipment platforms provide shelter.

Once something is through the roof plane it is into the ceiling void, which on
many of these buildings runs the full footprint and connects tenancies. That is
how a problem originating at a rooftop unit presents as droppings above a shop
floor at the other end of the building, and why chasing it at the point of
sighting rarely resolves anything.

Roof-level inspection is part of a proper downtown program and it is the part
most often skipped.

## What we will not sell you

**A block solution from a single-tenancy contract.** If the population is
shared, we will say that plainly before taking the work.

**More devices instead of a fix.** Where the driver is an enclosure that will
not close or a propped service door, adding interior stations is an indefinite
cost with no end date. We would rather write the finding and lose the upsell.

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Birds on a parapet and wildlife in a void are
outside our scope. We do insects and rodents, and we will point you to somebody
for the rest.

**Surface treatment for a void problem.** If the cockroaches are inside the
equipment, spraying the kitchen is theatre.

## How we work downtown

For a business we start with your operating constraints — trading hours, service
times, access, what may be placed where, and any audit or record requirement —
because those shape everything after them.

Then the exterior and the alley, the waste situation honestly assessed, the
service doors and thresholds, the basement, the ceiling void, and the roof. The
interior last, because by then we usually know what we are looking for.

For a residential building we start with the manager: how many units, which are
affected, what connects them, and where the refuse goes.

Either way you get a plain account — what we found, what is driving it, what
needs doing now, what can wait, and which items belong to a building owner, a
waste contractor or a maintenance budget rather than to us. In a district of
joined buildings, a good share of the answer is somebody else's to authorize,
and naming that clearly is more useful than working around it.

Assessments are free for every pest we handle except bed bug verification, and
no term agreement is required.

## Stored product, retail and back-of-house

Not every downtown business is a kitchen, and the non-food premises have their
own recurring issue that gets misread as a building problem.

Beetles and moths in dry goods — grain, flour, spices, pet food, birdseed, dried
botanicals, and the ingredients behind a café counter — arrive **inside the
product** rather than through a door. That single fact changes the entire
response, because treating a building perimeter for an insect that came in on a
pallet accomplishes precisely nothing.

The work is tracing back to the source. Start with whatever has been sitting
longest and furthest back, check spillage in shelving seams, under racking and
in the corners of a stockroom, and be systematic rather than hopeful. In most
premises the source turns out to be a single damaged bag or an old spill quietly
producing adults for months.

After that it is stock rotation, sealed containers, cleaning residue out of
seams, and monitoring long enough to confirm it is genuinely finished. We would
rather write that up than sell a treatment schedule that leaves the source
sitting where it was.

Retail back-of-house has a second issue worth naming: cardboard. Stock arrives
in it, it accumulates faster than it gets broken down, and a stack of flattened
boxes in a warm back room is both harborage and a transport route. Where a
business gets on top of cardboard handling, the improvement is quick and it
costs nothing.

## Vacancy between tenancies

Downtown turns over, and an empty unit behaves differently from an occupied one.

Nobody is there to notice anything. Heating is often reduced or off. Utilities
may be disconnected, which means dry drain traps — and a dry trap is an open pipe
from the sewer into the room, which is a genuine and frequently overlooked route
for both odor and insects. Post accumulates behind a door. A back door gets
propped during fit-out and stays propped.

So a unit that sat empty over a winter is frequently handed to a new tenant with
a problem already in it, and that tenant then spends their first trading months
believing they caused it.

The useful moment is the vacancy itself. An empty unit can be surveyed properly,
treated without working around trading hours, and sealed without moving anybody's
equipment. Nothing has to be scheduled around a service. For a landlord it is by
some margin the cheapest point in the cycle to deal with anything, and it is the
point most often skipped.

Running water through every drain periodically during a vacancy costs nothing
and closes one of the more unpleasant routes.

If you are taking on a downtown unit, ask what was there before, ask how long it
stood empty, and have somebody look before your equipment goes in. It is a
twenty-minute conversation that occasionally saves a very expensive first
quarter.
