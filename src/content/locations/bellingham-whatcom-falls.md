## The neighborhood is defined by its edge

Whatcom Falls is named for the park, and the park is not a detail of the
neighborhood. It is the organizing fact of it.

Two hundred and forty acres of forest, creek, gorge, trail and open water sit in
the middle of this part of Bellingham, with Whatcom Creek running through it from
the Lake Whatcom outlet down toward the bay. Houses face it, back onto it, look
down into the ravine at it and sit across quiet streets from it. Something in the
region of a mile and a half of residential boundary touches public land or the
creek corridor.

That produces a situation we do not encounter anywhere else in the city at this
scale: a large, permanent, high-quality habitat directly adjacent to housing,
which nobody can treat, clear, drain or fence.

We want to be plain about the consequence, because it shapes every honest
recommendation on these streets. You cannot reduce the pressure. You can only
change what happens when the pressure arrives at your building. That single
sentence is the difference between work that holds here and work that does not.

## Why this is an exclusion neighborhood

In most of Bellingham a pest problem has a source you can do something about. A
woodpile against the wall, a leaking hose bib, a neglected corner of a yard, a
gap under a shed. Remove it and the problem loses its base.

Here the source is a public park. It supports rodents, wasps, spiders and
insects in quantity because that is exactly what a healthy riparian forest is
supposed to do, and it will do so permanently.

So the leverage moves. It moves off the source, which is fixed, and onto the
boundary — the last stretch of your own ground, and the building envelope itself.

That means the work that pays here is unglamorous and physical. Sealing entry
points to a standard that actually excludes a mouse rather than looks tidy.
Screening vents in metal rather than plastic. Closing the gap where the deck
ledger meets the wall. Fitting proper crawlspace access covers. Correcting grade
so soil is not bridging to siding. Cutting the branch that reaches the roof.

It is less satisfying to sell than a treatment and it is what works. A quarterly
spray on a property backing onto two hundred acres of forest is, in practical
terms, a subscription to a problem rather than a resolution of one.

## What the riparian buffer means for advice

There is a complication here that most pest control advice ignores entirely, and
homeowners deserve to hear it before they act on generic guidance.

Land close to Whatcom Creek falls within the city's critical areas protections.
The vegetation inside that buffer is regulated. Clearing it, cutting into it or
grading within it is not something a property owner can simply decide to do, and
in some cases work there requires permits or is not permitted at all.

This matters because the standard rodent advice everywhere else — cut the cover
back hard, clear the vegetation, open up the ground — can be exactly the wrong
instruction on a creekside lot. Somebody following it enthusiastically can end up
in a genuine compliance problem, and in a worse position ecologically, having
removed the bank stabilization that keeps their own slope where it is.

Our position is straightforward. We do not advise clearing inside the buffer. We
work with the vegetation where it is, and we concentrate on the zone between the
protected margin and the building, which is generally unregulated and is where
the useful work sits anyway.

If you are unsure where your buffer begins, the city's planning department will
tell you, and it is worth knowing before anybody with a chainsaw offers to solve
your rodent problem.

## The creek and what we are willing to apply

Whatcom Creek is a salmon-bearing stream. It runs to the hatchery. It is not a
drainage ditch and we do not treat it as one.

That has a direct effect on our product decisions on this side of the
neighborhood, and we would rather commit to it here in writing than mention it
in passing on site.

Near the water we lead with exclusion and habitat correction over chemistry.
Where a product is genuinely needed, we prefer contained and targeted placement
to anything broadcast — bait in tamper-resistant stations, crack and crevice
work, void treatment — over perimeter applications that sit exposed on the
ground.

We watch rainfall. An exterior application on saturated ground with weather
coming is how a product ends up somewhere it was never intended to go, and on a
slope running to a fish-bearing creek that is not an abstract concern.

And where the honest answer is drainage, grading, screening or a repair, we say
so instead of treating. On these lots that is frequently the answer, and it is
the recommendation that costs us the recurring visit.

## Rats and the corridor

The creek corridor is a rodent highway, and it does not close for winter.

A watercourse with continuous cover along both banks provides the three things a
rat population needs — water, food and concealed movement — in one connected
line running the length of the neighborhood. Animals travel it, breed along it
and push outward from it into gardens and buildings at the edges.

This is why rat pressure here has a different rhythm from the rest of the city.
In drier neighborhoods, populations contract hard in the cold months. Along the
creek there is enough resource and enough shelter that the corridor sustains
numbers through the winter, and the pressure on adjacent houses is closer to
constant than seasonal.

The park's own use adds to it. Waste bins, food dropped along well-used trails
and the places people gather produce a supplementary food supply, and a fed
population expands at its margins. That is not an argument against the park, it
is simply the reason a house here needs to be tighter than a house on a street
half a mile away.

What we do about it is boundary work. Getting the last thirty feet of your own
property unrewarding — stored materials up off the ground, feeders reconsidered,
fallen fruit dealt with, compost properly contained, no accessible pet food, no
sheltered voids under decks and sheds. Then the building itself, closed
properly, checked at the roofline as well as the base, because a rat that can
reach a branch can reach a soffit.

One habit deserves naming, because it is close to universal on a lot backing
onto the ravine and nobody thinks of it as feeding anything. Grass clippings,
prunings, leaves and the occasional bag of spent soil go over the back fence
into the trees, where they are out of sight and out of the yard waste bill. It
is dumping on ground that is not yours, and it builds precisely the loose,
rotting, sheltered pile a rat family will nest in — six feet from your siding
rather than six hundred feet away in the park. A closed compost bin or a run to
the disposal site keeps that heap from being the thing your exclusion work is
competing with.

## Deer mice, and why they are treated differently

Alongside the rats, the wooded margin here delivers deer mice, and they get a
different response.

They are small enough to use openings a homeowner will not consider an opening,
they come in as the weather turns rather than living inside all year, and they
are the local species associated with hantavirus. That last point changes the
handling rather than the anxiety level — the risk is low, and it is managed by
not disturbing dry droppings and nesting material, ventilating a closed space
first, wetting the material rather than sweeping or vacuuming it dry, and
cleaning up rather than leaving it in place.

Practically, mice on this side of the neighborhood are an autumn problem with a
building-envelope answer. Traps deal with the animals already inside. Sealing
decides whether there is a new cohort next October.

## Water under the house

Ask us what we find most often in crawlspaces near the creek and the answer is
not an insect. It is water.

The water table in the low ground here responds to the creek and to the season.
A crawlspace that is merely damp in August can hold standing water in February,
and a lot of the housing stock sits at a level where that is normal rather than
exceptional.

What follows from a wet underfloor space is predictable. Moisture ants, which
want wood that is already wet and are a symptom of a water problem rather than a
pest problem in their own right. Springtails, sowbugs and millipedes in numbers.
Silverfish. Decay in joists and beams, which in this climate takes structural
capacity faster than any insect does. And a general elevation of everything that
lives on the ground beneath your floor.

The response is drainage and separation rather than treatment. Roof water
discharged well clear of the building. Grade falling away instead of toward.
Crawl vents open, screened and not blocked by landscaping. A vapor barrier
covering the ground properly, lapped and complete, rather than in torn sheets.
Sump provision where the ground genuinely holds water.

Treating moisture ants in a wet crawlspace without addressing the water is one of
the more reliable ways to be sold the same job repeatedly, and we will not do it
that way.

## Carpenter ants from the forest side

The park's forest supplies carpenter ants the way the creek supplies rodents,
and the pattern here is worth distinguishing from the neighborhood's other
timber pests.

The material they want is dead and softening rather than living and sound. The
park has it in abundance, permanently and by design — standing snags, fallen
timber, stumps, dead limbs in healthy crowns. That reservoir is not going
anywhere.

What we can influence is the last stretch. Firewood stacked against a wall.
Landscape timbers and old retaining structures rotting quietly at the back of a
garden. Fence posts failing at ground level. Deck framing that has been wet for
years. Branches bridging from the treeline to a roof.

Inside the building the tell is a satellite nest rather than a colony: coarse
frass beneath a window, workers on a counter after dark, a dry rustle in a wall
on a warm night. The main nest may well be in the park, in which case the honest
account is that we manage the building rather than eliminate the source, and we
would rather say that than imply a permanence we cannot deliver.

## Wasps at the park boundary

Late summer along the park edge is a wasp neighborhood.

Yellowjackets nest in ground cavities, banks, root plates and the undisturbed
margins between kept garden and wild ground — which describes the boundary of
nearly every property here. Colonies peak in August and September, and that is
also when people are outside using the ground where the nests are.

Bald-faced hornets take the gray paper nests in trees, and along a wooded
boundary they are frequently not spotted until the leaves come down, by which
point the colony is finishing on its own.

Paper wasps use eaves, deck rails and open sheds, and where a nest is somewhere
nobody goes, we will often recommend leaving it be rather than treating it for
the sake of doing something.

We treat what is near where you live and walk. We do not treat the treeline, and
anybody offering to make a park boundary wasp-free in August is selling
something that does not exist.

## Spiders, and the honest version

The wooded, damp edge here produces insects in quantity, and everything that
eats insects follows. Spider volume on these streets is genuinely higher than
the city average and there is no treatment that changes that for long.

What people notice in September and October is mostly males walking, looking for
females. It reads as an infestation and it is a mating season. It ends.

The measures that actually reduce what you see indoors are dull: door sweeps and
weatherstripping, screens repaired, exterior lighting reconsidered or shifted to
warmer color temperatures that draw fewer insects, webs knocked down regularly
so they are not re-used, and vegetation kept off the walls where that is outside
the protected buffer.

On the two spiders people ask about specifically — the brown recluse does not
live in western Washington and we have never encountered one here; the black
widow is possible in Whatcom County but rare, and in the damp wooded ground of
this neighborhood distinctly unlikely. The bites people worry about in this part
of the state are far more often something else entirely.

## The housing itself

The neighborhood was not built in one push, which means there is no single
failure pattern to look for.

Post-war and mid-century houses sit alongside considerably older stock and
newer infill, and a fair number of properties step down toward the ravine, which
produces daylight basements, partial crawlspaces and split levels. Those
transitions — where a crawl meets a basement, where an addition meets the
original building, where a deck is framed into a slope — are where the gaps are,
and they are gaps a generic perimeter treatment never touches.

Older exterior joinery, single-skin garages, original crawl vents with failed
screens, and utility penetrations added over decades without much sealing are
all normal here and all worth twenty minutes of attention.

## What we will not sell you here

**A promise to solve the park.** We cannot, nobody can, and any proposal built
on that premise is dishonest.

**Clearing inside the creek buffer.** It is regulated ground, it is holding your
bank together, and it is not the fix.

**Broadcast product near the water.** There is a hatchery downstream and we
would rather do the work properly than quickly.

**A recurring plan over an unaddressed wet crawlspace.** That is the local
version of paying forever for a symptom.

**Wildlife or bird work.** Backing onto a park, that question comes up
constantly. It is outside what we do — we handle insects and rodents, and we
will point you to somebody properly equipped for the rest.

## How we work a property on this boundary

We start at the fence line rather than the front door, because on these lots the
useful information is at the back.

We walk your boundary with the park or the ravine and look at the last thirty
feet of ground you actually control — cover, stored material, timber on soil,
food sources, the state of any structure sitting near the line. We note where the
buffer begins so that nothing we recommend puts you on the wrong side of it.

Then the building envelope, methodically. Base of walls and grade. Vents,
screens and crawl access. Utility penetrations. Deck ledgers and framing
junctions. Roofline, soffits and anything overhanging. The places an addition
meets the original house.

Then underneath, properly and with a light, all the way through rather than a
look from the hatch — because on this ground the crawlspace usually holds the
explanation.

You get a direct account: what is here, what is driving it, which parts we can
resolve and which parts we can only manage because their source is on public
land. We separate the work into what needs doing now, what can wait, and what
belongs to a drainage contractor, an arborist or a builder instead of to us.

Assessments are free for every pest we handle, with the single exception of bed
bug verification, which is charged and then credited against the treatment if
you go ahead. Nothing here requires a term agreement, and on a property backing
onto the park we would rather sell you a well-sealed building once than a
quarterly visit forever.
