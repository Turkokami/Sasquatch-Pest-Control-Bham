## Where lot size changes the work

Edgemoor is the Bellingham neighborhood where the size of a property genuinely
alters how the job is done, and it is worth being specific about why.

Most pest work anywhere in this city is bounded. A house sits on a modest lot,
the perimeter is a short walk, and the small number of places a problem can
originate are all within sight of the building. You survey the property in
twenty minutes because the property is twenty minutes long.

Here that assumption breaks. Properties run to half an acre and considerably
more, much of it wooded, with mature trees, understory, garden structures and
often several buildings. The land beyond the lawn is not landscaping; it is
habitat, and it belongs to the same address.

That changes two things. It changes where the source of a problem can be, and it
changes how long it honestly takes to find.

## Carpenter ants at scale

Edgemoor produces more carpenter ant work per property than anywhere else we
operate, and the reason is straightforward: the neighborhood has more of what
they want.

The biology is the same everywhere. They do not consume wood — they hollow
galleries in it to nest — and they favor timber that has already begun to
soften. What differs here is supply. A mature wooded lot carries standing
deadwood, old stumps left from original clearing, fallen limbs, snags, decades of
accumulated garden timber, retaining structures, and firewood in quantity.

And crucially, a mature population organizes itself with a **main colony
outdoors** and satellite nests inside a building. The satellites are what a
homeowner sees: workers on a counter, coarse frass beneath a window, a rustle in
a wall at night. The main colony can be a considerable distance away and still
supply them.

On a small city lot that main colony is one of perhaps six candidates and you
find it quickly. On two wooded acres it might be a hundred feet into the trees,
in a stump nobody has thought about since the house was built.

That is the honest difficulty of working here, and it is why the useful visit is
a search rather than a treatment. Where we find the main colony, the result
holds for years. Where we cannot, we will say so plainly and explain what a
building-focused approach can and cannot achieve, rather than implying we have
resolved something we have not.

## What a thorough search actually involves

Because this comes up in quoting, it is worth setting out.

A proper search on a large wooded lot means walking the whole property rather
than the building line: the treeline and understory, standing dead limbs and
snags, stumps from clearing and from later removals, fallen wood left to
decompose, old garden and landscape timber, retaining structures, fence lines,
the firewood store, sheds, and anywhere timber meets soil.

It means following foraging trails, which requires being there when the ants are
active rather than when the appointment was convenient. It means opening
suspicious timber rather than looking at it. And on a genuinely large property it
can be a half-day of work before anything is treated.

We would rather quote that honestly than sell a cheap visit that will not find
anything. Sometimes the source is obvious within twenty minutes and we say so and
charge accordingly. Sometimes it is not, and the choice belongs to the owner:
pay for a thorough search with a good chance of a durable result, or take a
building-focused approach that manages the symptom. Both are legitimate. Only one
of them is usually presented.

## The trees themselves

A point worth making plainly, because anxious homeowners sometimes reach for the
wrong solution.

A healthy, living mature tree is rarely the problem. Carpenter ants want dead and
softening wood, which means the material that matters is standing deadwood, dead
limbs within an otherwise healthy crown, old stumps, and fallen timber. Removing
a sound Douglas fir because there are ants in the kitchen is an expensive
misdiagnosis and a real loss, given that those trees are much of why the
neighborhood is what it is.

What is worth doing is more modest. Dead limbs come out, which is a tree
surgeon's job and good for the tree regardless. Stumps get ground or removed
where they are close to buildings. Fallen wood is either cleared or accepted as
habitat and kept well away from structures. Branches that bridge to a roof get
cut back, which closes a route.

Where a tree genuinely is failing and is close to a building, that is a
conversation with an arborist rather than with us, and we will say so.

## Several buildings, one property

Most Edgemoor addresses have more than one structure, and the outbuildings are
the single most commonly overlooked part of a scope.

Detached garages, studios and workshops. Greenhouses and potting sheds. Garden
stores, wood stores and pump houses. Guest accommodation. Pool and equipment
buildings. Older structures that came with the land and have been repurposed
several times.

From a rodent's point of view those are frequently more attractive than the
house: less disturbed, often unheated but sheltered, full of stored material, and
rarely inspected. A population based in a detached garage is a permanent supply
fifty feet from the back door, and a program that treats the house alone leaves
it completely intact.

They matter for carpenter ants too. An old timber-framed garden structure sitting
on soil is exactly the softening wood a colony wants, and on several properties
here the main nest has turned out to be in a garden building rather than in the
trees at all.

So a proper scope here includes the outbuildings. Sometimes the honest answer is
that a particular structure is beyond sealing and the sensible course is to
remove what attracts them and defend the house properly. We will say which it is
rather than quietly quoting for everything.

## A neighborhood with no single housing era

Unlike the hillside neighborhoods that went up in one concentrated push, Edgemoor
has been built out across a very long period, and that has a practical
consequence.

There is no template here. A house may date from the 1920s or from last year,
and everything between is represented. Older properties carry the classic
regional pattern — shallow crawlspaces over bare earth, foundation vents whose
screens failed decades ago, accumulated penetrations, framing that has taken
water somewhere. Midcentury houses bring cedar and daylight basements. Newer
construction brings a tight envelope with a handful of service penetrations
sealed with foam.

Many properties are also a composite: an older house extended two or three times
across different decades, with junctions between old framing and new that are
rarely as tight as either section on its own.

The implication is simply that your building has to be read on its own terms.
Somebody arriving with a fixed idea of what an Edgemoor house is will miss
things, and the survey has to start with the construction rather than with
assumptions about the neighborhood.

## Rodents on a wooded lot

The seasonal pattern is the regional one, delivered more efficiently than it is
in town.

Rats, house mice and deer mice live comfortably in wooded ground through the
summer. When the autumn rain saturates the soil and floods burrows, everything
needs somewhere dry within a few weeks, and here the cover runs continuously from
deep habitat right up to the buildings. Nothing has to cross open ground.

Deer mice are more prominent on these lots than in the dense parts of the city.
Where droppings need clearing in an enclosed space — a shed, a crawlspace, a
garage that has been shut up — ventilate the area first and avoid sweeping or
vacuuming dry material. That is a sensible precaution regardless of what the risk
turns out to be in a given building, and it costs nothing to do it that way.

The prevention window is August, when the ground and crawlspaces are dry, the
exterior is accessible, and the movement has not begun. On a property with
several buildings that is a larger piece of work than a single house, which is
another reason to plan it rather than react to it.

## Water, drainage and the ground

Large wooded lots handle water differently from small ones, and it produces
findings people do not expect.

Ground shaded by mature canopy stays wet longer. Leaf litter and needle drop
accumulate against foundations and in drainage, holding moisture where you least
want it. Roof water on a large house is a substantial volume and it has to go
somewhere — frequently to a soakaway or a discharge point that was adequate
thirty years ago and is now silted or overgrown.

Wherever that water ends up sitting, the moisture-driven problems follow. Ants
that need wet timber. Decay in framing, which in this climate costs structural
capacity faster than insects do. The damp-crawlspace population of sowbugs,
millipedes, springtails and silverfish.

A great deal of what we recommend on these properties is drainage, grading and
clearing rather than treatment: getting roof water discharged well away, keeping
litter off the foundation line, opening up drainage that has silted, and
maintaining a dry band at the base of the walls. It is unglamorous and it holds,
and it costs us the recurring service to say so.

## Wasps on large grounds

Extensive grounds mean more places for a nest and more chance of an unpleasant
discovery.

Yellowjackets take ground cavities along banks, in root plates, under stored
timber, beneath sheds and in the undisturbed margin between a lawn and the
treeline. On a property where somebody is mowing, strimming, clearing or
gardening across a large area in late summer, that is a genuine risk — the colony
is at maximum size and defensiveness in exactly the weeks the grounds get the
most attention.

Bald-faced hornets build the large gray paper nests in trees, and on a wooded lot
they are often not noticed until autumn leaf fall, by which point the colony is
finishing naturally.

Paper wasps take eaves, deck rails, sheds and open outbuildings, and where they
are somewhere nobody goes we will frequently suggest leaving them alone rather
than treating for the sake of it.

The practical measure on a big property is a spring walk before the grounds get
busy, and knowing where nests are even where they will not be treated, so that
whoever does the grounds work can be told.

## The saltwater edge

The western margin of the neighborhood runs out to Chuckanut Bay, and the lots
that sit on or near that edge behave differently again from the wooded interior.

Exposure is the first difference. A west-facing elevation above the water takes
wind-driven rain in a way a sheltered inland wall never does, and it takes it at
the joints — window heads, trim returns, deck ledgers, the top of a rake board.
Water that gets behind cladding at those points keeps a narrow band of framing
damp through the winter without ever showing on the inside. That band is what
carpenter ants and moisture ants are looking for, and it is the reason a house
on the bluff can have an ant problem while an identical house two streets inland
does not.

Salt air compounds it by shortening the working life of the metal that holds the
weather out. Flashing, fasteners, vent screens and crawl vent mesh corrode
faster on this side of the neighborhood, and a vent screen that has rusted
through is an open door for rodents that nobody has decided to leave open.
Replacing failed mesh on exposed elevations is routine exclusion work here and
it is cheap compared with what it prevents.

The shoreline itself brings its own margin — rock, driftwood, beach grass and
the tangle of vegetation above the tideline. It is good rodent habitat and it is
permanent, so the objective on a waterside lot is never to clear it. It is to
make the eighty feet between that habitat and the building unrewarding, and to
make the building itself genuinely closed.

## Houses that stand empty for stretches

A fair number of properties here are unoccupied for weeks at a time — travel,
second residences, a season spent elsewhere. That changes the pest picture in
one specific way: nothing gets noticed early.

A rodent that enters an occupied house is usually heard within days. In an empty
house it has weeks of quiet, and what would have been a two-trap job becomes
droppings through a pantry, chewed wiring insulation and a contaminated section
of loft. The same applies to a slow leak feeding an ant colony, or a wasp nest
building in a soffit above a door nobody uses.

The answer is not a subscription. It is a closed building before the empty
period starts, and somebody walking it while you are away who knows what to look
at. We are glad to be that walk-through, and equally glad to tell you it is not
needed if the building is already tight.

## Discretion and access

Two practical notes, because they come up here more than elsewhere.

Access varies. Long private drives, gated entrances, parking well away from the
building, and crawl access on the far side of a large house from anywhere a
vehicle can stop. None of that is an obstacle, but it changes what we can bring
and how long a job takes, so it is worth a sentence when you call.

And the work here is frequently done around people's lives on properties where
that matters. We schedule around it, we are quiet about what we find, and we do
not put anybody's address in marketing material. That should not be remarkable
and we mention it because clients occasionally ask.

## What we will not sell you

**Tree removal for an ant problem.** A healthy mature tree is very rarely the
nest, and losing one to a misdiagnosis is expensive and irreversible.

**A cheap search on a large lot presented as thorough.** If finding the source
needs half a day, we will say that and let you decide, rather than doing twenty
minutes and calling it done.

**A house-only scope where the outbuildings are the source.** We will include
them or explain why they are excluded.

**Recurring service over an unresolved drainage problem.** On these grounds that
is the easiest way to be overcharged indefinitely.

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Wildlife and birds are outside what we do,
and on a wooded acre that question comes up frequently. We handle insects and
rodents and will point you to somebody equipped for the rest.

## How a visit works here

We start with the grounds rather than the building, which is the reverse of most
of our work and the right order on a property this size. The treeline and
understory, deadwood and stumps, fallen timber, garden and landscape structures,
the firewood, drainage and where water actually goes, and every outbuilding.

Then the building exterior: grade, vents, penetrations, the deck and its ledger,
branches reaching the roof, and litter accumulation against the walls.

Then underneath and inside — properly, with a light, working the whole crawlspace
rather than reporting from the hatch.

You get a plain account of what we found and what is driving it, what needs doing
now, what can wait, and which items belong to an arborist, a drainage contractor
or a builder rather than to us. On a large wooded property a substantial share of
the honest answer is grounds management, and we say it even though it is not
work we are paid for.
