## A strip of sand with water on both sides

Semiahmoo is a spit — a narrow bar of sand reaching out between Semiahmoo Bay
on one side and Drayton Harbor on the other, with a resort, two golf courses, a
marina of some three hundred slips, condominium blocks and gated waterfront
housing strung along it.

Nothing here is far from saltwater. That single fact drives more of the pest
work on the spit than the housing stock does, and it drives it in two
directions at once: what the salt does to a building, and what the shoreline
supports right up against it.

The second defining fact is occupancy. A large share of the housing here is
second homes, seasonal residences and rentals, which means a great many
buildings sit closed for months with nobody walking through them. That changes
the nature of the work more than any construction detail does, and it is worth
starting there.

## An empty building is a different problem

Pest problems in occupied houses get caught early, and they get caught by
accident. Somebody sees a droppings trail on a counter, hears something in a
wall at two in the morning, notices a wasp coming in and out of the same gap
every afternoon. None of that requires expertise. It requires a person being
present.

Remove the person and the whole early-warning system goes with it. A mouse that
gets in during the first week of November is not discovered in November. It is
discovered in April, by which time it has bred, the insulation is fouled, there
is a smell in the wall and the entry point has been widened by use.

The practical consequence is that seasonal property should be inspected on a
schedule rather than on symptoms, because the symptoms are not going to reach
anybody. Two visits a year covers it for most buildings here: one in early
autumn before it is closed up, one in spring before it is reopened.

The autumn visit is the valuable one. It is the point at which the openings can
still be closed before the animals that are about to use them try.

## What the salt does to a sealed building

Exclusion work is the most durable pest control there is. Seal an opening
properly and it stays sealed, which is why so much of what this company does is
physical rather than chemical.

On the spit that has to be qualified, and we would rather qualify it than let
somebody discover it themselves in three years.

Salt-laden air on both sides of a narrow strip of land attacks the specific
materials exclusion depends on. Galvanized mesh corrodes from the cut edges.
Fasteners rust and lose their grip in the substrate. Door sweeps and weather
seals harden, crack and pull away. Flashing lifts as the fixings behind it
degrade. Vent screens go first, and they go from the ground side where nobody
looks.

None of that is a failure of the work. It is a coastal building doing what
coastal buildings do, and the answer is stainless or coated hardware where it
matters, and an honest expectation that a sealed building on a spit needs
looking at on a cycle rather than once. We would rather quote a maintained
result than promise a permanent one and be wrong.

## Condominiums, and why a unit is not a building

A substantial part of the housing here is attached — condominium blocks,
attached waterfront homes, buildings where several households share a
structure.

That changes the arithmetic of a treatment completely, and it is the single
most common source of frustration we hear about on the spit.

A shared-wall building is continuous behind the drywall. Wall voids, plumbing
and electrical chases, soffits, attic spaces and crawl areas frequently run
between units without a break. A rodent or insect population living in that
shared volume does not recognize your unit as a separate place. It treats it as
one room in a larger territory, and it moves between rooms freely.

So when a single unit is treated in isolation, what happens is predictable.
Activity in that unit drops. The population, undisturbed in the shared space,
carries on. Within weeks the unit is reoccupied, and the owner reasonably
concludes the treatment failed.

It did not fail. It was asked to do something it cannot do.

The work that actually resolves this is done at the building level, with the
association or the manager, addressing the shared voids and the exterior of the
whole structure. That is a harder conversation to start than a single service
call, and it is the one worth having. Where an owner wants unit-level work
anyway, we will do it, and we will tell them plainly what it will and will not
achieve.

## The marina and everything stored around it

A marina concentrates the four things that produce rodent populations, in one
place, permanently.

There is food — bait, catch, galley stores, waste containers. There is water.
There is continuous shelter in gear lockers, storage units, covered walkways
and the boats themselves. And there is very little disturbance, because most of
that storage is opened occasionally rather than daily.

Add the seasonality of the housing around it, and there is a large sheltered
resource with nobody watching it for long stretches.

What makes marina work different from house work is that the unit of the job is
the site rather than the building. Lockers stand in rows sharing walls and
roofs. Sealing one while the two either side stay open does not remove
anything; it relocates it a few feet. Useful work here means agreement across
the operator and the tenants, coordinated placements, and attention to waste
handling — which is unglamorous and does more than anything applied from a can.

Boats themselves are worth a separate mention. A vessel laid up for the winter
with soft furnishings, stored food and no traffic through it is an extremely
good place to be a rodent, and the damage found in spring is routinely to
wiring and upholstery rather than anything obvious.

## Carpenter ants out of the driftwood

Carpenter ants are on the Blaine list for a reason the owner of this company
gave directly: on this shoreline a meaningful share of them come out of beach
wood rather than out of the forest.

It makes sense once stated. Carpenter ants do not eat wood — they excavate
galleries in it to nest, and they excavate softened, damp wood far more readily
than sound dry timber. A drift pile that has been sitting on a tideline for
years is exactly that material, in quantity, and it is often within a hundred
feet of a building.

The building side of it is the usual story: a mature colony outdoors pushes
satellite colonies outward, and a wall void or a rim joist suits a satellite as
well as another log does. What is unusual here is how close the source can be
and how little it looks like a nest.

Practically, that makes ground clearance a real part of treatment on this
shoreline rather than a suggestion. Where large accumulations of beach wood and
stacked material sit close to a structure, moving them is often the largest
single thing that can be done, and it is not something we charge for doing.

## Landscaping on a spit, and what it hides

Planting here has to survive salt and wind, which pushes people toward dense,
tough, low evergreen material planted thickly and allowed to fill in.

It works, it looks appropriate, and it produces a continuous band of permanent
ground cover directly against the building — which is a rodent corridor and a
moisture trap in the same feature.

We are not going to tell anybody to tear out their planting. What is worth
doing is far smaller: keep an actual gap at the base of the wall so the
foundation line can be seen and reached, keep material off the siding rather
than resting against it, and accept that where a bed runs tight to a building
the wall behind it cannot be inspected and will not be.

Irrigation matters here too. On free-draining sand, planting gets watered, and
water applied against a foundation is the beginning of the moisture story that
carpenter ants and wood-boring insects need.

One more thing about the planting that is specific to a spit: what gets swept
or blown into a bed against a wall stays there. Wind here moves sand, leaf
litter and beach debris continuously, and a dense bed against a foundation
collects all of it into a deepening layer of damp organic material that nobody
turns over. Clearing that out once a year does more for the wall behind it than
anything applied to the wall itself.

## Gulls, and the birds nobody wants to discuss

Shoreline property attracts shoreline birds, and on the spit that means gulls
in numbers, along with the nesting they do on flat roofs, parapets and
sheltered ledges.

The pest consequence is not really the birds. It is what accumulates: nesting
material in gutters and drains, droppings on surfaces, and the insects that
live in old nests and then move inward when the nest is abandoned. Bird mites
and carpet beetles both do this, and both are frequently diagnosed as something
else entirely because the connection to a nest twenty feet up is not obvious.

Much of this is protected, and the timing and the method matter. Where there is
a nest we will say what can lawfully be done and when, and where the answer is
"not until it is finished" we will say that too.

## The under-floor space and the ground it sits on

Sand drains fast, which is a genuine advantage, and it makes people assume
moisture is not an issue here. Under a closed-up building it can be.

An unoccupied house has no heating cycle, no air movement and no one opening a
door. Marine air is humid year-round on this shoreline, and a cold, still,
unventilated under-floor space in that air will hold moisture regardless of how
well the ground drains.

That is how a spit property ends up with the same softened rim joist and the
same fouled insulation as a house in a wet inland valley, by an entirely
different route. It is worth looking at properly on the spring visit rather
than assuming the sand has handled it.

## Access, discretion and getting in at all

A fair amount of the property here is gated, managed, or behind an association,
and much of it is empty when we arrive. That is a logistical matter rather than
a complaint, but it changes how a visit has to be set up.

Access needs arranging in advance — through the owner, a property manager or
the association — and it needs arranging for everything, not just the front
door. Storage, garages, crawl access and any outbuilding are exactly where the
work is, and a visit that reaches only the living space is half a visit.

Where we work on an empty property we report in writing rather than leaving a
card behind a door nobody is going to open for four months. You get what we
found, what we did, what is still open and what we recommend, in a form you can
read from wherever you actually are.

Discretion is worth stating as well. Some of this housing is high-value, some
of it is rented out, and nobody wants a marked vehicle parked outside for an
afternoon announcing a problem. We can work around that if you tell us it
matters.

## Bed bugs, turnover and short lets

Anywhere with a high rate of people arriving and leaving with luggage has a bed
bug exposure, and a good deal of the housing on the spit is let short-term or
lent to visitors between owner stays.

Two things are worth understanding about that. The first is that bed bugs have
nothing whatever to do with cleanliness — they arrive in a bag, they are a
transport problem, and an immaculate property is exactly as exposed as any
other. Nobody should feel accused by finding them.

The second is that in an attached building they are a structural risk rather
than a unit-level one, for the same reason everything else here is: they will
move along a shared void or a service run into the next unit if the pressure in
the first one rises.

For a property that is let, the practical measures are unglamorous and they
work. Encasements on mattresses and box springs, so an early problem is visible
rather than hidden in a seam. Bed frames and headboards checked between guests
rather than only when somebody complains. Reported bites treated as a reason to
verify rather than a reason to argue.

We charge to verify bed bugs, and that fee comes off the treatment if there is
a problem to treat. That is deliberate: verification on a let property is
genuinely a job, it protects the owner from paying for a treatment they did not
need, and it protects the next guest from arriving into a problem nobody
confirmed either way.

## What we will not sell you on the spit

Two things, and they are both easy money we would rather not take.

We will not put an empty building on a year-round recurring treatment plan.
Product applied to a structure nobody enters for five months is largely spent
before anybody benefits from it. Scheduled inspection plus exclusion is the
service that actually fits a seasonal property, and it costs less.

And we will not sell a unit owner in a shared building a treatment we know will
be undone by the void behind their wall, without saying so first. If the honest
answer is that this needs to go to the association, we will say that, put it in
writing, and help make the case if it is useful.

## How a visit here works

We start outside, and on the spit we start at ground level: the foundation
line, vents and screens, the sweeps and thresholds, penetrations, and the state
of any exclusion already in place — which on this shoreline means checking what
the salt has done to it since anyone last looked.

Then storage. Garage, locker, shed, boat if it is part of the property. That is
where an unoccupied building's problems start and where they are still cheap.

Then underneath and inside, properly, with a light, and in a shared building
with attention to where your unit stops and the structure carries on.

You get a plain account: what is active, what is old, what is driving it, what
needs doing before the building is closed up, and what belongs to the
association rather than to you. Assessment is free for everything we handle
except bed bug verification, which is charged and then deducted from treatment.
There is no term agreement.
