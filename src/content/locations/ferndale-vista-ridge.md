## New houses on high ground

Vista Ridge is newer construction on elevated ground on the edge of Ferndale,
and it is close to the opposite of the older neighborhoods down by the river in
almost every respect that matters to us.

The buildings are recent, built to a modern code, with continuous foundations,
modern materials and consistent detailing. The site is higher and drains better.
The landscaping is young. And the whole development went up over a short period
to a common specification.

Every one of those changes what we look for, and most of them are advantages.
The mistake is assuming they add up to a house that nothing can get into,
because they do not.

## Tight is not the same as sealed

Modern construction is genuinely good at what it was designed for. It is
airtight to a standard older housing never approached, it manages water
intelligently, and it is insulated properly.

None of that is rodent exclusion, which is a different specification with
different materials, and it is not what anyone was checking on the way through.

The failures are consistent enough that we can predict most of them before
arriving.

**Service penetrations closed with foam.** Every house has a dozen — water,
waste, gas, electrical, data, refrigerant lines for a heat pump, the dryer vent.
On new construction they are typically sealed with expanding foam, which does
exactly what it is meant to do about air and water, and which a mouse chews
through without slowing down. This is the single most common route we find on
newer houses.

**Crawlspace vent screening stapled rather than fitted.** A screen that is
tacked over an opening rather than mechanically fixed into it comes away at a
corner within a few years, and then the vent is simply an opening.

**Garage door corners.** Standard doors meet standard slabs imperfectly, and the
bottom corners are where it shows. Stand inside with the door shut: daylight
means a gap.

**Behind siding trim and at transitions.** Where two materials meet, where a
run of siding stops at a corner board, and behind the trim around a bay or a
cantilever, there are voids that were never intended as access and function as
it.

None of that is bad building. It is a building detailed for weather rather than
for wildlife, and the fixes are small.

## The warranty question, worth asking early

On a house of this age some of what we find may not be a maintenance item at
all.

Missing or improperly fixed crawlspace screening, a garage door that does not
meet the slab, an unsealed penetration through a rim joist, a soffit vent with
no mesh behind it — depending on the age of the house and the terms, several of
those can be warranty items rather than owner responsibility.

We are not the people to interpret your warranty, and we will not pretend to
be. What we will do is write down precisely what we found and where, in a form
you can hand to a builder, before you decide who is paying to fix it. That
costs you nothing and it occasionally saves a fair amount.

## What higher ground gives you, and what it costs

Elevation and drainage are a genuine advantage and it is worth being specific
about which problems they remove.

The moisture-driven work largely goes away. Crawlspaces here are drier, so
there is much less decay, far fewer wood-boring beetles, and very little of the
softened framing that carpenter ants prefer. Moisture ants — which are a report
about wet wood rather than a pest in their own right — are rare up here and
common on the valley floor.

That is real, and it means a large part of what we do downtown is simply not
needed in Vista Ridge.

What replaces it is exposure. An elevated, open, sunlit site with wind across
it is precisely what the autumn invaders are looking for, and it is what several
of them find first.

## The autumn arrivals on a sunny wall

From late September the warm elevations of these houses collect the seasonal
crowd, and a new subdivision on a ridge gets it more than a sheltered street
does.

Multicolored Asian lady beetles work whichever wall holds the low afternoon
sun, gathering in numbers on the siding before pushing into gaps around trim,
laps and vents. Brown marmorated stink bugs do the same on the same walls.
Cluster flies collect in upstairs rooms and window channels. Western conifer
seed bugs turn up individually indoors and alarm people considerably more than
they deserve to.

A lady beetle will nip if it is picked up. That aside, nothing in the group
reproduces indoors or does the structure any harm.

That last point decides the whole approach. Because they do not reproduce
inside, the number in the house is entirely a function of how many got in.
Spraying indoors repeatedly through the winter kills what is present and does
nothing about the supply. Sealing the openings, and treating the exterior in
the narrow window before the wave arrives, addresses the supply.

Timing is the part that goes wrong. Too early and the exterior work has faded
before they arrive; too late and they are already on the wall. The date shifts by a fortnight or more from one
year to the next depending on how the first cold afternoons fall, so it is a
thing to have arranged in advance rather than to chase once the beetles are
already on the siding.

## Rodents from open ground

Ferndale's rodent pressure comes off adjacent farmland, and a development on
the edge of town sits directly in the path of it.

The pattern is the same one every property near worked ground gets, and it is
not about cold weather. Cover disappears when ground is cut or worked, over a
few days, and the population living in it has to relocate immediately. The
nearest buildings with a gap are where a good share of it goes.

On a new house, that meets the builder gaps described above. A brand-new
subdivision with foamed penetrations and stapled vent screens is not a harder
target than an old house; it simply has fewer routes, and they are more
predictable.

Because the development is uniform, this is unusually efficient to deal with.
Find the route on one house and it very likely exists on the whole street, and
a specification that closes it can be applied to all of them.

## Young landscaping is a window that closes

Right now the planting here is small, set back and open, and the foundation
line can be seen and reached the whole way round most of these houses.

That is a real advantage and it is temporary.

Every mature neighborhood in this county has the same story: planting installed
when the houses were new, allowed to grow, and now hard against the siding over
a deep bed of accumulated duff. That combination — permanent cover touching the
wall, over damp organic material — is the single most productive thing to fix
on an older lot, and it takes years of growth to create.

Keeping a genuine gap between planting and siding now, and keeping mulch off
the wall, costs nothing. Reclaiming one in fifteen years means taking out
established shrubs somebody has grown attached to.

The irrigation matters as much. New landscaping gets watered heavily to
establish, and water applied against a foundation is the start of the moisture
story that this ridge otherwise does not have.

## Heat pumps, line sets and modern mechanicals

Newer houses here carry equipment that older ones do not, and it introduces
routes and harborage that nobody thinks to check.

A heat pump line set passes through the wall, and that penetration is
frequently the largest single hole in the building envelope. It is usually
sealed with foam and covered with plastic trunking, which handles weather and
does nothing whatever about a mouse. It is one of the first places we look on a
house of this age.

The outdoor unit sits on a pad against the wall, generally with a gap
underneath, and that gap is dry, sheltered and undisturbed. Good harborage,
directly against the building.

Condensate lines and their drains are worth a look as well, because standing
water in a warm sheltered spot is exactly what small flies breed in.

None of this is difficult to deal with. It is simply newer than most of what
gets checked, and a technician working from habit on older housing can walk
past all three.

## The first five years, and what to do in each

On a new house the useful work is front-loaded, and doing it early costs a
fraction of doing it later.

In the first year, while everything is still accessible and the planting is
small, close the builder gaps: penetrations done mechanically rather than with
foam alone, vent screening fixed into the opening rather than stapled over it,
and the garage door seal checked from the inside with the door shut. That is a
morning, and it is most of the battle.

Through the first few years, keep the landscaping off the walls as it
establishes and keep the irrigation off the foundation. That window closes
permanently once the planting matures, and reclaiming it later means removing
shrubs somebody has grown attached to.

From about the fifth year, start looking at the roof line, because that is when
trees planted at handover begin to reach it. A branch on a roof bypasses
everything sealed at ground level.

## Spiders, lighting and modern siding

New houses get a reputation for spiders that surprises their owners, and the
explanation is not the house.

Exterior lighting concentrates flying insects, and where the insects are, the
spiders that eat them follow. Modern siding, trim, and the sheltered vertical
faces around entries, gables and soffits give them somewhere to build.

The autumn wave is mostly giant house spiders, and what people see is males
moving to find females — which is why they appear singly, at speed, across a
floor, in September and October, rather than as an accumulation.

Changing exterior lighting to warmer, lower, more directed fittings does more
than anything applied to a wall, because it removes the food supply rather than
the individuals.

## Wasps in the soffits

Wasps in new-construction soffits are on the Ferndale list for a reason and
this is where it shows most clearly.

Modern soffit and fascia detailing creates long continuous voids with small
openings at intervals — vents, joints, the end of a run at a gable — which is
what a queen is looking for in spring. They are twenty feet up, they are not
visible from the ground, and nobody inspects them.

The result is a nest that goes unnoticed until midsummer, by which time it is
established and above a door or a patio.

The useful move is looking in June rather than reacting in August, and on a
uniform development the vulnerable detail is in the same place on every house.

Bald-faced hornets build the large gray paper nests people photograph. European
paper wasps build small open combs under railings and deck rails and are far
less inclined to defend them. Honey bees are not our work, and if a colony has
taken up residence in a wall we will point you at somebody who removes them.

A recurring perimeter treatment on a five-year-old house whose actual problem
is a foamed penetration and a garage door gap will produce a visible result
forever and will never finish.

The honest quote on most Vista Ridge rodent calls is a morning of hardware:
proper vent units, hardware cloth mechanically fixed, penetrations closed with

It does not repeat.

## What a new house does not have, and why that matters

It is worth listing what is genuinely absent here, because it explains why a
quote for a Vista Ridge house should look nothing like a quote for one downtown.

There is no accumulated duff against the foundation, because the beds are new.
There is no forty-year-old landscape timber going soft in the ground. There is
usually no outbuilding yet. There is no post-and-pier skirt to repair, no
disused chimney, no basement in wet soil, and no century of abandoned service
penetrations from three generations of heating.

That is a genuinely short list of problems, and it is why the honest job on
most of these houses is small. A morning of hardware, a conversation about
landscaping before it matures, and a look at the roof line every few years.

Anybody quoting a new house in this development for a comprehensive ongoing
program should be asked which of the items above they are actually addressing,
and what the plan is expected to finish rather than merely to suppress. A fair
answer to that question is easy to give when there is one.
