## Established lots, and the thing that changed

The Meadows is larger-lot single-family housing that has been in the ground
long enough to have settled into itself. The houses are solid and mostly well
kept. The lots are generous. The streets are quiet.

And nearly every pest problem we deal with here is caused by something other
than the building.

What changed on these properties is the ground. Landscaping installed when the
houses were new is now forty years mature. Trees planted as saplings now reach
the roofline. Beds have been topped up with bark year after year and are now
deep and permanently damp. A deck went on at some point. A shed appeared, then a
shop. Firewood found a wall to lean against.

None of that is neglect — it is what happens to a property somebody has been
living in and improving for decades. But collectively it is a far more
significant change than anything that happened to the structure, and it is
where the work is.

## Mature planting against siding

This is the single most productive thing to address on an established lot, and
it is the one people are most reluctant about.

Shrubs put in eighteen inches from a wall as small plants are now touching it.
Beneath them, four decades of leaf fall, needle drop and topped-up bark have
built a bed that holds moisture permanently and is never turned over. Above
them, foliage covers the wall.

That combination gives a continuous sheltered corridor at ground level, running
past every opening in the building, over damp organic material, with the
foundation line completely hidden. Rodents use it as a route. Ants, beetles and
spiders live in it. Moisture sits against the siding. And nobody — homeowner or
technician — can see or reach the wall behind it.

The fix is not removal, and we would rather say that clearly because people
brace for being told to tear out a garden they have grown. What is needed is a
genuine gap: daylight and air between planting and wall, beds pulled back, mulch
kept off the siding, and enough clearance that the foundation line can be seen
and reached the whole way round.

That can be staged over a couple of seasons. It costs nothing but time and it
does more than anything we could apply.

## What is living under the deck

Most lots here gained a deck at some point in the last thirty years, and on
decks of that age the space underneath is almost never screened.

It is a nearly ideal space: dry, sheltered from weather and from view,
undisturbed, and directly against the foundation. Rats in particular find it
quickly. And because a deck usually runs along a substantial length of wall, it
covers the one stretch of the building that cannot be inspected without
dismantling something.

When a rodent problem on one of these properties does not add up — the
crawlspace looks clean, the vents are sound, the garage checks out — the deck
is where we go next, and it is very often the answer.

Screening the perimeter of a deck properly with quarter-inch hardware cloth,
taken down to or below grade rather than tacked to the fascia, closes it
permanently. It is a half day's work and it is the highest-value single repair
available on a lot with a deck.

## Outbuildings, and the order things fill up

On a larger lot there is usually a shed, a shop, a detached garage, or several,
and they change the shape of the job entirely.

Outbuildings sit closer to the cover at the back of the property and are built
and sealed to a much lower standard than the house. They fill first, reliably.
And once one is occupied, the house is not being colonized from the boundary —
it is being supplied from forty feet away by something that already has a
population, shelter and a food store.

That is why sealing a house while the outbuildings stay open does not do what
people expect. It does not remove a population; it concentrates it and moves it
closer to where it is unwelcome.

On a property with several buildings the honest unit of the job is the site. If
somebody only wants the house done we will do it, and we will say plainly what
that will achieve and what it will not, so the result in six weeks is not a
surprise.

## Trees at forty years

The planting that matters most for insects is the timber, and on lots this age
there is a lot of it that nobody thinks about as timber.

Landscape ties. Old raised beds. A retaining wall built of sleepers. The
original deck framing. Fence posts. A stump left when something came down.
Fallen limbs stacked at the back. Firewood that has been in the same place long
enough to have gone soft at the bottom.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood — they excavate galleries in it to nest, and they
take softened, damp wood far more readily than sound dry timber. Every item in
that list is exactly what they want, and every one of them is within a short
distance of a building.

The living trees are usually not the problem, and we would not recommend
removing them. What matters is deadwood, stumps, punky bases and that list of
landscape timber, plus one thing about the living trees specifically: a branch
touching the roof is a bridge that bypasses everything sealed at ground level.
Rats climb well and they will use it. Trimming contact between vegetation and
the building is worth as much as most foundation work.

## What the crawlspace tells you

On a house of this age the crawlspace is the most informative half hour
available, and most owners have not been down there in years.

What twenty or thirty years does under a floor is predictable. A vapor barrier,
if there is one, has been walked on by trades, torn, and pulled aside for
access, and rarely put back. Insulation sags out of the joists, which is both a
symptom and excellent nesting material. Vent screens corrode from the ground
side, where nobody looks. Ducting gets damaged. And drainage changes as
landscaping matures and beds are built up against the house, so ground that
drained away from the building in 1990 may not now.

None of that announces itself upstairs until it is well advanced. A moisture
problem under a floor becomes a decay problem, then a carpenter ant or
wood-boring beetle problem, and by the time it is visible from inside there is
real repair involved.

Going under and looking properly is the point of a first visit here, and it
frequently changes the recommendation from a treatment to a repair.

## The rural edge, and the seasonal arrival

Larger lots on the edge of town mean open ground is not far away, and Ferndale's
rodent pressure comes off adjacent farmland.

The pattern is the one every property near worked ground gets, and it is not
about cold. Cover disappears when ground is cut or harvested, over a few days,
and the population living in it relocates that week. The nearest structures with
a gap are where a good share of it goes — and on these lots the nearest
structures are the outbuildings.

Because it is event-driven rather than temperature-driven, it arrives suddenly
and in numbers, weeks before there is any cold to blame. A property with nothing
at all in July has a genuine problem by late August, and nothing about the house
changed.

The useful consequence is that it is predictable. The sealing work is worth
doing before the ground behind you is next worked rather than after.

## Water features, irrigation and drainage that has drifted

Established lots accumulate water as well as planting, and after thirty years
the drainage rarely does what it originally did.

Ponds and water features attract what open water attracts: midges and other
non-biting flies emerging in numbers on still evenings, frogs, and the animals
that come to drink. Most of that is a nuisance rather than a problem, and where
that is the honest answer we will give it rather than sell a treatment.

Irrigation matters more. Systems installed decades ago get extended, repaired
and rerouted, and a head that has drifted to spray a wall instead of a bed will
keep a strip of siding damp for years without anybody noticing. That is the
beginning of the moisture story that carpenter ants and wood-boring insects
depend on.

Grade is the third piece and the one most often missed. Beds get topped up
annually, and forty years of mulch raises the soil line. Ground that fell away
from the foundation when the house was built can end up level with it or
higher, which puts damp soil against siding and, in bad cases, above the top of
the foundation wall entirely. That one is worth checking with a straight edge
rather than by eye.

## Wasps on a larger lot

Yellowjackets here are a late-summer story and the dangerous ones are in the
ground.

Ground nests along boundaries, banks, rough edges and beneath shrubs get found
by machine rather than by eye — a mower, a strimmer, a tractor working a
boundary — which is how people end up in an emergency room. On a lot with rough
edges, knowing where the activity is before August is worth a great deal, and it
costs nothing to have somebody look while they are already on the property.

Aerial nests in eaves, sheds and outbuildings are more visible and easier to
deal with, though a nest in a shop doorway in August is still a problem. Bald-
faced hornets build the large gray paper nests people photograph; European
paper wasps build small open combs under railings and deck rails and are far
less inclined to defend them.

Honey bees are not our work. If a colony has established in a wall or a chimney
we will identify it and point you at somebody who removes them properly.

## Spiders, and the fall wandering

Established lots with mature planting, outbuildings and a deck produce more
spiders than a new subdivision does, and the autumn is when people notice.

Most of what turns up indoors is giant house spiders, and what people are
seeing is males moving to find females rather than an infestation. That is why
they appear singly, at speed, across a floor, in September and October.

Reducing them is largely a matter of reducing what they eat and where they get
in: the same exclusion work as everything else, plus attention to exterior
lighting, which concentrates the insects they hunt. Warmer, lower, more
directed fittings do more than anything applied to a wall.

## The autumn crowd

From late September the seasonal visitors gather on whichever elevation holds
the afternoon sun. Multicolored Asian lady beetles on the warm siding, working
into gaps around trim and vents. Brown marmorated stink bugs alongside them.
Cluster flies in upstairs window channels.

None of them bite, breed indoors or damage the building, which is what decides
the approach rather than a reason to shrug at them.

Because they do not reproduce inside, the number in the house depends entirely
on how many got in. Sealing the openings and treating the exterior in the
narrow window before the wave addresses the supply; spraying indoors through
the winter addresses what is already in the room. On a mature lot the supply is
larger, because the surrounding planting supports more of them.

## Storage, firewood and the back of the property

Larger lots accumulate. Firewood, garden materials, a trailer, timber left over
from a project, pots, and things put behind the shed with the intention of
dealing with them.

Individually none of it matters much. Collectively it is a set of sheltered,
undisturbed harborages spread across a property, and it is why a large lot can
support a population that a small one cannot.

Firewood is the one that consistently matters most, because it is nearly always
against a wall. Getting the stack off the building and off the ground, and
bringing in what will be burned rather than a week's worth to sit by the stove,
removes a route that most households have without realizing.

## What is worth paying for and what is not

The ranking on an established lot is unusually clear, and most of it is not a
treatment.

The gap between planting and siding first, because it restores the ability to
see and reach the building. Then the deck perimeter, because that is the
stretch of wall nobody can inspect. Then the outbuildings, because that is where
the population is. Then the crawlspace — vapor barrier, drainage, vent screens.
Product, where it is needed at all, comes after those.

A recurring perimeter treatment on a mature lot with shrubs against the wall,
an open deck and a full shop will produce a visible result every time and will
never finish. We would rather quote the repairs.

## The compost heap, and the one thing people get wrong about it

Nearly every established lot here has a compost heap or a green-waste pile
somewhere at the back, and it is worth a paragraph because the advice usually
given about it is wrong in both directions.

It is not a reason to stop composting, and anybody who tells you it is has an
invoice in mind. A well-managed heap that is turned, kept damp rather than wet,
and free of cooked food and meat is not a significant rodent attractant.

What does matter is a heap that has become a permanent unturned mound against a
fence or a building, with kitchen waste going in, sitting undisturbed for
years. That is shelter, warmth and food in one place, and it is usually the
warmest spot on the property in January.

The fix is management rather than removal: turn it, keep it away from
structures, and keep cooked food out of it.

## How a visit works here

We start at the boundary and work in: what the lot backs onto, how close the
cover gets, whether the ground beyond is worked, and what has accumulated at
the back of the property.

Then the outbuildings, because on a lot with any of them that is usually where
the population actually is.

Then the house at ground level — the whole perimeter, which on a mature lot
means getting into the beds: foundation line, vents and screens, sweeps,
penetrations, garage door corners, and the deck perimeter.

Then underneath, with a light, through the whole crawlspace rather than a look
from the hatch, checking the vapor barrier, the insulation and the drainage.

You get a plain account of what is active, what is historic, what is driving
it, what needs doing before the ground behind you is next cut, and which items
belong to an arborist, a drainage contractor or a builder rather than to us. On
an established lot a substantial share of the honest answer is grounds work,
and we say so even though it is not work we are paid for. Attending and quoting costs you nothing. Bed bug verification carries a fee that is returned against the treatment, and no term commitment is asked for.
