## New buildings, old ground

Cordata is the newest large piece of Bellingham, and it produces a conversation
we have more often here than anywhere else in the city: a homeowner who cannot
understand how a house built five years ago has rodents.

The confusion is reasonable. These buildings genuinely are better than the older
stock — proper sheathing, continuous housewrap, sealed sill plates, conditioned
crawlspaces in the newer phases, and none of the century of accumulated
penetrations that defines the Lettered Streets or Sunnyland.

But the ground underneath them was open field, pasture and second-growth forest
within living memory, and the animals that lived on that ground did not relocate
to another county when the survey stakes went in. They lost their cover and
their food source over a single season, and then a few hundred warm buildings
appeared with landscaped beds, irrigation, refuse and pet food.

From a rodent's point of view a new Cordata development is an upgrade, and the
first autumn after a phase completes is reliably the busiest that phase will
ever have.

## Where a tight building actually leaks

Modern construction here is sound until a trade puts a hole through it. Every
penetration is drilled by a subcontractor working at pace, and the standard
closure is expanding foam.

Foam is an air seal. It is not a barrier — a rodent opens it in an evening — and
the failure points repeat from house to house with almost no variation.

The dryer duct where it crosses the rim joist, particularly once the exterior
flap has warped or a louver has snapped. The hose bib and the irrigation supply,
both drilled generously to allow for the escutcheon. The heat pump or
refrigerant line set, which enters at an angle and leaves a crescent-shaped gap
at the top of its hole. The electrical and low-voltage entries, frequently
clustered behind a panel in one oversized opening. And the bottom seal of a
garage door on a slab that has settled a few millimeters, which is present on
nearly every property we survey.

Add to those the crawlspace or foundation vents in the vented phases, where the
factory screen is a light mesh a rat opens without much effort, and the
attic-to-garage transition, which in production framing frequently has an
unsealed top plate.

None of that is a defect. It is a set of small, cheap oversights that only
matter because they sit on the outside of a warm dry box in a county where the
rain drives everything indoors in October. Sealing them properly is a finite
job with an end point, and once it is done it stays done.

## Landscaping is the other half

Newer neighborhoods are landscaped to a standard older ones are not, and the
landscaping is doing more pest work than most residents realize.

**Irrigated beds against warm walls.** A drip line or a spray head keeping a bed
damp against the foundation, under bark mulch that never fully dries, produces
exactly the conditions odorous house ants and pavement ants want. They then
enter along the slab edge, through the sill, or via one of the penetrations
above, and appear in a kitchen. In spring this is the single most common call we
take from this neighborhood.

**Mulch and grade.** Bark piled above the siding line puts moisture against
material that was never meant to sit in it, and provides a covered approach
right to the wall.

**Young trees and shrubs planted close.** They are small at handover and nobody
thinks about them. Five years later a branch is touching a roof and a shrub is
pressed against a vent.

**Landscape timber and retained beds.** Softening timber is carpenter ant
nesting material, and on a newer lot it is frequently the only such material
available, which makes it the parent colony site by default.

The corrections are landscaping decisions rather than pest treatments: pull
mulch back off the foundation, leave a dry band at the base of the wall, adjust
heads that spray the building, and keep planting off the siding. Where a
homeowner or an HOA makes those changes the treatment holds. Where they do not,
we are back next spring, and we would rather say that than take the repeat
booking quietly.

## The open ground behind the fence

Cordata still has undeveloped parcels, greenways, retention ponds and
agricultural land at its edges, and properties backing onto any of those are on
a different footing from those in the middle of a finished phase.

The population out there is permanent. Nothing applied to your property reduces
it, and any company suggesting otherwise is selling something. What it does is
supply continuous pressure against the buildings on the boundary.

Two events concentrate it. When adjacent open ground is cut, cleared or built
on, cover disappears over a matter of days and everything living in it looks for
the nearest structure. And when the autumn rain saturates the ground and floods
burrows, the same thing happens on a larger scale.

That makes late summer the decisive window for anyone on the edge of a phase —
dry ground, dry crawlspaces, easy exterior access, and the movement still weeks
away. A house sealed in August does not have the winter problem at all.

Retention ponds and swales deserve a specific mention. They are wet, vegetated,
undisturbed and usually nobody's individual responsibility, which makes them
good habitat sitting inside a residential area. They also produce insects in
quantity on summer evenings, which is why houses near them see more spiders on
the water-facing elevation.

## Apartment complexes

A large share of Cordata is purpose-built rental housing, much of it near the
college, and multi-unit work follows different rules from a single house.

In a building with shared structure the pests are a **building-level
population**. Wall voids, plumbing chases, shared attics and common service runs
connect units, and insects use them without regard to who holds which tenancy.

Treat one apartment thoroughly and the population next door is untouched. It
moves back. The resident concludes the treatment failed and the manager
concludes the resident reintroduced it, and neither is right — the scope was
wrong.

What resolves it is a coordinated plan: establish which units are affected,
identify the likely source, treat in one pass rather than as separate call-outs,
and follow up on an interval matched to the insect rather than to the billing
cycle.

The practical advantage in Cordata is that these are mostly larger managed
buildings with one owner or one management company. That means one
decision-maker and one schedule, which makes a building-level plan far easier to
arrange than it is in a neighborhood of individually owned divided houses. When
a manager will authorize it, the result is genuinely different rather than
marginally better.

**Bed bugs** are the clearest case. There is real turnover in this rental stock,
turnover moves furniture, and furniture is how bed bugs travel. It is a
transport problem rather than a hygiene one, and any property with regular
turnover is exposed regardless of how well it is kept. Managers who accept that
and build detection into the turn between tenancies do dramatically better than
those who treat each report as an anomaly.

The turn itself is the opportunity: an empty unit can be inspected properly and
treated without coordinating around anybody, and it costs a fraction of dealing
with the same problem three months later across several units.

Bed bug verification is the only assessment we charge for, and it comes off the
treatment when you go ahead.

**German cockroaches** are the other. They arrive inside appliances, cardboard
and kitchen equipment, they live in voids rather than on surfaces, and they
breed faster than an unplanned schedule can knock back. In a block with shared
kitchens and high turnover they need the same coordinated treatment and the same
follow-up discipline.

## Refuse in a managed building

If there is one physical change that moves rodent outcomes in this neighborhood,
it is the shared refuse area.

Enclosures sized for fewer occupants than a building actually has. Gates that no
longer close against the ground. Overflow left beside a container. Cardboard from
online deliveries stacked rather than broken down and inside. Recycling that
fills with rain and organic residue.

Each of those is a food supply available around the clock, and in a dense
neighborhood the effect compounds across buildings. By October it is sustaining
a population that then looks for somewhere dry, which is the buildings the bins
belong to.

None of that is ours to fix, which is exactly why it goes in the report rather
than getting quietly serviced around. A manager who corrects capacity, closure
and collection frequency sees a difference within a season.

## Wasps and newer construction

Newer neighborhoods produce more wasp work than older ones and the reasons are
structural.

Vented ridges, continuous soffit venting and generous eave overhangs give
sheltered cavities with easy access at exactly the height a founding queen
wants. Young landscaping has not yet matured enough to compete for space or to
obstruct approach. And the building lines are long and uniform, which means a lot
of eave per property.

Yellowjackets also nest in the ground along the edges of lawns backing onto open
ground, greenways and retention areas, which describes a great many Cordata
properties. Those are the nests that cause genuine incidents, because they are
invisible until a mower or a child finds them, and the colony peaks in late
summer when everybody is outdoors.

The cheap intervention is a spring walk of the eaves and the lawn edges, when a
founding nest is trivially dealt with. By August the same nest is a different
conversation.

## Medical, office and business park

Cordata carries a substantial amount of non-residential building, and those
accounts are documented programs rather than visit schedules.

Access is the first constraint. Clinical and professional environments have
areas that cannot be entered casually, hours that cannot be disturbed, and in
some cases requirements about what may be placed and where. Those constraints
shape the program rather than obstructing it, and a provider who treats them as
an inconvenience is the wrong provider.

The useful survey starts outside: the loading area, the waste enclosure, the
landscaping against the building, the drainage, and every service penetration.
On a modern commercial building the interior findings are usually downstream of
something exterior, and adding interior devices without addressing that is an
indefinite cost.

Records matter here as much as results. Devices mapped, service history in a
readable sequence, and findings written plainly — including the landscaping,
housekeeping and maintenance items that are somebody else's contract to fix.

## The Cordata year

**Winter.** Rodent activity indoors at its height, and the season when autumn
damage becomes visible: staining, chewed ducting, insulation disturbed, or a
smell that is difficult to locate.

**Spring.** The ant call. Odorous house ants and pavement ants become active as
soon as the ground warms, and in irrigated landscaping they frequently appear
indoors before anyone has seen them outside. Wasp queens found nests, which is
the cheapest possible moment to deal with them.

**Summer.** Wasp season, spider egg hatch, and the correct window for anything
structural. In managed buildings it is also the heaviest turnover period, which
makes it the right time to inspect units between tenancies.

**Autumn.** The decisive stretch. The rain returns, open ground saturates, and
the population on the boundary moves toward buildings within a few weeks. Work
done before this prevents the winter problem; work deferred becomes remediation.

## Spiders, and the September calls

Giant house spiders bring the autumn calls here as everywhere in the city. They
are harmless, they have been established across western Washington for
generations, and the large fast ones people find indoors are wandering males
searching for mates rather than anything seeking warmth.

No brown recluse has ever been recorded in this county, and Washington has no
established population of them anywhere.

Exterior lighting matters more in a new neighborhood than most residents expect,
because the fixtures are bright, numerous and uniform along the building lines.
A light concentrates the insects spiders eat, and the spiders arrive to eat
them. Changing a fixture or its color temperature frequently achieves more than
a perimeter treatment would.

## How a visit here works

For a house we start outside and work the penetrations methodically — the vent,
the bib, the line set, the electrical entry, the garage seal — then the grade and
the landscaping, then the crawlspace or the attic depending on the phase, then
inside. On a newer building that exterior walk usually finds the entire problem,
and it is quick.

For a managed building or a commercial site we start with the manager and the
constraints, then the exterior, waste and landscaping, then the affected units
or areas.

Either way you get a plain account: what we found, what is driving it, what
needs doing now, what can wait, and which items belong to a builder, a
landscaper or a waste contractor rather than to us. On a five-year-old house
that answer is frequently six penetrations and a bark bed, and telling you that
is worth more than a service plan.

Assessment is free for every pest we handle except bed bug verification, and
nothing here requires a term agreement.

## Conditioned crawlspaces, and what changed

The newer phases here use sealed and conditioned crawlspaces rather than the
vented ones that define most of Bellingham, and it is worth explaining what that
actually buys you, because it is genuinely better and it is not a force field.

A conditioned crawl has a continuous sealed liner over the ground, insulation at
the perimeter walls rather than between the joists, no open foundation vents,
and often a small amount of conditioned air or a dehumidifier. The result is a
space that stays dry, which removes most of the moisture-driven cast that fills
our older-neighborhood pages: moisture ants, decay fungus, sowbugs, springtails
and Oriental cockroaches all lose the conditions they need.

That is a real improvement and it is the single best thing about newer
construction here.

What it does not do is exclude anything by itself. The liner is a vapor barrier,
not armor, and a rodent that gets in through a rim penetration is now inside a
warm, dry, sealed, undisturbed space with insulation on the walls. If anything,
an animal that reaches a conditioned crawl is better housed than one in a vented
one, and it is less likely to be noticed because there is no draft, no smell of
damp earth and no reason for anybody to open the hatch.

So on these phases the exterior penetrations matter more rather than less, and
the access hatch itself is worth checking — a hatch that no longer seats properly
undoes a good part of the design.

Where a liner has been disturbed by a trade working under the house, we will say
so. It is usually a cheap repair and it protects an expensive system.

## Buying or renting somewhere new

A short note for anyone moving into this neighborhood, because the useful moment
is early and almost nobody uses it.

The handover period on a new house, or the vacancy between tenancies on a
rental, is when the exterior can be walked and the penetrations sealed without
working around anybody's furniture, schedule or landscaping. It takes an hour or
two and it is the cheapest that work will ever be.

If a house is a year or two old and has never had that done, it almost certainly
has the same handful of open penetrations as its neighbors, because the same
trades built the row. That is genuinely useful to know — a problem in one house
on a street is a reasonable prompt to check your own, and we will happily say
what we found next door in general terms without naming anybody.

For a builder or a developer finishing a phase, sealing before handover is
cheaper than warranty calls in the second autumn, and it is a straightforward
addition to a punch list.
