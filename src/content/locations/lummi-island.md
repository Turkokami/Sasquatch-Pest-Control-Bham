## An island, and the six minutes that decide how we work it

Lummi Island is reached one way: the county ferry *Whatcom Chief*, a
six-minute crossing from Gooseberry Point on Lummi Nation land. That crossing
shapes the logistics of a visit more than anything about the pests. Every trip
is planned around the boat, which is why we would rather do a job properly in
one visit than split it across two.

It does not shape the price. There is no ferry surcharge and no trip minimum
for the island, and you book it the same way you would book a house in
Bellingham. The boat is ours to plan around.

The island is long and narrow — about eight and a half miles from end to end —
and the two ends are different places to work. The north end is lower and more
settled, with most of the year-round homes and the open ground around them. The
south end rises steeply under heavy timber, and the houses there sit in the
trees. The west side looks out over Legoe Bay and the reef-net fishery toward
Rosario Strait, and takes the weather first.

## Places that stand empty half the year

The single biggest thing about pest work on Lummi Island is that a lot of the
houses are not lived in year-round. The island's population roughly doubles in
summer, when second-home owners from both sides of the border arrive, and many
of those places are shut from autumn to spring.

A closed house over a wet winter is close to ideal for mice. It is dry, it is
quiet, nobody disturbs the nest, and there is usually something left in a
cupboard. The gap that let them in was often there all along; it simply went
unnoticed because nobody was home in November to hear anything.

What the owner finds in May is the result: droppings along the counters and in
the drawers, chewed packaging, nesting in the insulation or behind the stove.
None of that means the place was kept badly. It means the building sat empty
through the months when the animals were looking for somewhere to be.

The practical answer is to reverse the order. Have the house looked at in early
spring, while it is still empty, and have the entry points closed before the
next autumn rather than after it. Cleanup, trapping and exclusion on an
unoccupied house are simpler, and you arrive to a house you can use.

## Cleaning up after mice without breathing it in

Deer mice are part of the island's wildlife, and that matters for the cleanup
more than for the trapping. The
[Washington State Department of Health's hantavirus guidance](https://doh.wa.gov/you-and-your-family/illness-and-disease-z/hantavirus)
is the reason we tell every returning owner the same thing: the hazard is in
the dust, so never sweep or vacuum droppings dry.

Open the place up and let it air before you start. Wet the droppings and nesting
down, pick them up with gloves on, and bag them. If a cabin has been heavily
used by mice, the insulation or stored bedding may be the larger part of the
job, and that is worth a proper look before anybody pulls it out by hand.

## Carpenter ants under the trees

The south end in particular is timber country, and [carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) are the pest
that follows it. They do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in wood that
moisture has already softened, which is why the problem on the island tends to
be a particular corner of a building rather than the whole house.

The pattern is consistent. A deck ledger that never dries, a sill on the weather
side, a roofline under overhanging branches that holds rain against the fascia,
a crawlspace where the framing sits damp for months. Branches touching the roof
give the ants a bridge from the trees straight onto the structure.

Treating the ants without finding the damp wood is the approach that brings
them back every spring. We look for the wet section first, because it is the
part of the job that actually ends the problem, and we will tell you plainly
if it is a carpentry fix rather than a pest one.

## The long outdoor summer, and the wasps that come with it

Island summers are lived outside — decks, gardens, beach fires, meals on the
porch — and that puts people and yellowjackets in the same places at the same
time. Colonies start small in spring and are at their largest and most
defensive in late summer, which is exactly when the island is fullest.

The nests worth dealing with are the ones near where people are: in the ground
beside a path, in a wall void by the back door, under the deck. Paper wasps
building open combs under the eaves are usually less of a concern and can often
be left if they are out of the way. We will tell you which you have before
anything is treated, and a nest nowhere near anyone may not need treating at
all.

WSU's [Pestsense](https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/) library covers yellowjackets
and the rest of the household list for readers in this state.

## Moisture on the weather side

The same wet winters that bring the carpenter ants shape the crawlspaces. A
building on the exposed west side takes wind-driven rain, and older island
houses often have vented crawlspaces over bare ground, with little to stop the
damp rising into the framing.

Moisture is the thread that ties most of the island's problems together. It
softens the wood carpenter ants look for, it draws in the moisture-loving
insects that follow damp framing, and a wet crawlspace is a far more hospitable
place for rodents than a dry one. A vapor barrier, working vents and a clear
drainage path around the foundation do more to prevent pest work here than any
treatment.

## Outbuildings, boathouses and sheds

Many island properties have more than one building: a garage, a shop, a guest
cabin, a boathouse or a shed near the water. Those structures are usually
sealed to a lower standard than the house, sit closer to cover, and are visited
less often, so they are where a population builds up first.

Working on the house alone tends to move the problem rather than end it. On
properties like this we look at the whole site, and we will say which buildings
matter and which are fine to leave.

## How a Lummi Island visit works

You call or text the same number as anyone on the mainland, and we book a day
that suits you. We plan the crossing around the ferry, bring what the job is
likely to need so nothing waits for a second trip, and walk the whole property
before quoting. The quote is in writing before any work starts.

If you are off the island and the house is empty, say so when you call. Early
spring, before you come back for the season, is often the best time to have us
out.
