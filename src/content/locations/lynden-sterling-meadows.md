## New housing on the south edge

Sterling Meadows is newer subdivision housing on the south side of Lynden,
built over a short period to a consistent specification, on ground that was
agricultural before it was residential.

Two facts drive everything here and they pull against each other.

The buildings are modern: continuous foundations, current materials, proper
detailing, and a short list of failure points that are the same on every house.
That is genuinely good, and it means the honest job on most of these properties
is small.

The surroundings are not modern at all. Open ground is close on the south side,
it is worked on an agricultural calendar, and the animals living in it behave
exactly as they have always behaved.

## Tight is not sealed

New construction is excellent at what it was designed for. It is airtight to a
standard older housing never approached, it manages water intelligently, and it
is insulated properly.

None of that is rodent exclusion. That is a different specification, with
different materials, and it was not what anybody was checking on the way
through.

The failures are consistent enough that we can predict most of them before
arriving.

**Service penetrations closed with foam.** Every house has a dozen or more —
water, waste, gas, electrical, data, the heat pump line set, the dryer vent —
and on new construction they are typically sealed with expanding foam. That is
exactly right for air and water, and a mouse chews through it without slowing
down. This is the single most common route we find on newer houses anywhere.

**Crawlspace vent screening stapled rather than fitted.** A screen tacked over
an opening instead of mechanically fixed into it lifts at a corner within a few
years. After that the vent is simply an opening.

**Garage door corners.** Stand inside with the door closed and look at the
bottom corners. Daylight means a gap at floor level, in a space that connects
directly to the house.

**Behind siding trim and at transitions.** Where two materials meet, at corner
boards, and behind the trim around a bay or a cantilever, there are voids that
were never intended as access and work as it.

None of that is bad building. It is a building detailed for weather rather than
for wildlife, and every one of those fixes is small and permanent.

## The warranty question, worth asking early

On housing of this age some of what we find may not be a maintenance item at
all.

Missing or improperly fixed crawlspace screening, a garage door that does not
meet the slab, an unsealed penetration through a rim joist, a soffit vent with
no mesh behind it — depending on the age of the house and the terms, several of
those can be warranty items rather than owner responsibility.

We are not the people to interpret your warranty and we will not pretend to be.
What we will do is write down precisely what we found and where, in a form you
can hand to a builder, before anybody decides who is paying to fix it. That
costs you nothing and it occasionally saves a fair amount.

## One era, one set of details, one advantage

Almost everything about a single-period development is a disadvantage for
character and a considerable advantage for diagnosis.

When a development goes up over a few years to a consistent specification,
every building has the same details, materials and shortcuts. So when a problem
on one house traces to a particular vent type, a particular garage door seal,
the way the dryer vent was run, or a gap where the siding meets the foundation,
the odds are very high that the identical detail exists on every house in the
street.

That turns a search into a check. On a first visit we will find the route on
your house and then look specifically for it everywhere it should also exist,
which is faster and more thorough than starting from scratch.

It also means a well-chosen repair specification can be applied across a whole
street. Where neighbors want to do it together, the cost per household drops
sharply and the result is better, because it removes the source rather than
moving it next door.

## Ground that was farmed, and ground that still is

Land does not stop being what it was because it has houses on it now.

The immediate effect is historical: ground that was cleared and graded for
development displaced whatever was living in it, and the nearest structures at
the time were the new houses. That is why so many people in newer subdivisions
get mice within months of moving in, and why it feels like a verdict on the
building when it is nothing of the kind.

The ongoing effect is agricultural, and it is the one that matters now.
Farmland close to the south edge is worked on its own schedule. When it is cut
or harvested, several hundred acres of cover becomes bare ground within a week,
and every animal living in it has to be somewhere else immediately.

That arrives suddenly and in numbers, usually weeks before there is any cold
weather to blame it on. A property with nothing at all in July has a genuine
problem by late August, and the house has not changed.

Because it is event-driven, it is predictable, and the work to close a building
is worth doing before the ground goes rather than after.

## Drainage on former farm ground

Development on agricultural land inherits the drainage that was there, and it
is worth a look on a property of this age.

Agricultural ground here was tiled and ditched over generations, and a
subdivision built across it sits on top of whatever remains of that. Combined
with new stormwater facilities, the result is usually fine and occasionally
produces a wet corner nobody expected.

The reason it matters to us is that a persistently damp crawlspace, or ground
that no longer falls away from a foundation, is the beginning of the moisture
story that carpenter ants, wood-boring beetles and decay depend on. On a house
this new that should not be happening yet, and where we find it we will say so
plainly, because it is a building issue rather than a pest one and it is much
cheaper to address early.

## Landscaping: the window that is open now

Right now the planting here is young, set back and open, and on most of these
houses the foundation line can be seen and reached the whole way round.

That is a real advantage and it is temporary.

Every mature neighborhood in this county tells the same story: planting
installed when the houses were new, allowed to grow, and now hard against the
siding over a deep bed of accumulated material. That combination — permanent
cover touching the wall, over damp organic matter — is the single most
productive thing to fix on an older lot, and it takes years of growth to
create.

Keeping a genuine gap between planting and siding now costs nothing. Reclaiming
one in fifteen years means removing established shrubs somebody has grown
attached to.

Irrigation matters as much. New landscaping gets watered heavily to establish,
and water applied against a foundation starts the moisture problem this ground
otherwise would not have.

## Heat pumps, line sets and modern mechanicals

Houses here carry equipment older housing does not, and it introduces routes
and harborage nobody thinks to check.

The heat pump line set passes through the wall, and that penetration is
frequently the largest single hole in the building envelope — usually foamed
and covered with plastic trunking, which handles weather and does nothing about
a mouse.

The outdoor unit sits on a pad against the wall, generally with a gap
underneath. Dry, sheltered, undisturbed, directly against the building.

Condensate lines and their drains hold standing water in a warm sheltered spot,
which is what small flies breed in.

None of this is difficult. It is simply newer than most of what gets checked,
and somebody working from habit on older housing can walk past all three.

## The first autumn, and every one after

From late September the sunny elevations collect the seasonal crowd, and on the
edge of open ground there is nothing to intercept them.

Cluster flies are Lynden's particular version. They breed outdoors in soil,
have nothing to do with hygiene or waste, gather on warm walls, work into gaps,
and overwinter in wall voids and attics — then appear indoors on mild winter
days in numbers that seem impossible relative to any visible opening.
Multicolored Asian lady beetles and brown marmorated stink bugs do the same on
the same walls.

The lady beetle can deliver a small bite; the stink bug cannot. Neither raises
young indoors or harms the structure. Since
they cannot reproduce inside, whatever ends up in the house got there by
walking or flying in, and the count reflects nothing except how many openings
were available — so the answer is sealing, plus exterior work timed to the
short window ahead of the wave. Repeated interior spraying deals with what is
already in the room and nothing else.

Timing is what goes wrong. Too early and the exterior work has faded; too late
and they are already on the wall. Since it is the temperature rather
than the calendar that starts it, the useful week moves by a fortnight or so
each year, and a household that waits until it sees them has already missed
it.

## Wasps in the soffits

Modern soffit and fascia detailing creates long continuous voids with small
openings at intervals — vents, joints, the end of a run at a gable — which is
precisely what a queen is looking for in spring. They are twenty feet up,
invisible from the ground, and nobody inspects them.

The result is a nest that goes unnoticed until midsummer, by which time it is
established and frequently above a door or a patio.

Because every house shares the detail, a good nesting year produces nests along
a whole street rather than at one address. That is normal and it is worth
knowing before somebody concludes their house is unusual.

The useful move is looking in June rather than reacting in August.

## What is worth doing, in order

Close the builder gaps first: penetrations done mechanically rather than with
foam alone, vent screening fixed into the opening, and the garage door seal.
That is a morning and it is most of the battle.

Keep the landscaping off the walls and the irrigation off the foundation while
that is still easy.

Get the autumn exterior work timed properly for one season and then judge
whether it is needed every year.

And from about the fifth year, start looking at the roof line, because that is
when trees planted at handover begin to reach it, and a branch on a roof
bypasses everything sealed at ground level.

## Fence lines, and what collects along them

On lots this size the boundary between two properties is one of the more
productive things to look at, and almost nobody does.

A fence line collects from both sides at once. Leaf litter builds against it.
Shrubs get planted beside it and grow into it. Bins, firewood, garden
furniture, bikes, offcuts and cuttings get stored there because it is out of
the way.

The result is a continuous strip of sheltered, undisturbed material running the
whole depth of the lot, joining whatever is behind the development to the
buildings at the front. It functions as a route and a harborage in the same
feature, and it exists on most lots in a development of this age once the first
few years have passed.

Clearing a foot either side, and keeping stored material up off the ground, is
free, takes an afternoon, and does more than most of what could be applied.
Where two neighbors do it at once, the strip stops being continuous, which is
the part that actually matters.

## When the street is worth doing together

Because every house here shares a specification, this is one of the few places
where doing several at once is genuinely better rather than merely cheaper.

A route that exists on your house exists on your neighbors', which means a
population is not working one building — it is working a row, and closing one
building pushes it along rather than removing it. Where three or four adjoining
households have the same work done in the same week, the source goes rather
than moving.

It is also the only situation in which a repair specification can be written
once and reused, which is what makes it cheaper per household.

If neighbors have mentioned the same problem, say so when you call.
