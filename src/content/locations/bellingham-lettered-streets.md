## The oldest ground in the city

The Lettered Streets are where Bellingham's residential history starts. This is
the oldest continuously occupied housing in the city, laid out on a tight grid
north of downtown, on small lots with alleys running behind, under mature street
trees, with a great deal of the stock predating 1930.

It is a genuinely lovely neighborhood and it is, from our side of the work, the
most consistently challenging housing in Bellingham. Not because anything was
built badly, but because it was built to a standard that had never heard of air
sealing, and because a hundred and twenty years is a long time for a building to
accumulate openings.

Nearly every job we do here comes back to the same three features.

## Post-and-pier over bare earth

Most of these houses sit on posts and piers with a short crawlspace beneath, and
in a large share of them the floor of that crawlspace is still soil.

That single condition drives more of our work here than anything else. Bare
earth puts ground moisture continuously into the space and into the joists
above it, in a climate that supplies about thirty-five inches of rain a year
across a long gray season. The framing above never fully dries.

Everything moisture-driven follows. Moisture ants colonize wood that has
softened. Decay fungus runs where it stays wet, and decay rather than insects is
what actually costs structural capacity here. Sowbugs, millipedes, springtails,
silverfish and Oriental cockroaches live in the damp and occasionally come up
into the house through a gap around a pipe.

The correction is not a treatment. It is a sound vapor barrier over clean
ground, drainage that takes roof water away from the foundation rather than into
it, and grade that slopes away from the building. Where a crawl has been damp
long enough that insulation is hanging loose, that material comes out before
anything else — wet fiberglass holds moisture, accelerates decay above it and
makes excellent nesting material.

We will say plainly when the person you need next is a drainage contractor. On
this housing stock that happens often, it costs us the recurring service, and it
is the correct answer.

## Framing that runs floor to roof

The second feature is the one that produces the most baffled phone calls.

These houses were built with balloon or near-balloon framing, where the stud
cavity runs from the sill plate all the way to the top plate with no firestop in
between. A mouse that gets under the house at grade can be in the attic without
ever crossing an occupied room.

So the classic Lettered Streets call is somebody hearing scratching in an
upstairs ceiling and being unable to work out how anything got up there. The
answer is nearly always that it never came in upstairs at all — it came in at the
crawlspace and used the wall.

That is why treating where the noise is accomplishes very little here, and why
the work has to start underneath. Find the entry, close it properly, and the
route stops being available. Trap in the attic without doing that and you are
removing individuals from a population that still has a door.

## A century of penetrations

The third is simple accumulation. Gas, water, waste, electrical, three
generations of telephone and cable, an abandoned oil line, a chimney thimble
from a stove nobody remembers. Every one drilled generously by whoever was doing
that job on that day, and essentially none of them sealed afterwards.

Add to that board sheathing that has shrunk over a century and left gaps, vent
screens whose original light mesh rusted through decades ago, skirting that no
longer meets the ground, and crawl hatches that no longer seat.

None of this is a defect in the houses. It is what a hundred and twenty years
looks like. But it means a proper exclusion job here is genuinely a job — not
half a dozen gaps but two or three dozen, and finding them is most of the value.

## The alleys, and why the block matters

This is the feature that makes the Lettered Streets different from anywhere else
in the city, and it is worth being honest about.

Alley-served neighborhoods concentrate waste. A block where every can sits out
on the same alley on the same night supports a rodent population that belongs to
the block rather than to any one address. Add mature landscaping, garages and
outbuildings of various ages, compost, fruit trees and the occasional backyard
flock, and the alley becomes a continuous corridor of food and cover running the
length of the block.

So there is a limit to what sealing one house achieves. That house stops having
an interior problem, which is genuinely worth doing and is what most people
actually want. But the number of animals in the alley does not change.

We say this at the first visit rather than after the third. Where several
neighbors will coordinate — bins that close, feed and fruit picked up, a couple
of sealed garages — the difference is substantial and quick. Where they will not,
we can still protect your building, and you should know that is what you are
buying.

## Rats, and the Bellingham autumn

Norway rats are the most common single reason anyone in this neighborhood calls
us, and the local pattern is not the one national material describes.

Through spring and summer they live outdoors and do well. Himalayan blackberry
along the alleys and vacant corners, decking with a gap underneath, woodpiles,
compost, fruit trees, bird feeders and the creek corridors give them everything
they need, and most residents never see one.

Then the rain sets in. Somewhere between late September and early November the
ground saturates, burrows flood, and that entire outdoor population needs
somewhere dry within a few weeks. Your crawlspace is dry. Your garage is dry.
The space above your porch ceiling is dry.

There is no mystery in it. It is a housing crisis with a predictable date, and
it is why the single most valuable thing we do in this neighborhood is exclusion
work in August. A house sealed before the rain never has the interior problem. A
house sealed in December has an interior problem plus a sealed-in animal, and
the order of operations gets considerably more complicated.

Backyard chickens deserve a mention because they are common here. We do not tell
people to get rid of them. We do tell them that spilled feed and an open feed
bin are what turn an ordinary yard into a rat-supporting one, and that a coop
needs building as though it is trying to keep rats out, because it is.

## Carpenter and moisture ants

Both are common in this stock and the distinction changes everything.

Moisture ants are the honest ones. They colonize wood that is already wet and
already decaying, so finding them is useful information rather than merely a
problem. On these houses the source is usually a gutter that has been
overflowing down a wall for years, a downspout discharging at the foundation, a
porch roof that has leaked quietly, siding in contact with soil, or a sill plate
sitting too close to grade.

Carpenter ants prefer wood that has begun to soften and keep a parent nest
outdoors — a stump, a fence post, a landscape timber, a woodpile — with satellite
nests inside the structure. The satellites are what people see. Treating those
while the parent sits untouched in the back garden is why this work gets a
reputation for returning.

In spring, winged carpenter ants get reported as termites regularly. The
simplest tell is the waist: sharply pinched on an ant, uniform on a termite.
Getting that right first costs nothing and changes the entire approach.

## Bed bugs and shared walls

The Lettered Streets carry a substantial rental stock, including a lot of older
houses divided into units, and that changes how bed bug work has to be scoped.

In a building with shared structure the population is a building-level
population. Wall voids, plumbing chases and the framing cavities described above
connect units, and insects move through them without regard to who pays which
lease. Treating one unit while the ones either side go untreated relocates the
problem, usually next door and often back again a month later. Tenants
reasonably conclude the treatment failed; it did not fail, it was scoped wrong.

What works is a building plan — map the affected units, identify the source,
treat in a coordinated pass, and follow up on an interval that matches the life
cycle rather than the billing cycle. Landlords who resist that generally pay for
the same problem three times.

Bed bugs are the one service where our assessment carries a fee, credited
against treatment if you proceed. Everything else costs nothing to have us look
at.

## Spiders, and the reassuring version

Every September the calls start about large fast spiders indoors, and in
century-old housing with this much crawlspace and this much mature planting the
volume is high.

They are giant house spiders. Harmless, long established throughout western
Washington, and the large ones people find are wandering males looking for mates
rather than an invasion responding to cold. The ones stranded in a bathtub are
males that got in and could not climb out.

Brown recluse has never been recorded in this part of Washington, and the species is not established anywhere in the state.
Anyone telling you otherwise while holding a quote is selling fear, and on a
house of this age there is plenty of genuine work to discuss without inventing
any.

What reduces the population is unglamorous: clearing exterior webbing, dealing
with harborage under porches and in crawl vents, moving firewood off the wall,
correcting exterior lighting that concentrates the insects they feed on, and
sealing the door sweeps and thresholds most of them use.

## Garages, outbuildings and the back of the lot

The buildings behind the house matter more here than in most neighborhoods, and
they are routinely left out of scope by whoever quoted before us.

A hundred years of back-lot construction sits along these alleys: original
carriage sheds, 1940s single garages, later additions, workshops, and storage
built at various times to various standards. Many have earth floors or slabs
that have cracked and settled, siding that no longer meets the ground, and doors
that have not closed properly in decades.

From a rodent's point of view that is ideal — dry, undisturbed, close to the
alley food supply, and connected to the rest of the block. A population living
in a garage is a population with a permanent base twenty feet from the house,
and sealing the house alone leaves it entirely intact.

So a proper scope here includes the outbuildings, even where the complaint is
about the kitchen. Sometimes the honest answer is that a garage is beyond
sealing and the sensible move is to remove what is attracting them and defend
the house properly. We will say which it is rather than quietly quoting for
both.

Stored items are the other half. Cardboard, old furniture, bagged pet food,
birdseed and garden supplies in an unsealed outbuilding provide harborage and
food together. Hard containers and a clear floor change the picture more than
anything we can apply.

## Renovation, and what opening a wall reveals

A lot of these houses are being restored, and renovation is both the best and
the worst moment for pest work.

Best, because a wall that is open is the only time anybody sees what a century
did to the framing. Old carpenter ant galleries, moisture damage around a window
that failed decades ago, a run of soft sill plate, a rodent nest in a cavity
nobody could reach — all of it becomes visible at once, and the correction is
never cheaper than while the finish is already off.

Worst, because a building under renovation is wide open. Doors propped, siding
off, temporary covers, materials stacked outside, and nobody living there to
notice anything. We see houses acquire problems during a remodel that they did
not have before it started, particularly over an autumn.

The useful sequence, if you are planning work: have somebody look before the
walls close, seal the envelope as part of the project rather than after it, and
be deliberate about the crawlspace while access is easy. Sealing penetrations is
trivial during a remodel and awkward forever afterwards.

Old damage is worth a specific note. Galleries in a joist and old exit holes are
evidence of history, not of current activity, and damage persists for decades
after the insects are gone. We would far rather establish which you are looking
at than treat something that finished twenty years ago, and on a restoration we
will tell you when the finding is structural rather than ours.

## Heating, chimneys and the winter list

Older houses here have been heated several different ways over their lives and
each has left something behind.

Disused chimneys and flues are a recurring entry point — an uncapped stack, a
thimble left open when a stove was removed, a fireplace that has not been used
in thirty years. Anything that can climb has a route in, and anything that gets
in frequently cannot get out again.

Retrofit insulation is the other winter factor. Blown-in material added to a
cavity that was never designed for it is warm, undisturbed and ideal nesting
material, and rodents tunnel through it rather than merely crossing it. Attic
insulation that has been contaminated is both a hygiene issue and an ongoing
attractant, and where that is the situation the sequence matters: exclude first,
remove and replace second. Doing it the other way installs new material into a
building that is still open.



We start outside and walk the whole perimeter, including the alley side, which
is the elevation most people never look at and where a good share of the
findings are. Grade and drainage, the siding-to-foundation transition, vents,
penetrations, skirting, vegetation contact, the state of any previous exclusion
attempt, and what the garage and outbuildings are doing.

Then underneath, properly, with a light and on our back if the clearance
requires it. On these houses that is where the answer is and it is the step most
commonly skipped by whoever came before us. A report written from the crawl
hatch is not worth much.

Then the attic, then the interior.

You get a plain account of what we found, what is causing it, what needs doing
now, what can wait, and which items belong to a contractor, a roofer or a
drainage specialist rather than to us. On a century-old house a real share of
the answer is not work we get paid for, and we say it anyway.

Inspections are free for every pest we handle here, bed bugs excepted, and there

If you are not sure whether what you are hearing is an animal or simply a
