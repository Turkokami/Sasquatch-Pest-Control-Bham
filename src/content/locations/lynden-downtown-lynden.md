## A small core in the middle of farm country

Downtown Lynden is a compact commercial street with turn-of-the-century and
mid-century houses on the blocks immediately behind it, and the whole of it
sits surrounded by dairy and berry ground.

That last point is not scenery. It is the single biggest difference between
pest control here and pest control in a downtown of similar size somewhere
else, and it accounts for the calls that people find hardest to understand.

The other two features are more familiar. The commercial buildings are attached
and continuous behind their finishes. The houses behind them are genuinely old,
with everything that implies about how many ways there are into them.

## Flies that were not produced in your building

The most common complaint in a Lynden food premises in July is flies, and the
most common assumption about them is wrong.

House flies and stable flies develop in moist organic material — manure packs,
soiled bedding, silage spoilage, feed waste, wet margins around waterers. There
is a great deal of that within a short flight of the town, and adults travel
considerably further than most people assume from where they were bred.

So a premises can be run immaculately, with the bins right and the doors right
and the cleaning right, and still receive flies in numbers, because the supply
is not on the property and never was.

That changes what a sensible response looks like. Interior spraying kills what
is in the room now and does nothing about the next arrivals, so it becomes a
weekly cost with no end. What actually works is keeping them out and giving
them nothing: door discipline and air curtains where they are justified, fly
screening on openings that stay open, light traps positioned away from the
door rather than beside it, and waste handled so the premises does not become a
local breeding site in its own right.

The last of those is the part any business can control, and it is the part
worth spending on.

## Drain flies and fruit flies, which are your building

Small flies are a different problem entirely from the ones arriving off the
fields, and they are usually very fixable.

Drain flies breed in the organic film on the inside of a drain line, under a
floor waste, or in the trap beneath equipment that is rarely moved. Fruit flies
breed in anything fermenting: a mop bucket, the tray under a dispenser, the
underside of a soda gun, a crate of produce.

In both cases the adults are the visible end of something living somewhere
specific and small, which is good news — find it, clean it properly, and they
stop within days and stay stopped.

Finding it is the actual work. Moving equipment rather than looking around it,
checking floor wastes and the joint where equipment meets the floor, and taping
a simple overnight trap over a suspect drain to confirm rather than guess. A
premises that gets sprayed weekly for small flies has usually never had that
half hour spent on it.

## Attached buildings, and why one unit is not the job

The commercial row behaves as one building whatever the addresses say.

Wall cavities run past party walls where they were never properly stopped.
Chases pass vertically between floors. Suspended ceilings create a connected
plenum across a whole floor plate. Roof voids run the length of a terrace. In
older commercial stock, services were cut in as needed across decades and
closed up rarely.

A population living in that connected volume works a territory that contains
several units and moves between them freely. Which is why a treatment in one
unit produces two good weeks and then stops: it cleared what was inside, and
the source was never inside.

The version that finishes is done at building or block level, with the owner or
the neighbors involved, addressing the shared voids and the exterior of the
whole structure. That is a harder conversation than a single service call and
it is the one worth having, and we will put what we found in writing so it can
be taken to the people who need it.

## Where a business sits above or below a home

Mixed use is normal on a street like this, and the dynamic is worth naming
plainly because it usually gets discussed as a dispute when it should be
discussed as plumbing.

A commercial kitchen is a concentration of food, warmth, moisture and shelter
operating daily. That is not a criticism; it is what a kitchen is. What matters
to whoever lives above is that the routes connecting the two are almost always
open — the risers, the extract runs, the penetrations cut for gas, water, waste
and power, and the gaps around them that nobody closed.

Sealing that path protects everybody in the building at once and is cheaper
than any of the alternatives. Either party can start it.

## Cockroaches, and why they persist downtown and nowhere else

German cockroaches are largely a shared-building problem in this county, and a
compact commercial core is where the shared buildings are.

They arrive on goods, deliveries, packaging and secondhand equipment. They
establish in warm harborage close to food and water. And in an attached
structure they disperse through the same routes as everything else, so a single
untreated unit keeps a whole row supplied.

Two things decide the outcome. The treatment is baiting and harborage
reduction rather than spraying surfaces — the strains in circulation respond to
bait and are frequently driven deeper into voids by residual sprays, which
looks like progress and is not. And it has to be done across the affected part
of the building at the same time rather than one unit at a time.

That second point requires somebody with authority over the building to say
yes, and it is usually what decides whether a block clears in a month or
struggles for two years.

## The houses behind the street

The residential blocks immediately behind Front Street hold the older stock,
and it behaves like old housing everywhere.

A century of services has been cut into these buildings. Knob and tube, then
rewiring. Wood or coal, then oil, then gas or electric. Galvanized, then
copper. Waste lines replaced in sections. Phone, then cable, then data. A dryer
vent through a wall in the seventies. Rooms added, porches enclosed, kitchens
moved.

Every one of those was made by somebody focused on making the new thing work,
and most were never closed behind. The result is a building with many small
routes rather than one obvious one, and a survey that finds three of them has
not finished.

The crawlspace is where this gets settled. On older stock the under-floor space
tells you what the last thirty years actually did: whether there is a vapor
barrier and what state it is in, whether the perimeter is sound, whether the
framing near the ground has gone soft, and whether the vent screens have
corroded from the side nobody looks at.

## Cluster flies, lady beetles and the autumn wall

From late September the sunny elevations collect the seasonal crowd, and in
open country around a small town they arrive in numbers.

Cluster flies are the local specialty and they cause the most confusion. They
breed outdoors in soil, parasitizing earthworms, and they have nothing whatever
to do with hygiene, food or waste. In autumn they gather on warm walls, work
into gaps around trim and vents, and overwinter in wall voids and attics —
appearing indoors on mild winter days in numbers that seem impossible relative
to any opening anybody can find.

Multicolored Asian lady beetles and brown marmorated stink bugs do the same
thing on the same walls at the same time.

Nothing in that group bites anybody, raises a generation indoors, or harms the
fabric of a building, and it is that fact rather than any wish to make light of
them that settles how they are dealt with. Because they do not
reproduce inside, the number in the building depends entirely on how many got
in. Sealing the entry points and treating the exterior in the narrow window
before the wave addresses the supply; spraying rooms through the winter deals
with what is already in them and nothing else.

## Rodents, and the ways in that get used

The routes downtown are ordinary and consistent.

At ground level on the commercial side: worn door sweeps, the gap under a
roller shutter, unsealed service penetrations, a broken air brick, a light well
with no cover, and a rear yard with waste stored against the wall. On the older
houses: damaged skirting, a crawl hatch that is a board leaning on an opening,
corroded vent screens, and the space under a porch or deck that was never
screened.

And the one people forget on older stock, which is the drainage. Disused or
broken drain runs under and beside old buildings are a genuine route into a
structure, and a rat problem that resists everything sensible at ground level
is occasionally coming up rather than in.

Waste in a shared rear yard is the other half. Bins that do not close, stored
cardboard, and food waste held between collections will support a population
that no treatment out-runs. Where several businesses share a yard it has to be
agreed rather than assumed.

## Deliveries, storage and what arrives in the boxes

Retail and food premises get a family of problems that houses do not, and they
walk in through the delivery door.

Indianmeal moths, sawtoothed grain beetles and weevils live in dry goods —
flour, grain, pulses, nuts, dried fruit, spices, pet food, birdseed. They arrive
inside sealed packaging, because the infestation started upstream, and emerge
on a shelf weeks later.

The detail worth knowing is that webbing inside intact packaging means it
arrived that way. That single observation decides whether the conversation is
with a supplier or with the premises, and getting it the wrong way round wastes
a lot of effort.

Stock rotation, an inspection gap around and behind storage, dealing with
spillage in the awkward places under fixtures, and pheromone monitoring so a
problem is caught below the level anybody would notice by eye — that is the
whole program.

## Birds on the storefronts

A retail street with parapets, signage and sheltered ledges attracts nesting,
and the pest consequence is usually not the birds.

It is what accumulates: nesting material in gutters and downspouts, droppings
on frontages and walkways, and the insects that live in an occupied nest and
move inward when it is abandoned. Bird mites and carpet beetles both do this,
and both get diagnosed as something else because nobody connects them to a nest
above a shop sign.

Much of what nests here is protected, and the timing and method matter. Where
there is an active nest we will say what may lawfully be done and when, and
where the answer is that it waits until the nest is finished, we will say that
too.

## Wasps above a public street

Downtown wasp nests are mostly aerial and mostly high — soffits, parapets,
signage voids, ventilation grilles and roof edges, in places nobody visits
between one year and the next.

They matter more here than on a residential street because a nest above a
pavement or a shop doorway is above people who never chose to be near it, and a
shop entrance with a nest in the fascia in August is a hazard and a trading
problem at once.

Access is the constraint. Some of these need equipment, and some need doing at
a time when there is nobody underneath.

Honey bees are separate and we do not treat them. In berry and dairy country
pollinators are worth more than we are, and a colony in a wall or an old
chimney needs somebody who removes them properly.

## Vacant units, and the gaps between tenants

An empty commercial unit in an attached row is a liability for everybody in the
building, and it is nobody's priority until it becomes a problem.

Nothing is being cleaned. Nobody notices droppings, a smell or activity behind a
fixture. Waste from the fit-out or the previous tenant frequently sits.
Utilities may be off, so there is no heating cycle and no air movement. And the
unit is connected to its neighbors through exactly the same shared voids as
every occupied one.

What that produces is a quiet, undisturbed reservoir in the middle of a row,
supplying the units either side, with nobody in a position to report it.

The practical answer is that a vacant unit should be checked periodically by
whoever holds it rather than left until a new tenant opens the door. It is a
short visit and it is far cheaper than the alternative, which is a fit-out that
turns into a treatment program for three businesses.

If you are next door to one and having a problem you cannot explain, that is
worth saying when you call.

## What we will not do here

We will not treat one unit in an attached row, call it solved, and book you in
again in six weeks. If the source is in the shared structure we will say so on
the first visit and put it in writing.

We will not spray a premises weekly for flies that are being produced on farm
ground a mile away. That is charging for the weather.

And we will not sell a business a program for what is genuinely a waste,
storage or delivery problem. Where the answer is a bin lid, a cleared alley or
a routine for breaking down cardboard, that is the answer and it does not
involve us.

## Storage above and behind the shops

The upper floors and back rooms of an older commercial building are frequently
used for storage rather than trade, and they get looked at rarely.

Stock, fixtures, seasonal display, paperwork and packaging sitting undisturbed
in a low-traffic space is exactly the material that supports both rodents and
stored-product insects, and being out of the customer's sight means a problem
develops there for months before anything appears downstairs.

Keeping an inspection gap around the walls and getting stored material up off
the floor makes that space checkable, which is most of the battle.

We start outside and at the back — the service side, the yard, the bin store,

Then the interior, and in a food premises the harborage rather than the

Then the vertical connections — risers, chases, ceiling plenums, roof void —
