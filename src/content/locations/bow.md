## Open ground, and what it means

Bow is farm country. Open agricultural land through the Bow and Edison flats,
running west to Samish Bay and rising into wooded ground along the Chuckanut
edge, with scattered farmhouses, outbuildings and a small amount of village
commercial.

That geography produces a very particular kind of pest work, and it is almost
the opposite of what we do in a city. In Bellingham the problem is usually a
building with a specific defect. Out here the building may be perfectly sound
and still be under constant pressure, because it is standing in the middle of
habitat.

The single most useful thing to understand if you live here: the population
outside your fence is permanent. It is not going to be reduced, and nothing
applied to your property will reduce it. What is achievable — and it is genuinely
achievable — is making your buildings unattractive and properly sealed, which is
a finite piece of work with an end point.

## The harvest effect

This is the local mechanism that catches people out, and it has nothing to do
with temperature.

Open ground supports a large resident rodent population all season: voles, deer
mice, house mice and Norway rats living in field margins, ditch banks, hedgerows
and the crop itself. Nobody sees them and nobody has a problem.

Then a field is cut. Cover that existed in the morning is bare dirt by evening,
and the entire population living in it needs somewhere else within hours. What
is available is the nearest structure — your house, your shop, your barn.

Then, a few weeks later, the autumn rain arrives, the low ground saturates,
burrows flood, and it happens again with everything that was still outdoors.

So the rodent calendar here runs on cutting and rainfall rather than on cold,
and the decisive weeks are September through November. Work done before that
prevents the winter problem entirely. Work deferred to spring is remediation of
a winter's worth of damage.

Which is why we push exclusion in August out here harder than anywhere else. Dry
ground, dry crawlspaces, easy exterior access, and the movement still weeks
away.

## Cluster flies, the classic Bow phone call

If you live surrounded by pasture and your windows fill with slow, clumsy, dark
flies on the first mild afternoon of the year, you have cluster flies, and they
are the most misunderstood pest in this kind of country.

They do not breed in your house. They have nothing to do with your rubbish, your
drains or your housekeeping. The larvae are parasites of earthworms in the
surrounding fields, which is exactly why the more pasture and cropland around a
building, the worse it gets. A farmhouse in the middle of open ground gets them
badly and it is not a reflection on the house.

The seasonal pattern is simple. In late summer and early autumn the adults look
for somewhere to overwinter and choose the warmest available surface, which is a
south or west-facing wall in afternoon sun. They enter through gaps in the
siding, around window frames, under fascia, through unscreened gable and soffit
vents, and settle into the wall void, the attic or the space behind trim to go
dormant. On a warm day they wake, head for the light, and emerge on the interior
side.

By February, killing the ones in the room accomplishes very little. There are
thousands more in the wall and they will keep emerging on every sunny day until
spring.

The work that matters happens in late August and September, on the outside:
sealing the gaps, screening the vents, and treating the sunny elevations before
they move in. Do that and the following February is quiet. Stink bugs and lady
beetles follow the same pattern and respond to the same work.

## Farmhouses and their additions

Much of the housing here is old, and nearly all of it has been added to.

That matters more than the age itself. Every addition created a junction between
old framing and new — at the roofline, at the rim joist, where a new floor meets
an old one, around whatever services were rerouted at the time — and those
junctions are rarely sealed as well as either section is on its own. On a
farmhouse that has grown three times over a century, there are a lot of them.

Underneath, the usual regional pattern: shallow crawlspaces over bare earth,
vapor barriers that are absent or have been pushed aside, foundation vents whose
screens failed decades ago, and framing that has taken water somewhere and now
supports moisture ants.

The porch, the mudroom and the utility addition are worth specific attention.
They are commonly the least well built parts of a rural house, frequently on
piers or a shallow slab, often with the crawl access inside them, and they are
where we find entry points more often than anywhere else.

## Outbuildings, honestly

Every property here has them and the honest position is worth stating.

A pole barn with a gravel floor and a roll-up door was never going to be
rodent-proof, and quoting as though it could be sealed is how somebody ends up
disappointed by a program that could never have delivered.

The realistic hierarchy is: close the buildings that can genuinely be closed,
protect what matters inside the ones that cannot, and monitor rather than
pretend.

**Feed is the pivot.** Grain, pellets, seed and pet food in open storage will
support a population indefinitely, and nothing we apply overcomes an unlimited
food supply. Metal bins with tight lids, spillage cleaned rather than swept into
a corner, pallets kept off the wall so runways stay visible, and a door that
closes against a proper threshold are worth more than any product. On properties
where that happens, everything else we do works.

**Wiring is the priority that persuades.** Damage to shop electrics, control
panels and the harness on machinery parked for the season is expensive and
occasionally dangerous. A warm engine bay full of insulation is exactly what a
rat wants in November, and the bill for a rewired harness is the number that
convinces a skeptical owner a program is worth having.

**Placement is constrained.** Anything used has to sit where livestock, poultry,
barn cats and working dogs cannot reach it, which in practice means
tamper-resistant stations anchored on the exterior and mechanical control
inside. Where we use rodent control products at all we choose programs and
active ingredients selected to limit secondary poisoning risk — this is raptor
country, and the owls and hawks working these fields are doing free work we
would rather not undermine.

## Stored-product insects

Common on farm property and routinely misdiagnosed as a building problem.

Beetles and moths in grain, seed, flour, pet food and animal feed arrive inside
the product rather than through a door. Treating a building perimeter for an
insect that came in on a pallet accomplishes nothing, and money gets spent that
way regularly.

The work is tracing back to the source: starting with whatever has sat longest
and furthest back, checking spillage in seams, under racking and in the corners
of a feed room. It is usually a single forgotten sack or an old spill that has
been quietly producing adults for months. After that it is rotation, sealed
containers, cleaning the residue out, and monitoring long enough to be sure.

## The bay side and the exposure

Properties closer to Samish Bay pick up a set of coastal factors that inland
Bow does not.

Salt and wind work on the building envelope continuously. Vent screens corrode.
Fasteners fail. Sealants degrade. Trim and flashing loosen. Every one of those
is an entry point, and out there they open considerably faster than they do a
few miles inland.

That drives our material choices rather than our method. Ordinary galvanized
hardware cloth is not adequate in that exposure — it corrodes, and an exclusion
that fails in three winters was money wasted. Stainless mesh, appropriate
fasteners and sealants specified for coastal use cost more at the outset and are
the only version of the job worth doing. We would rather quote the durable
specification and explain why than win the work with a cheaper one.

Wind-driven rain is the other factor. It goes into assemblies designed for rain
that falls downward, which is how a wall on a weather elevation ends up
persistently damp and supporting moisture ants in framing that looks fine from
inside.

## The Chuckanut edge

The western and northern edge of the area rises into wooded ground, and property
up there behaves like forest edge rather than farmland.

Carpenter ants in softened cedar, with the parent colony out in a stump, a dead
limb or a woodpile rather than in the house. Rodents using continuous cover to
reach the buildings. Heavier spider and centipede pressure, driven by abundant
insect production in damp wooded ground.

The fix is at the interface: trim the branch bridging to the roof, screen the
vents properly, close the gap where a deck meets the house, and move the
firewood off the wall. Firewood deserves the same mention it gets everywhere
rural — stacked against a building it is harborage, it holds moisture, and it is
frequently the carpenter ant parent colony we have been looking for.

## The small commercial side

There is a modest amount of food and hospitality business through the area, and
seasonal trade around it.

Seasonal operations deserve a program that scales with the season rather than an
identical monthly visit year-round, which overserves the quiet months and
underserves the busy ones. Waste handling is where most of the available
progress sits, as it usually is with food service — a bin that cannot be closed
or cardboard staged outside will support a rodent population that no service
schedule overcomes.

Buildings in open country also get the full autumn movement described above,
which for a commercial kitchen means the exclusion work needs doing before
September rather than after the first sighting.

## What we will not sell you

**Wildlife of any kind.** Birds, bats, squirrels, raccoons and everything else
with fur or feathers are outside our scope. We do insects and rodents, and on a
farm property we will tell you plainly which you have and who handles the rest.

**Mole trapping.** Constant request on open ground and not something we do. The
methods that actually work are restrictively regulated in Washington, and most
of what is sold to homeowners is theatre.

**A rodent-free farm.** Nobody can deliver one. What we quote is control over
where the population is allowed to be and protection for the things that cost
money when they are damaged.

**A recurring program on top of an open feed supply.** If the grain is in an
open sack in an open shed, we are managing a symptom forever and we will say so
at the first visit rather than the fourth.

## Fly pressure where there is livestock

Where there are animals there are flies, and on a working property it is a
production issue rather than a comfort one.

House flies breed in moist decaying organic material — manure packs, spilled
silage, wet bedding, feed fermenting at the edge of a bunk — and move between
waste and feed. Stable flies bite, taking blood from the legs of cattle, and a
heavy population measurably reduces intake because animals spend the day
stamping and bunching instead of eating.

Both develop fast. In warm weather the cycle from egg to adult runs a week to
ten days, which means a breeding site missed on Monday is producing adults
before the weekend. That short cycle is why fly programs succeed or fail on the
sites rather than on the adults, and why a program built on knockdown alone
quietly stops working every summer.

The sites are usually a small and predictable number: the wet margin of a manure
pack, the ground under a leaking waterer, feed accumulating at the end of a
bunk, bedding that has not been turned, the drainage low point that never dries.
Finding and correcting those does more than any spray schedule, and on the
operations where we get real results it is because somebody agreed to change a
management practice rather than because we applied something new.

Where treatment has a place it is as support — targeted work on resting surfaces
rather than blanket application, baiting placed away from feed and water, and
attention to resistance, because rotating nothing is how a program stops working
after two seasons.

## Spiders and the domestic list

The household side out here is the standard regional set, with rural emphasis.

Giant house spiders bring the autumn calls, and the answer is reassuring: they
are harmless, long established in western Washington, and the large fast ones
are wandering males rather than an infestation. There is no brown recluse in
this corner of the state. Exterior lighting on a rural house concentrates the
insects they feed on, so a fixture change frequently does more than a treatment.

Silverfish, sowbugs and millipedes belong to the damp crawl and are reporting
moisture. Wasps peak from late July, and on open ground a yellowjacket nest in a
bank or under equipment is a genuine safety problem rather than a nuisance,
because it is invisible until something disturbs it.

## Ants, and reading them correctly

Two ants matter on farm property and the distinction changes everything.

Moisture ants colonize wood that is already wet and already decaying. They
cannot establish in sound dry lumber, so finding them is genuinely useful
information rather than merely a problem: the water is the disease and the ants
are the symptom. On a farmhouse the source is usually a gutter that has been
overflowing down a wall for years, a downspout discharging at the foundation,
siding in contact with soil, or a porch addition with no flashing worth the
name. Treat the ants without correcting that and a new colony arrives in the
same timber within a season or two.

Carpenter ants prefer wood that has begun to soften and keep a parent nest
outdoors — a stump, a fence post, a landscape timber, a woodpile. The satellites
inside the building are what people see, and treating those while the parent
sits untouched is why this work gets a reputation for returning.

In spring, winged carpenter ants get mistaken for termite swarmers constantly.
The quickest tell is the waist: an ant is sharply pinched through the middle and a termite is not. After that, look at the antennae — elbowed on an ant, straight and beaded on a termite. Correct identification is the cheapest thing we do on a visit and it
changes the entire approach.

## How we work in Bow

Bow sits on scheduled route days rather than same-day availability, though being
on the corridor makes it straightforward for us to reach.

On a working property we start with your operation — where we may drive, what
may be placed and where, and what your calendar looks like — because those shape
everything after them. On a house we start outside with grade and drainage, the
vents and penetrations, the additions and their junctions, the firewood, and
what the surrounding ground is doing.

Then underneath, properly and with a light, which on a rural house is nearly
always where the answer is. Then the attic and the interior.

You get a plain account: what we found, what is causing it, what needs doing
now, what can wait, and which items are a repair or a housekeeping change rather
than a pest treatment. If the single biggest available improvement is a lid on a
feed bin or a downspout moved three feet, that is what we lead with, before
anything we would be paid for.

Inspections are free for every pest we handle, with bed bugs the one exception,
and there is no long-term contract.
