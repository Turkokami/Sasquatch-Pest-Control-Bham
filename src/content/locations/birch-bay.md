## A summer place that became a town

Birch Bay was built as somewhere to spend July. Much of the housing went up in
the sixties and seventies as cabins, beach places and weekend property, on small
lots, close together, near the water and built to a standard appropriate to
three warm months of occupancy.

Then people moved in permanently. Cabins were insulated, extended, re-roofed,
converted and lived in through the winter, and a substantial year-round
population now occupies a housing stock that was not originally designed for it.

That gap between what a building was built for and what it is now asked to do
explains most of our work here, and it is a genuinely different situation from
Blaine a few miles up the road — that is a working town with a harbor and a
border crossing, this is a recreation community with a rental economy.

## What the original construction did

The seasonal cabin stock shares a set of characteristics, and every one of them
is a pest consideration once the building is occupied year-round.

**The underfloor space.** Very often skirting, piers or a shallow crawl rather
than a proper sealed foundation. It was never intended to be part of a
conditioned building, so it has bare earth, no vapor barrier worth the name, and
access points nobody thought twice about. It is the single easiest place on most
Birch Bay properties for something to get in.

**Light framing and thin envelopes.** Small dimension lumber, minimal sheathing,
and insulation retrofitted decades later into cavities that were not designed
for it. Retrofit insulation is warm, and warm is exactly what a rodent is
looking for in October.

**Additions.** Most of these places have been added to at least once. Every
addition created a junction between old framing and new, and those junctions are
where the gaps are — at the roofline, at the rim, where a new floor meets an old
one, and around whatever services were rerouted at the time.

**Decks and skirting.** Nearly universal, frequently enclosed at the base, and
the enclosed space underneath is dry, sheltered and undisturbed. It is prime
harborage, and on a lot of properties it connects directly to the underfloor
space.

**Plumbing and services run underneath.** Water lines, waste lines and heat
ducts running through or under an unsealed floor cavity, each with a penetration
around it. Where a rodent gets into that space it has access to the whole
building's underside.

## The rental economy

Birch Bay has a large short-term rental market and it changes the pest picture
in a way that catches owners out.

**Turnover is the transport mechanism.** A house that changes occupants weekly
sees more people, more luggage and more soft goods through it in a season than a
family home sees in a decade. That is the mechanism by which bed bugs travel,
and it is why rentals carry a risk profile that owner-occupied property simply
does not.

**Guests do not report things.** A visitor who sees a mouse or gets a bite
frequently says nothing to the owner, leaves a review, and goes home. So the
first the owner knows about a problem is a rating drop or a cluster of
complaints arriving at once, by which point the situation has been running for
weeks. Occupied family homes catch problems in week one. Rentals catch them in
week six.

**The season compresses everything.** The window when the property is earning is
the same window when wasps peak and when the building is most heavily occupied,
and it is the window in which a treatment is most disruptive. Which means the
useful work happens in the shoulder seasons, and owners who plan for that have a
far easier summer than owners who do not.

Our practical advice to rental owners here is consistent. Deal with wasps in
spring, before the nests are established and before the bookings start. Get
exclusion done in the autumn after the season ends, when the crawl is dry and
the rodent movement is beginning. Use mattress and box spring encasements as
standard, because they make bed bugs both harder to establish and far easier to
detect. Brief whoever cleans between bookings on what to look for, because they
are on site more often than anyone else. And do not try to compress a treatment
that needs a follow-up interval into a single changeover day.

## Bed bugs specifically

It is worth being direct about this because rental owners are understandably
sensitive to the subject.

A bed bug problem in a rental is not a hygiene failure and it is not a
reflection on housekeeping. It is a transport problem, and any property with
regular guest turnover is exposed regardless of how well it is kept. The
operators who handle it best are the ones who accept that and build detection
into their routine, rather than the ones who assume it cannot happen to them.

Early detection is everything. A problem caught in one room while it is still
one room is a contained job. The same problem three months later, after guests
have moved between bedrooms and soft furnishings, is a whole-property treatment
at several times the cost and with far more disruption to the calendar.

Bed bugs are the one service where our assessment is not free. There is a
verification fee, and it is credited against the treatment if you go ahead. We
charge it because verification is slow, careful work and because free bed bug
callouts attract speculative visits that push out real appointments. Everything
else we handle here costs nothing to have us look at.

## Wasps, and why vacant property gets the worst of it

The wasp pattern in Birch Bay is driven by the same occupancy gap as everything
else.

A queen founds a nest in a sheltered spot in April or May — an eave, a soffit, a
deck rail, the underside of a bench, a gap in the skirting. At an occupied house
somebody notices it in June while it is still small and trivially dealt with. At
a property standing empty between the winter and the season, nobody notices
anything, and the colony grows without interruption until somebody opens the
place up in July and finds it at full size beside the front door.

The colony is also at its most defensive late in the season, exactly when the
property is busiest with guests and children. Which is why the single most
cost-effective thing a Birch Bay owner can do is have somebody walk the eaves
and the deck in spring.

Yellowjackets additionally nest in the ground along lawn edges, in the base of
skirting and under decking, and those are the ones that produce genuine
incidents, because they are invisible until something disturbs them.

## The bay, the berm and the exposure

The shallow bay and its tidal flats shape the shoreline properties more than
people expect.

Tidal flats produce insects in enormous quantity, and that production feeds
everything above it. Shoreline houses get a heavier flying-insect load on warm
evenings than anywhere inland, and consequently a heavier spider population,
because the spiders follow the food. Exterior lighting matters a great deal
here: a bright white fixture on a summer night is doing more to create a spider
problem than anything else on the property, and changing the fixture is more
effective than treating for the spiders.

Salt and wind work on the building envelope continuously. Vent screens corrode.
Fasteners fail. Sealants degrade. Trim and flashing loosen. Every one of those
is an entry point, and out here they open faster than they do a few miles
inland. Any exclusion work worth paying for on this shoreline has to be
specified for the exposure — stainless mesh and appropriate fasteners rather
than ordinary galvanized, which will not survive.

The berm and the shoreline drive concentrate housing tightly along the water,
which means properties are close together and a problem rarely belongs to one
building. Rodents in particular treat a run of closely spaced shoreline houses
as a single opportunity.

## Septic, drains and the damp-building list

A good deal of the community is on septic rather than sewer, and the older
systems here are exactly as old as the cabins they serve.

Drain flies and fungus gnats around a failing system, a slow drain or a wet spot
in a yard are a symptom worth reading rather than a pest to be sprayed. Where we
find them the useful conversation is about the drainage and the system, not
about an application.

The same logic runs through the whole damp-building list that this climate and
this housing stock produce together: sowbugs, millipedes, springtails,
silverfish and Oriental cockroaches in an underfloor space that stays wet, and
moisture ants in any framing that has been damp long enough to soften. Every one
of those is telling you about water. Treating them without addressing the water
is a temporary result on a permanent condition, and on a lightly built cabin
over bare earth the water is usually not hard to find.

## The Birch Bay year

The rental calendar and the pest calendar are the same calendar, which is what
makes planning possible here.

**Late winter.** The quiet stretch, and the right time for anything disruptive.
Overwintering insects appear at sunny windows on mild afternoons. Rodent
activity indoors is at its peak in properties that were closed up in the autumn,
and nobody is there to hear it.

**Spring.** The most valuable eight weeks of the year for a rental owner. Wasp
queens are founding nests and are trivially dealt with now. Ants become active. Anything
done in this period costs a fraction of what the same problem costs in July.

**Early summer.** Occupancy climbs and the opportunity for disruptive work
closes. Wasp nests are established and growing. This is when problems that were
ignored in spring start generating guest complaints.

**High summer.** Peak occupancy, peak wasp activity, peak spider load on the
shoreline, and the worst possible time to be doing anything invasive. Work here
is reactive and has to fit into changeover gaps.

**Autumn.** The season ends and the second valuable window opens. Crawlspaces
are still dry and the rodent movement is just beginning. Exclusion done now prevents the whole winter problem. Deferred to
spring, it becomes remediation of a winter's worth of damage instead.

## Rodents, and the specific Birch Bay version

Rodents are the largest single category of work here, and the pattern is set by
the housing rather than by the animals.

Norway rats and house mice both do well in this community for the same reason:
the buildings offer an unusually easy way in. Where a house in town presents a
continuous foundation with a handful of sealed penetrations, a converted cabin
presents skirting with a gap at one corner, an underfloor space with bare earth,
a deck enclosure connecting to it, and services running through the floor
cavity. That is not one entry point to find, it is a dozen.

The seasonal driver is the same as the rest of the county — the autumn rain
floods outdoor burrows and everything looks for somewhere dry — but the
consequence is sharper here, because the underfloor space of a lightly built
house is warmer, drier and less disturbed than almost anything else available.

Once inside that cavity, the damage runs to insulation, ducting and wiring, and
the smell that eventually appears in a bedroom is usually something that died in
a space nobody can easily reach. Getting it right means going under, assessing
the whole underside rather than the one spot where droppings were seen, and
closing the envelope properly with material that will last in this exposure.

Where a property is on piers or skirting, the honest answer is sometimes that
the underfloor space needs enclosing properly as a piece of construction before
any pest work will hold. We would rather say that than take payment for repeated
trapping against an open building.

## Ants, and what they are reporting

Ant calls here divide cleanly into two kinds and the distinction decides the
whole approach.

Odorous house ants and pavement ants come in from outside, usually along the
slab edge, the sill, or a service penetration, drawn by whatever is available in
a kitchen. They are a nuisance rather than a structural signal, and they respond
to a combination of exterior treatment and closing the specific route they are
using.

Moisture ants are different and more useful. They colonize wood that is already
wet and already decaying, which on this housing stock is a common condition —
lightly built cabins over bare earth, additions with imperfect junctions,
skirting that traps damp against a rim joist. Finding them means finding a water
problem, and treating them without correcting it produces a new colony in the
same timber.

Carpenter ants take the middle ground, preferring softened wood and keeping a
parent colony outdoors in a stump, a woodpile or a section of deck framing that
stays wet. The satellites indoors are what people notice.

On a rental in season, the practical answer is often to deal with the immediate
problem quickly and schedule the structural correction for the shoulder season,
and we will say which is which rather than pretending one visit resolves both.

## Spiders and the autumn calls

Every September the phone starts about large fast spiders indoors, and on this
shoreline the load is heavier than inland for a specific reason: the tidal flats
produce insects in quantity, and the spiders follow the food.

They are giant house spiders — harmless, long established here, and the ones
found in a bathtub are males that got in and could not climb out. What actually
reduces the population is unglamorous: knocking down exterior webbing, dealing
with harborage under decks and in skirting, correcting the exterior lighting
that concentrates their prey, and sealing the door sweeps and thresholds most of
them use to get in.

For a rental owner this matters commercially as well, because a guest who finds
spiders in a bathroom writes about it.
