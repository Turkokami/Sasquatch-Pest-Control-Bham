## An industrial neighborhood in a residential city

Irongate is Bellingham's light industrial quarter, and almost nothing that
applies to the rest of the city applies here.

There are no crawlspaces to speak of, very little of the century-old timber
framing that defines the older neighborhoods, and hardly any residents. What
there is instead: metal and tilt-up buildings on concrete slabs, roll-up doors
by the dozen, yards full of stored material, contractor and trade premises,
fabrication and auto shops, storage facilities, distribution, and long stretches
of every week when the entire area is empty.

That produces a genuinely different discipline. Industrial pest control runs on
realistic goals for buildings that cannot be closed, on documentation, and on a
willingness to tell a client that the reason the program is not working has
nothing to do with the program.

## The building cannot be sealed, and that is the starting point

The single most useful thing we bring to an Irongate account is refusing to
pretend otherwise.

A shop with two or three roll-up doors open through the working day, a personnel
door propped in warm weather, a slab that has settled slightly at one corner and
a dock leveler with a gap beneath it is not a sealable envelope. It never was.
Quoting as though it could be is how a client ends up disappointed by a program
that was never capable of the result.

What is achievable, and it is genuinely worth having:

**A defended perimeter.** Exterior placements positioned against how the
building is actually approached rather than spaced decoratively around it.

**Controlled interior placement** where the sensitive equipment, stock and
wiring are, rather than uniform coverage of a large empty floor.

**Attention to the specific openings that matter**: door seals and sweeps that
have worn through, the gap under a settled roll-up, thresholds, and the
penetrations where compressed air, power and dust extraction leave the building.

**Honest findings about the yard**, which is where the population actually
lives.

That is a program with a shape and a rationale rather than a subscription, and
it holds up when somebody asks what they are paying for.

## The yard is the problem

Most interior rodent activity in this neighborhood is the visible end of an
exterior population, and the exterior is almost always more habitable than the
building.

**Stacked material.** Lumber, pipe, steel, fencing, formwork, offcuts and
pallets stored on the ground provide dry, sheltered voids in enormous quantity.
A stack of pallets is a purpose-built rodent apartment block, and every contractor
yard has one.

**Equipment and plant standing outdoors.** Machinery, trailers, skips, mixers
and anything that has not moved in a month is harborage, and frequently
harborage with wiring in it.

**Vegetated margins.** The strip along a fence line, the ditch at the back of a
lot, the undeveloped parcel next door and the untended ground between two
premises. It is nobody's explicit responsibility, it runs continuous cover the
length of the block, and it delivers animals directly to your doors.

**Waste and salvage.** Skips without lids, scrap accumulations, cardboard baled
outdoors and awaiting collection, and the informal pile that every yard has in
one corner.

None of that is treatable in the way a building is. What changes it is storage
practice: material up off the ground on racks where possible, pallets stacked
and rotated rather than left to settle, a managed gap between stored material
and the building wall, and vegetation cut back off the fence line.

We put those findings in writing rather than quietly servicing around them. A
client who racks their material sees a difference in a season. A client who does
not is paying us to manage a symptom indefinitely, and they deserve to know
which of those they have chosen.

## Nobody is here at night

This is the factor that most distinguishes Irongate from a retail or hospitality
setting, and it cuts both ways.

Industrial premises are frequently unoccupied from late afternoon Friday until
Monday morning, and many are quiet every evening. That is a great deal of
undisturbed time — exactly the condition rodents prefer — and it means the early
signals nobody is present to notice go unnoticed for weeks rather than a day.

A restaurant finds out it has a problem quickly because twenty people work in it
every evening. A fabrication shop finds out when something fails, or when a
technician happens to look behind a rack.

Two practical consequences follow. Monitoring matters more here than in a
staffed environment, because the devices are doing the noticing that people do
elsewhere. And the service record matters more, because it is the only
continuous account anybody has of what has been happening in the building.

It also means our scheduling should suit the site rather than the route. On many
Irongate accounts the useful visit is early, before machinery is running and
before a yard fills with vehicles and deliveries.

## Vehicles and fleet

The most expensive damage we deal with in this neighborhood is almost never in a
building.

A vehicle parked for weeks is a warm, dry, undisturbed enclosure containing
insulation, upholstery and a wiring harness. For a rodent looking for somewhere
to nest in November that is close to ideal, and the resulting damage is
expensive, occasionally dangerous, and completely invisible until something
stops working.

Fleet vehicles, seasonal plant, trailers, work trucks over a shutdown, and
anything stored on gravel beside a vegetated margin are all exposed. So is the
machinery that comes out of storage each spring.

Where a client has vehicle or plant storage, it belongs in the scope rather than
being treated as somebody else's problem. Practical measures are unglamorous and
they work: park away from stored material and fence lines where you can, keep the
ground around the parking clear, avoid leaving food or wrappers in cabs, and
have somebody check stored vehicles periodically over winter rather than
discovering it in April.

## Break rooms, and the food nobody counts

A recurring conversation here starts with a client saying there is no food on
site, so a rodent problem makes no sense.

Rodents need harborage and water as much as they need food, and industrial sites
supply both generously — floor drains, wash bays, condensate, leaking taps and
outdoor standing water. But there is nearly always food too, and it is in
predictable places.

The break room and its bin. The vending area and whatever has fallen behind it.
Desk drawers in the office. A microwave nobody cleans. Crumbs under a mess-room
bench. Pet food if somebody keeps a yard dog. Birdseed or grain if the business
handles it at all.

None of that is a criticism of anybody's housekeeping; it is simply where the
food is on a site that does not think of itself as having any. It takes very
little to sustain a population that is otherwise living out in the yard, and it
is usually the easiest thing on the whole findings list to fix.

## Stored product and materials

Where a business handles feed, seed, grain, packaged food, pet products or dry
goods of any kind, there is a second category worth separating out.

Beetles and moths that infest stored product arrive **inside the product**, on a
pallet, rather than walking in through a door. Treating the building perimeter
for them achieves nothing and a good deal of money gets spent that way.

The work is tracing back to the source lot: starting with whatever has sat
longest and furthest back, checking spillage in racking seams, under pallets and
in the corners of a storage area, and being systematic. It is usually one
damaged bag or an old spill quietly producing adults for months. After that it
is rotation, sealed containers where practical, cleaning residue out of seams,
and monitoring long enough to be sure it is finished.

For businesses storing packaging, textiles, upholstery materials or anything with
natural fiber, dermestid beetles are worth knowing about as well. They scavenge
protein and fiber, and an accumulation of dead insects in a light fitting or a
neglected corner will sustain a population that then damages stock.

## Self-storage and long-term storage

There is a good deal of storage capacity in this neighborhood and it has its own
character.

Units are sealed, dark, undisturbed for months at a time, and full of exactly
the materials that make good nesting: cardboard, textiles, paper, upholstered
furniture, and frequently food that somebody did not think about when they
packed.

Corridors and roller doors provide travel routes between units, and a problem in
one unit is not contained by a partition that stops short of the deck.

For an operator the useful approach is perimeter and corridor focused, with
attention to the loading areas and the exterior, plus a tenancy policy about
food storage that is actually enforced. For a customer, the practical advice is
simple: nothing edible, nothing in cardboard where a hard container would do,
and check anything upholstered before it goes in.

## Roofs, mezzanines and the spaces above

Industrial buildings put a great deal of service equipment on the roof and a
great deal of storage on mezzanines, and both are under-inspected.

Roof penetrations for extraction, compressed air, power and refrigeration are
sealed on a much shorter cycle than the roof membrane itself. Ponding water
where drainage is imperfect accelerates that. Once something is through the roof
plane it has the ceiling space, and on a large building that connects everything.

Mezzanines and racking tops collect dust, dropped material and long-forgotten
stock, and they are rarely walked. On more than one Irongate site the finding
that explained months of activity was on a mezzanine nobody had been up to in a
year.

Both belong in a proper survey, and both are commonly skipped by whoever quoted
before us.

## Documentation

For most accounts here the record is a real part of the value, and it should be
built to be read by somebody other than the person who ordered the service.

Devices mapped and numbered so a replacement technician, an insurer or an
auditor can follow the logic. Service records in a sequence that shows trend
rather than just attendance. Findings written plainly, including the
housekeeping, storage and maintenance items that we could quietly work around
instead.

That last part is the difference between a program and a subscription. If the
driver is a fence line nobody cuts or a stack of pallets against a wall, the
report should say so even though saying so reduces what we can sell.

## What we will not sell you

**A sealed industrial building.** Repeating it because it is the promise most
often made in this neighborhood and it cannot be delivered.

**More devices instead of a yard fix.** Where the population lives in stored
material, adding interior stations is an indefinite cost with no end point.

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Birds on an industrial roof and wildlife in a
yard are outside what we do. We handle insects and rodents and we will point you
to somebody for the rest.

**Perimeter treatment for stored-product insects.** They arrived in the product.
Treating the outside of the building for them is money spent on the wrong
problem, and it is one of the most common misdiagnoses in this kind of work.

**A quarterly program on a site with an unmanaged skip.** We will quote the
program and name the skip, and if only one of those gets actioned it should be
the skip.

## Wasps, and why they matter more on an industrial site

Stinging insects are a safety issue in this neighborhood in a way they are not
at a house, and the reasons are specific to how these premises are used.

**Yellowjackets nest in the ground** along fence lines, in banked or disturbed
ground, under stored material, in the void beneath a pallet stack and inside
equipment that has not moved for a few weeks. Every one of those is somewhere a
forklift, a mower, a delivery driver or somebody moving stock is going to
encounter without warning, and the colony is at its largest and most defensive in
late summer — exactly when yard activity peaks.

**They also take wall and soffit cavities** on metal buildings, entering through
a gap in cladding, a failed vent screen or the junction where a roof meets a
wall. On a large building that nest can be twenty feet up and completely
invisible until somebody is working at height near it.

**Paper wasps** favor the underside of canopies, inside open equipment,
electrical cabinets and unused vehicle cabs. A nest inside a cab or a control
panel is a genuine hazard for whoever opens it next.

The practical answer is the same one that works everywhere and is almost never
done: a walk of the eaves, the yard edges and the standing equipment in late
spring, when a founding nest is the size of a golf ball. That is a cheap
half-hour. The same nest in August is a scheduling problem, a stop-work
conversation and occasionally an incident report.

For a site with employees, contractors and delivery traffic, it is also worth
knowing where the nests are even when they are not going to be treated, so that
people can be told.

## Water, drains and the wet corners

Industrial sites supply water more generously than anyone realizes, and it is
half of what sustains a population that has no obvious food source.

Wash bays and their drainage. Floor drains and trench drains, and the organic
film that lines them. Compressor and refrigeration condensate. Coolant and
process water. A leaking outside tap nobody has reported. Roof drainage
discharging onto ground that never dries. Standing water in a low corner of a
yard, in an unused sump, or in equipment left uncovered through a wet season.

Drain flies breed in the film inside drains rather than in the room, so spraying
achieves nothing and mechanical cleaning ends it within a fortnight. Where a
site has a persistent small-fly issue, the drains are the first place we look
and very often the last place anybody has cleaned.

Standing water in a yard is also a straightforward rodent asset. It is not
usually expensive to correct, and correcting it does more than another quarterly
visit would.

## Neighboring sites, and the shared perimeter

One thing worth saying plainly to anyone in a business park.

Your fence line is somebody else's fence line. The undeveloped parcel next door,
the yard behind you, the ditch running between two premises and the margin along
the access road all support the population that reaches your building, and none
of it is under your control.

That does not make your program pointless — a defended building in a poor
neighborhood still gets a materially better result than an undefended one. But
it does mean expectations should be set against reality, and it means that where
two or three adjacent operators will each manage their own margins, everybody's
outcome improves at once.

Where we service several premises in one park we will say, without naming
anybody, when the pressure on one site is plainly coming from an adjacent one.
That is more useful than letting a client conclude their program has failed.
