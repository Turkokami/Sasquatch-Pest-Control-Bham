## A small town with a large front door

Sumas has a very small population and an international land border crossing, and
that combination produces a pest profile out of all proportion to its size.

The commercial and logistics footprint here — truck crossing, brokerage,
staging, warehousing, fuel and the businesses that serve cross-border traffic —
means a meaningful share of our work in this town is commercial rather than
residential. Around it, in every direction, is farm ground. And underneath it all
is a town that has been through severe flooding and rebuilt.

Those three things account for nearly everything we get called about here.

## Freight, warehousing and the commercial side

Goods arrive from somewhere else, and whatever traveled with them arrives too.
That is the fundamental fact of pest work anywhere freight concentrates, and it
is why a warehouse in Sumas has an exposure that a house a mile away does not.

**Warehousing and staging.** High ceilings, large openings, and dock doors that
are open for much of the working day out of operational necessity. Sealing such
a building completely is not achievable and quoting as though it were would be
dishonest. The realistic approach is a defended perimeter, controlled interior
placements, and attention to the specific points where product and pallets are
handled.

**Yards and open ground.** Staging yards, trailer parking and the vegetated
margins around them support rodent populations that then work into the adjacent
structures. A great deal of what determines a warehouse's interior pressure is
happening outside its walls, in ground nobody thinks of as part of the building.

**Pallets and product.** Wooden pallets stored outdoors and brought in are a
classic introduction route, for rodents and for stored-product insects alike.
Where a client can stage pallets away from the wall and rotate stock properly,
the difference is measurable.

**Documentation.** Commercial and cross-border accounts frequently answer to
somebody other than the owner — an auditor, a customer, an insurer. So the
program is built to be read: devices mapped and numbered, service records that
follow a logical sequence, and findings written plainly, including the ones
about housekeeping and sanitation that we could quietly service around instead.
We put those in the report because a client who fixes a dock housekeeping
problem gets a better result than one who buys more visits.

**Food handling.** The businesses serving a crossing run at irregular hours with
heavy turnover, and the waste handling reflects that. As with food service
anywhere, most of the genuinely available improvement is in the bins, the
enclosure and the timing rather than in anything applied.

## Flood recovery

Sumas took severe flood damage in November 2021, and a large share of the
town's buildings have been through inundation and rebuilding since. That history
is still the most important thing about many of these structures from our point
of view.

The sequence on a flood-affected building is not negotiable. **Wet insulation
comes out** — it does not recover, it holds moisture, it accelerates decay in
the framing above it, and it is ideal nesting material. **The space gets dried**
properly, with attention to the framing and not just the surface. **The vapor
barrier is re-established** over clean ground. Only then does exclusion and
treatment hold.

Treating a wet, insulated crawlspace produces a building that is still damp,
still attractive to everything moisture-driven, and still deteriorating, with an
invoice attached. We would rather explain that than take the work in the wrong
order.

Rebuilt buildings also have new geometry. Sections repaired at different times
meet at junctions that did not previously exist, utilities were rerouted, and
skirting and venting were sometimes replaced quickly under real pressure. Those
junctions are where we find entry points, and it is why we go underneath rather
than quoting from the driveway.

Decay fungus is the quiet consequence. Sustained moisture in framing produces
decay that continues long after the water has gone, and decay rather than
insects is what actually costs structural capacity in this climate. Where we
find it we say so, and frequently the person you need next is a contractor.

## Farm ground on every side

The agricultural work around Sumas is the same trade as the rest of the north
county, at a scale between Everson and Lynden.

**Feed-room rodents** are the center of it. An open shed with grain or pellets
supports a population indefinitely, and nothing we apply overcomes an unlimited
food supply. Metal bins with tight lids, spillage cleaned rather than swept
aside, and pallets kept off the wall so runways stay visible do more than any
program. Where an owner does that, everything else works.

**Equipment and wiring** are the priority that persuades skeptics. Rodent damage
to harness wiring, shop electrics and machinery parked for the season is
expensive and sometimes dangerous.

**Placement constraints** are the same as anywhere with livestock: tamper-
resistant stations anchored outside, mechanical control within buildings, and
nothing reachable by cattle, poultry, barn cats or working dogs. Where rodent
control products are used at all we choose programs and active ingredients
selected to limit secondary poisoning risk to the raptors and pets that share
this country.

**Stored feed** is the reservoir. Grain and pellets in open storage are an
introduced species without the same federal protection, so netting, roost
exclusion and closing gable openings are all available. Scaring them is not a
durable answer — they habituate within a fortnight and return to a food source
that has not changed.

**Flies around livestock** are a production problem rather than a nuisance one,
and they are won or lost on breeding sites rather than on adults. The wet margin
of a manure pack, a leaking waterer, feed fermenting at the end of a bunk,
bedding that has not been turned, the drainage low point that never dries — a
short and predictable list, and correcting it beats any spray schedule.

## Stink bugs and Asian lady beetles

Both are overwintering insects rather than a structural problem, and both are a
real and recurring Sumas call. They gather on sunlit walls in autumn — south and
west faces, warm afternoons — and then work into the building through the same
small gaps everything else uses. The important thing to know is that once they
are inside a wall void, treating them there is the wrong move: they die in the
cavity, and what is left behind draws carpet beetles the following spring. The
work is exterior, and it belongs before they gather rather than after.

## The residential town

The housing in Sumas is a small-town mix, with the added complication that much
of it has been repaired or rebuilt in the last few years.

Older stock carries the standard regional pattern: shallow crawlspaces over bare
earth, failed vent screens, framing that has taken water and now supports
moisture ants, and the damp-crawl company of sowbugs, millipedes, springtails
and Oriental cockroaches. Newer and rebuilt sections tend to be tight envelopes
with a handful of service penetrations closed with expanding foam, which stops
drafts and does not stop a rodent.

Because the town sits in open agricultural country, the harvest effect is
pronounced. Cutting a field removes cover in an afternoon and the displaced
population goes to the nearest structure. Combined with the autumn rain and the
low ground, that makes September and October the decisive weeks of the year
here.

**Cluster flies** are heavy, as they are anywhere surrounded by pasture. They
breed in earthworms out in the fields, overwinter in south and west-facing
walls, and emerge indoors on the first mild afternoon of the year. Nothing about
that reflects on the house. The durable fix is exterior work in late summer,
sealing the gaps and screening the vents before they move in, rather than
chasing them at the window in February.

## Carpenter ants and moisture ants

Both are common here and the distinction matters.

Moisture ants colonize wood that is already wet and decaying. Finding them is
useful information: the water is the problem and the ants are the symptom.
Treating without correcting the source produces a new colony in the same wood,
which on flood-affected property is a particularly common outcome.

Carpenter ants excavate galleries to nest and prefer wood that has begun to
soften. The parent colony is nearly always outdoors — a stump, a fence post, a
landscape timber, a woodpile — with satellites inside the structure. The
satellites are what people see, and treating them without locating the parent is
why these jobs develop a reputation for coming back.

In spring, winged carpenter ants get mistaken for termite swarmers constantly.
Ants have an elbowed antenna, a pinched waist and wings of unequal length;
termites have a straight antenna, a uniform body and four wings of equal size.
Correct identification changes the whole approach, and we would rather establish
it on a free visit than sell the wrong treatment.

## The Sumas year

**Winter.** Interior rodent work and damage assessment, in houses and in
commercial buildings alike. On rebuilt property this is when the gaps left by a
hurried repair start showing themselves.

**Late winter.** Cluster flies and lady beetles emerge at sunny windows on the
first mild afternoon, which surprises people who associate flies with summer.

**Spring.** The busiest planning stretch. Anything structural on a commercial building has to be identified and scheduled now rather than reacted to in July, because summer is when the freight and the wasps both peak. Ants become active,
and wasp queens found nests that are trivial to deal with at this stage.

**Summer.** Peak freight movement, peak fly pressure around livestock, and peak
wasp activity from July as colonies mature and workers switch to sugar. It is
also the correct window for structural exclusion work: dry ground, dry
crawlspaces, and access at its easiest.

**Harvest and autumn.** Fields are cut, cover disappears, the rain returns and
the low ground saturates. A displaced population moves toward buildings over a
few weeks. For commercial premises this is when yard margins and staging areas
matter most, because that is where the pressure is coming from.

## What we will not sell you

**Bird work or wildlife removal.** Businesses under time pressure ask, and the answer does not change: it is not what we do. We will say so on the phone and point you to somebody who handles it rather than taking the booking.

**A sealed warehouse.** A building with dock doors open through the working day
cannot be made rodent-proof and anyone promising otherwise is either
inexperienced or selling. What we quote is a defended perimeter, controlled
interior placement and an honest account of the housekeeping points that matter.

**A recurring program over a wet crawlspace.** On flood-rebuilt property this is
the easiest way to be overcharged. Drying and removal come first; service laid
over an unresolved moisture problem is a subscription rather than a solution.

**Mole trapping.** Common request on acreage here, and not something we do. The
methods that work are restrictively regulated in Washington and most of what is
sold to homeowners does not work, so we would rather refer you than take the
money.

## Stored-product insects

Worth its own section here, because a town with warehousing sees these more than
a town without one, and because they are routinely misdiagnosed.

Beetles and moths that infest stored grain, flour, seed, pet food, dried goods
and animal feed arrive inside the product rather than walking in through a door.
That distinction changes everything about the response. Treating a building
perimeter for an insect that came in on a pallet accomplishes nothing.

The work is finding the source. That means tracing back through stock to the
infested lot, looking at what has been sitting longest and furthest back,
checking spillage in seams and under racking, and being systematic rather than
hopeful. In a feed room or a warehouse the source is frequently a single
forgotten bag or a spill in a corner that has been quietly producing adults for
months.

After that it is stock rotation, sealed containers, cleaning of the residues in
racking and floor seams, and monitoring to confirm it is actually finished. On a
commercial account we would rather write that up plainly than sell a treatment
schedule that leaves the source in place.

## Spiders and the domestic list

The household side of Sumas is the standard regional set. Giant house spiders
generate the autumn calls and are harmless — the large fast ones are wandering
males, not an invasion, and there is no brown recluse in this state's northwest
corner. Silverfish, sowbugs and millipedes belong to the damp crawl and are
reporting a moisture condition rather than a pest problem in their own right.

## Ants in a town that has been rebuilt

Carpenter and moisture ants are worth a second look here because the rebuild
history changes where they turn up.

A building repaired after water damage has new framing meeting old framing, and
the junctions between them are rarely as tight as either section on its own.
Where a repair was done quickly and under pressure — as many were — there can be
original material left in place that took moisture and never fully dried, sitting
directly against new lumber.

That is precisely the condition moisture ants want, and it is why we sometimes
find them in a building that looks freshly finished. The ants are not a comment
on the repair. They are reporting that something behind the new surface is still
damp, and that is genuinely useful information for an owner to have before it
becomes a decay problem.

Carpenter ants work differently, preferring wood that has begun to soften and
keeping a parent nest outdoors. On these lots the parent is usually in a stump,
a fence post, a woodpile or a landscape timber, and finding it is what makes the
treatment hold rather than repeat.

In both cases the diagnostic question is the same one: where is the water coming
from, and is it still coming.

## Spiders and the seasonal domestic list

The household calendar here is the standard regional one.

Giant house spiders generate the autumn calls, and the answer is reassuring:
harmless, long established in western Washington, and the large fast ones are
wandering males rather than an infestation. Exterior lighting concentrates the
insects they feed on, so a fixture change frequently does more than a treatment.

Stink bugs and lady beetles overwinter alongside the cluster flies in sunny
walls and emerge on the same mild afternoons. Silverfish and sowbugs belong to
the damp crawl. Wasps peak from late July, and on a rebuilt building the new
soffit and eave detailing is often exactly the sheltered cavity a colony wants.

## Moisture, and why it is the recurring theme

Almost everything on this page traces back to water, which is worth stating
plainly.

The flood history is the obvious part, but the ordinary Whatcom County pattern
runs underneath it regardless: no sustained hard freeze, a long wet season, and
wood that never fully dries. Populations are never reset by winter, and damp
framing supports the whole moisture-driven cast — moisture ants, decay fungus,
dampwood termites, silverfish and the crawlspace insects.

So the diagnostic question on nearly every visit is the same. Where is the water
coming from, is it still coming, and is the answer a treatment or a repair. On a
rebuilt building it is frequently a repair, and saying so costs us the recurring
service.
