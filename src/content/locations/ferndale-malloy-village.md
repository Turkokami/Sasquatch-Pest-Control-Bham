## Attached housing beside a highway

Malloy Village is townhomes and family streets on the freeway side of Ferndale.
Two features shape the pest work and they pull in different directions.

The housing is largely attached or closely spaced, built to a consistent
standard over a short period, which means the buildings share structure and
share failure points. And the development sits near the interstate corridor,
which puts a long strip of undisturbed rough ground within easy reach.

One of those makes problems spread. The other supplies them.

## The party wall is not a wall

The most useful thing anybody in attached housing can understand is that the
wall between units is not the barrier it looks like.

Behind the drywall, an attached building is substantially continuous. The party
wall cavity frequently runs uninterrupted from the foundation to the roof
space. Plumbing and electrical chases pass vertically between floors and
sometimes laterally between units. Soffits run the length of a row. Roof spaces,
unless properly fire-stopped and sealed at every division, are effectively one
volume with partitions in it.

A rodent or insect population living in that shared space does not recognize
the addresses. It works a territory that includes several units and moves
between them freely.

This explains the single most common experience in this kind of housing: a
treatment works, and then it stops working. Activity drops for a couple of
weeks because what was inside got dealt with. The population, undisturbed in
the shared space, carries on and moves back in.

It did not fail. It was scoped to a unit when the problem was scoped to a
building.

## What can actually be done about that

The complete answer is coordinated work across the affected units and the
exterior of the whole structure, arranged through the association or the
property manager. Where that is possible it is much cheaper per household than
everybody solving it separately, and it is the only version that finishes.

Where it is not possible — and often it is not, because one owner will not
engage — there is still real work available on a single unit, and it is worth
being clear about what it is.

Sealing the shared routes on your side limits what reaches you. That means the
penetrations through the party wall for services, the point where the chase
passes your ceiling, the gap where your unit's roof space meets the next, and
the exterior openings on your own elevation. None of that removes a population
living next door. All of it reduces what arrives.

We will do that work and we will tell you plainly what it achieves, rather than
selling it as a solution to somebody else's untreated unit.

## The highway verge

The strip beside a major road is one of the more reliable rodent habitats in
any developed area, and almost nobody thinks about it.

It is continuous, it runs for miles, it is rough rather than maintained, nobody
walks it, nothing disturbs it, and it collects litter — including food waste —
along its whole length. It is, in effect, a linear reserve.

The properties closest to it take the most pressure, and the pattern is
seasonal in the same way it is on farmland: when the verge is cut or cleared,
the animals living in it relocate that week, and the nearest structures with a
gap in them are where they go.

None of that can be fixed at the source, which is why the work here is entirely
about the building. What matters is whether there is a route in, and on
uniformly built housing the routes are consistent enough to be found quickly.

## One construction era, one set of failure points

Almost everything about a single-period development is a disadvantage for
originality and an advantage for diagnosis.

When a development goes up over a few years to a consistent specification,
every building has the same details, the same materials and the same
shortcuts. So when a problem is traced on one unit to a particular vent type, a
particular garage door seal, the way a dryer vent was run, or a gap left where
the siding meets the foundation, the odds are very high that the identical
detail exists on every unit in the row.

That turns a search into a check. On a first visit we will find the route on
your unit and then look specifically for it everywhere it should also exist,
which is faster and more thorough than starting from scratch each time.

It also means that where an association is willing to act, a single well-chosen
repair specification can be applied across the whole development and close a
route on every building at once.

## Garages, and the seal at the bottom corners

Attached and townhome housing nearly always has an integral garage, and the
garage is the most consistently productive place we look in this kind of
development.

The bottom corners of a roll-up door are where the seal fails first, and the
test costs nothing: stand inside with the door closed and look at the corners.
Daylight means a gap, and a gap at floor level in a space that connects
directly to the house is the most convenient possible entrance.

Everything else about a garage helps as well, from the pest's point of view. It
is unheated but sheltered, it is quiet, it holds stored soft material, and
frequently it holds pet food, birdseed or garden products in bags rather than
containers.

Fitting a proper seal is inexpensive and permanent, and moving stored food into
solid containers costs nothing at all.

## Ants along an irrigated foundation

Irrigated planting against a foundation is standard in developments of this
kind, and it produces the most common non-rodent call we get here.

Odorous house ants and pavement ants forage along a watered, warm foundation
line and come indoors along it. They are a nuisance rather than a structural
problem, and they respond to the right approach quickly — which is baiting
along the trail rather than spraying the trail, because a sprayed trail
frequently splits a colony into several and makes the following month worse.

Carpenter ants are a different matter and the identification is the point.
Carpenter ants excavate galleries in wood, and finding them indoors means
something about moisture or a nearby colony rather than a passing trail.
Moisture ants mean wet wood specifically. Three insects that look broadly
similar to most people, with three different meanings.

Watering the foundation less, and keeping a gap between planting and siding,
does more than most of what can be applied.

## Waste, recycling and shared collection points

Where bins are shared or grouped, waste becomes the most important thing on the
site and almost nobody treats it that way.

A shared bin store or a row of containers at a collection point concentrates
food waste in one place, permanently, on a predictable weekly cycle. If the
lids do not close, if bags get left beside rather than inside, or if cardboard
is stacked against the containers, that will support a population no treatment
can out-run.

The arithmetic is worth stating plainly. A treatment program costs money every
month. Bin lids that close cost nothing after the first repair, and they remove
the food supply rather than the individuals eating it.

Where collection is grouped this is an association matter rather than a
household one, and it is the single highest-value thing an association can act
on in this kind of development.

## What renting changes, and what it does not

A share of this housing is rented, and the split of responsibility causes more
delay than the pest problem does.

The pattern is familiar. A tenant reports something and waits. A landlord is
uncertain whether it is a maintenance issue or a housekeeping one. Weeks pass
while a problem that was small gets larger, and in attached housing it reaches
next door in the meantime.

Our position is straightforward and we will put it in writing for either party.
We report what we found, where it is, what is causing it, and which items are
structural. A gap where a service passes through a wall is a building matter.
Food stored badly is a housekeeping matter. Most reports contain some of each,
and saying which is which lets people get on with it rather than argue about
it.

Anybody can call us. We will not discuss a tenant's home with a landlord, or
the reverse, beyond what the person who booked the work has agreed to.

## Wasps in new-construction soffits

Wasps are on the Ferndale list specifically for the soffits, and townhome rows
are a good example of why.

Modern soffit and fascia construction creates long continuous voids with small
access points at intervals — vents, joints, the gap where two runs meet, the
end of a run at a gable. Those are exactly what a paper wasp or a yellowjacket
queen is looking for in spring, and they are usually twenty feet up where
nobody inspects them.

Because a soffit run can extend across several units, a nest in one is
frequently reached most easily from another, which is one more thing that
argues for treating the row rather than the address.

European paper wasps build small open combs under railings, deck rails and
window frames and are markedly less defensive. Yellowjackets in a soffit void
are not, and a nest above a front door in August is a genuine problem for a
household with children.

This is family housing with small yards, and that shapes how work gets done

Placement matters. Anything applied or placed goes where children and pets do

than open placements, and interior work planned around the rooms that get used.

And where there is a version of a job that uses less or nothing at all — a

## Bed bugs in attached housing

Bed bugs travel with people, not with housekeeping, and in attached housing
they present the same structural problem as everything else here.

They arrive in luggage or on secondhand furniture. Under pressure in one unit,
they will move along shared voids and service runs into the next. A treated
unit surrounded by untreated ones does not stay treated.

Reporting early is the whole game and shame is the obstacle. Nobody should feel
accused by finding them, and a development where an early report is treated as
useful rather than as fault does far better than one where people wait.

We charge to verify and credit it against treatment. On a shared building that
verification is valuable on its own — it establishes whether what somebody has
been bitten by is actually a bed bug before several units get treated on an
assumption.

## Storage, and small yards with a lot in them

Small lots concentrate storage. A shed, a stack of firewood, garden furniture
under a cover, bikes and toys against a fence, and bins that live at the side
of the house because there is nowhere else for them.

Each of those is undisturbed sheltered material against a building, and
collectively they are usually the most productive thing to change on a small
lot. Not because any one matters much, but because there are six of them within
twenty feet of the wall.

The one that consistently matters most is waste. Bins that do not close, or
that are stored hard against the house, will support what no treatment will
out-run.

## Cluster flies, lady beetles and the shared roof space

The autumn invaders behave differently in attached housing than they do on a
detached house, and it is worth knowing why before anybody starts spraying
indoors.

Lady beetles, stink bugs and cluster flies overwinter in voids rather than in
rooms, and in a townhome row the void they choose is frequently the shared roof
space. They enter high, through a soffit vent or a gap at a gable end, settle
in the attic over the whole row, and then filter down into individual units
through light fittings, loft hatches and the gaps around service runs.

That is why one household can be overrun while their immediate neighbor sees
almost nothing, and why treating a single unit's rooms achieves so little. The
population is above all of them.

None of these bite, breed indoors or damage the building, so the sensible work
is at the entry points on the roof line and correctly timed exterior treatment
in early autumn, done for the row rather than for an address. Where a
management company will authorize that, it is much cheaper per household than
eleven separate interior visits that do not fix anything.
