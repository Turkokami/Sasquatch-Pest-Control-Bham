## Cedar, slope and forest

Sudden Valley is one of the most distinctive places we work, and its pest
profile follows directly from three facts about how it was built.

It went up largely in the 1970s, as a recreational and second-home community
around a golf course and a lake, on wooded and often steep ground. The
construction of that era and that intent means cedar and wood siding, generous
venting, split levels and daylight basements cut into slope, decks projecting
over grade, and buildings placed deliberately close to standing timber because
the trees are the point.

Fifty years on, most of it is full-time housing. The trees are larger, the cedar
is weathered, and the same features that make the community attractive are the
ones producing the work.

## Carpenter ants, which are the signature problem

If Sudden Valley has one defining pest, this is it.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in it to nest, and they
strongly prefer wood that has begun to soften — which is precisely what
weathered cedar trim, fascia, deck framing and the bottom edge of siding become
after several decades in this climate.

The part that matters, and that most treatment gets wrong, is the colony
structure. A mature carpenter ant population here almost always has a **parent
colony outdoors** — in a stump, a standing dead tree, a fallen log, a landscape
timber, a fence post, a woodpile — with **satellite nests** inside the structure.
The satellites are what people see: workers on a counter, frass under a window,
a rustling in a wall at night.

Treating the satellite while the parent sits twenty feet away in a stump gets
you a quiet couple of months. That is the whole reason carpenter ant jobs
develop a reputation for coming back.

So the work here is investigative before it is anything else. We are looking for
the parent colony on the property: the stump nobody thinks about, the section of
deck framing that stays wet, the dead limb over the roof, the timber retaining
wall. Where we find it, the result holds. Where the parent sits on a neighboring
lot or on common ground, we will say so plainly and plan around it rather than
implying we have solved something we have not.

Frass is the useful diagnostic. Carpenter ants push out shavings, so the debris
below a gallery is fibrous and often contains insect body parts. That is
different from termite pellets and different from the fine powder a wood-boring
beetle leaves, and the distinction changes the treatment entirely.

## The watershed, which constrains everything

Sudden Valley sits in the Lake Whatcom catchment, and Lake Whatcom is the
drinking water reservoir for Bellingham and much of the surrounding area. A
great deal of this community drains toward it.

We treat that as a real constraint on how we work rather than as something to
mention and move past.

In practice it means we lead with exclusion, habitat modification and contained
placements rather than anything broadcast, particularly on the lake side of a
property. It means we pay attention to forecast rain before scheduling exterior
work, because an application ahead of heavy weather in a catchment is a poor
decision regardless of what the label permits. It means bait and rodent control
products go in tamper-resistant, weather-protected stations rather than anywhere
open. And it means that when the correct answer is a drainage correction, a
regrade, a gutter repair or removing the wet timber that is feeding the problem,
we say so, even though none of those is work we get paid for.

The raptor consideration reinforces the same approach. This is owl, hawk and
eagle country, and those raptors hunt the exact population we are managing. Where
we use rodent control products at all we choose programs and active ingredients
selected to limit secondary poisoning risk to non-target wildlife and to pets, a
point that matters more here than in most places.

None of this is a marketing posture. It is what working inside a drinking water
catchment on a forest edge actually requires, and it is one of the clearer
differences between a local crew and a route driver working a national script.

## What the slope and the construction do

Steep lots change the building envelope in ways that matter.

A daylight basement means part of the foundation is buried and part is exposed,
and the transition between them is where water and insects both find their way
in. Sloped crawlspaces collect water at the low end and stay wet there long
after the rest has dried, which is the classic moisture ant and decay fungus
situation. Retaining walls and timber terracing hold moisture against structure
and, when they are wooden and old, become parent colony sites in their own
right.

Decks over grade create a sheltered, dry, undisturbed space underneath that is
excellent harborage, and they attach to the house at a ledger — which in this
housing stock is very frequently flashed badly or not at all. A poorly flashed
ledger produces exactly the sustained wet framing that carpenter ants, moisture
ants and decay fungus all require. It is the single most common structural
defect we find in this community and it is worth checking on any house of this
era.

Cedar shake roofs, where they remain, hold moisture and support moss, and the
resulting damp roof deck is its own set of problems.

## Working within the association

Sudden Valley is a covenanted community with an association, and exterior work
frequently needs approval — vent covers, screening, netting, chimney caps and
anything else that changes how a building looks from outside.

We are used to this and it is not an obstacle, but it does change the sequence.
Tell us early if approval will be required, so we can specify the work in a form
likely to be approved the first time rather than doing the paperwork twice. In
most cases there is a way to do exclusion properly that is also visually
acceptable, and where the choice is between an ugly effective solution and a
tidy ineffective one we will tell you which is which and let you decide.

Where a problem originates on common ground rather than on your lot — a parent
colony in a stump on a greenbelt, for instance — that is worth raising with the
association, and we will set out the recommended work and its location in a form
you can hand over. Where the organism is one of the wood-destroying group, note
that a document recording findings of damage or infestation is a regulated
report we do not issue; we will say so rather than hand the association
something that resembles one.

## Wasps and stinging insects on wooded lots

Wooded, sloped property generates a particular wasp problem and it is worth
understanding before somebody meets it with a mower.

**Yellowjackets** nest in the ground, and on these lots the ground is banked,
terraced, root-bound and covered in leaf litter — full of cavities. A nest in a
slope beside a path or a driveway is invisible until it is disturbed, and late
in the season a disturbed colony is a genuine medical problem rather than an
inconvenience. They also take wall voids and the space behind skirting, entering
through a gap in the siding or a failed vent screen.

**Bald-faced hornets** build the large gray paper nests people photograph, often
in a tree or under an eave, and on a wooded lot they are frequently not noticed
until the leaves come down.

**Paper wasps** under eaves, deck rails and in the corners of outbuildings are
the mildest of the three, and where they are somewhere nobody goes we will often
recommend leaving them alone rather than treating for the sake of it.

The seasonal shift matters everywhere but especially here. Early in the summer
colonies are feeding protein to brood and are largely uninterested in people.
From late July they switch to sugar, the colony is at maximum size, and that is
when they arrive at food on a deck and when defensive incidents happen.

The cheapest intervention by a wide margin is a spring walk of the eaves, decks
and outbuildings, when a founding nest is the size of a golf ball.

## The Sudden Valley year

**Winter.** Rodent activity indoors at its height, and the season when attic and
crawlspace damage from the autumn becomes obvious — soiled insulation, chewed
ducting, a stain in a ceiling.

**Early spring.** Carpenter ants become active, and because they have wintered
inside damp framing the first sighting is usually indoors rather than out.
Swarmers appear on windowsills and get mistaken for termites. Wasp queens begin
founding nests, which is the cheapest possible moment to deal with them.

**Late spring.** Ant activity peaks and the moisture ants appearing indoors are reporting a water problem rather than an ant problem. On these sloped lots that usually means the low end of a crawlspace or a deck ledger that was never flashed properly.

**Summer.** Wasp season, spider egg hatch, and the correct window for everything
structural. Dry crawlspaces, dry ground, and access to the exterior at its easiest. Anything preventive should be scheduled
here.

**Autumn.** The busiest stretch. The rain returns and rodents move off the forest edge toward
buildings within a few weeks. Spiders come indoors during the wandering season.
Exclusion completed now is the difference between a quiet winter and a
remediation job.

## Rodents and the underfloor question

Rodent work here has its own character because of how the houses sit on the
ground.

A daylight basement or a split level built into slope means the building meets
earth on more sides and at more levels than a house on flat ground. Crawlspaces
are often stepped, partly excavated and awkward to move through, and the low end
holds water. Decks over grade give a sheltered approach right up to the
structure. Utility runs enter at several elevations.

The result is that finding the entry point takes longer here than it does in
town, and skipping that step is why so much rodent work on these lots fails.
Placing traps where droppings were seen addresses the symptom. The animal is
getting in somewhere, and on a wooded sloped lot that somewhere is frequently
not obvious from standing height.

Insulation is the other recurring issue. A lot of the original 1970s insulation
in this community has been supplemented, disturbed or partially replaced over
five decades, and rodent-soiled insulation in a crawl or attic is both a
contamination problem and an ongoing attractant. Where that is the situation the
sequence matters: exclude first, remove and replace second. Doing it the other
way round means installing new material into a building that is still open.

Deer mice specifically come off the wooded ground rather than from neighboring
houses, which makes exclusion more effective here than it sometimes is in dense
housing. Where droppings need clearing in an enclosed space we will tell you to
ventilate the area first and avoid sweeping or vacuuming dry material, which is
the sensible precaution regardless of what the risk turns out to be in a
particular building.

## Wet crawlspaces on sloped ground

This is the underlying condition beneath a large share of what we get called
about here, and it is worth treating as its own subject.

A crawlspace cut into a slope does not drain evenly. Water moves through the
soil downhill, arrives at the uphill foundation wall, and finds the low end of
the crawl. What you get is a space that is dry at one end and persistently damp
at the other, with the joists and subfloor above the wet end quietly taking on
moisture for years.

Everything moisture-driven follows from that. Moisture ants colonize framing
that has softened. Decay fungus runs where the wood stays wet, and decay rather
than insects is what actually costs structural capacity in this climate.
Sowbugs, millipedes, springtails and Oriental cockroaches live in the damp end
and occasionally come up into the house. Dampwood termites need exactly these
conditions and nothing else.

The corrections are drainage and grading work more than pest work: getting roof
water discharged well away from the uphill wall, correcting grade that slopes
toward the building, and re-establishing a vapor barrier over clean ground.
Where a crawl has been wet long enough that insulation is hanging down or the
barrier has been pushed aside, removal comes before anything else — wet
fiberglass holds moisture, accelerates decay above it and makes excellent
nesting material.

We will tell you when the person you actually need is a drainage contractor. It
costs us the recurring service and it is the correct answer.

## Access, and what it changes

Worth mentioning because it affects scheduling more than people expect.

The roads through this community are narrow and winding, a good number of
driveways are steep, and many houses sit well below or above the road with the
crawl access on the downhill side. That is not an obstacle so much as something
worth telling us about when you call, because it changes what we can bring and
how long a job takes.

Steep lots also mean the exterior work is genuinely physical. Walking a
perimeter here is not a stroll around a flat rectangle; it is often a slope with
retaining walls, terracing and mature planting held tight against the building.
That is exactly where the interesting findings are — grade against siding,
landscape timber in contact with structure, a downspout discharging onto a bank
that drains straight back at the foundation.

## What we will not sell you

**Wildlife of any kind.** On a wooded lot the question comes up constantly — something in the attic, something under the deck, something on the roof at night. Our answer is the same every time: insects and rodents are ours, everything else belongs to a wildlife operator, and we will tell you which you have.

**Repeated trapping against an open treeline.** If the building is not sealed,
trapping is a subscription rather than a result.

**A recurring program over a wet crawlspace.** Drying and drainage come first.
Service laid on top of an unresolved moisture problem is an indefinite cost.
