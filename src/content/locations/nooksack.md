## The town is small and the ground around it is not

Nooksack has fewer than two thousand residents and covers less than a square mile.
Almost everything that decides pest pressure here happens outside that square mile,
in the berry fields, the dairy ground and the open country that runs from the edge
of town to the foothills.

That is the single most useful thing to understand about pest control in this
place. The problems people call about are usually not generated by their own
building. They are generated by several hundred acres of cover and food nearby,
and they arrive at the building on a schedule set by farm work rather than by
anything the homeowner is doing.

It means the timing of a job matters more here than the products used in it, and
it means a house that had no trouble for four years can be overrun in a fortnight
without anything about the house having changed.

## Berry ground, and what harvest does to it

Whatcom County grows more red raspberries than anywhere else in the country, and
Nooksack sits in the middle of that. Raspberry and blueberry acreage surrounds the
town on most sides, along with the dairy and grass ground mixed through it.

A mature berry field is excellent rodent habitat for most of the year. It is dense
at ground level, it holds moisture, it is rarely disturbed through the growing
season, and from midsummer it carries fruit. Populations build under that cover
through spring and summer without anybody seeing much evidence of them.

Then harvest happens. Machines move through, the ground is disturbed, fruit is
gone, and within a few weeks the canes are cut and the cover is removed. The
animals living in that field do not disappear when it is cut. They relocate, and
they relocate to the nearest structure that offers what the field has stopped
offering.

This is why the busiest stretch for rural rodent calls around Nooksack is late
summer into autumn rather than the middle of winter, which is when most people
expect it. By the time it is properly cold, the animals that were going to come
inside have usually been inside for weeks.

## What that looks like at a building

The first structures to take it are the ones nearest the field and least sealed:
equipment sheds, detached shops, pump houses, barns and older outbuildings. Those
buildings frequently have gaps that nobody minds, because until now nothing was
using them.

From there it moves to the house. A crawlspace with an unscreened vent, a garage
with a worn door seal, a gap where a plumbing line passes through a rim joist —
all of these are ordinary and all of them are enough. Rodents do not need a
failure, only an opening, and a quarter of an inch is enough for a mouse.

The practical response on a rural property is to treat the outbuildings as part of
the same job rather than as separate ones. Sealing a house well while a shed
forty feet away stays open is not solving the problem, it is moving the address.

## Cluster flies, and why they concentrate here

Cluster flies are one of the defining rural pests of this part of the county, and
Nooksack gets them heavily.

They are not a hygiene problem and they have nothing to do with the state of a
kitchen. The larvae develop in earthworms in pasture and field soil, which means
the population is produced by the ground around a building rather than by anything
inside it. Open country with permanent grass and moist soil produces a great many
of them, and there is a great deal of that here.

In late summer and early autumn the adults look for a place to overwinter. They
choose warm vertical surfaces, gather on them in numbers through the afternoon,
and work into whatever gaps that wall offers — under siding, around window frames,
into attic spaces and wall voids. Through winter they sit dormant in those voids.
On the first warm days of late winter, some of them wake up on the wrong side and
end up in the room.

That last part is what most people actually call about: flies appearing indoors in
February with the windows shut and no obvious source. The source is a decision the
insects made the previous September.

## Stink bugs and Asian lady beetles do the same thing

The brown marmorated stink bug and the multicolored Asian lady beetle follow the
same pattern on the same walls at roughly the same time, and the three are
routinely found together.

None of them breeds indoors. None of them damages the building. None of them bites.
They are a nuisance rather than a threat, and that distinction matters, because it
changes what a sensible treatment looks like. Nobody should be fogging a house
repeatedly through winter for insects that are dormant in a wall and cannot
reproduce there.

The work that helps is done before they arrive: closing the gaps they use, and
treating the walls they gather on in the right few weeks of late summer. Once they
are inside the void, the honest options narrow to removing what emerges and doing
the exclusion properly the following year.

## The overwintering window is short

The reason we push people to plan this in August rather than October is that the
window is genuinely narrow.

Treating the exterior walls too early means the residual has degraded before the
insects gather. Treating after they have already worked into the siding means
treating a wall they are no longer on. The useful period is the few weeks when
they are massing on the outside and have not yet moved through, and in this part
of the county that is usually late August into September depending on the year.

Sealing can be done at any time and is worth more in the long run. It is also the
part most people put off, because a gap under a piece of trim does not look like
anything until several hundred insects have used it.

## Everson is a mile away and this is not that page

Nooksack and Everson sit about a mile apart and share a school district, a valley
and a good deal of housing stock. It would be easy to write the same page twice.

They are not the same job. Everson sits directly on the Nooksack River, and water
is the first thing about pest work there — flood-affected buildings, rebuilt
crawlspaces, and rodents pushed off saturated ground. Nooksack sits slightly back
from the river, and the fields matter more here than the water does.

Both towns took serious flooding in November 2021 and there are buildings in both
that were repaired afterwards. Where that is true of a property here, the same
principle applies as anywhere: material that has been wet stays a problem until it
comes out. Wet insulation holds moisture, feeds decay and makes excellent nesting
material, and sealing a building around it simply traps the conditions in place.

## Carpenter ants and moisture ants

The ant work here follows the buildings rather than the fields.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They excavate it to nest in, and they strongly
prefer wood that is already softened by moisture. In practice that means the
places on a rural building where water has been getting in quietly for years: a
sill plate under a leaking hose bib, the framing around a poorly flashed window,
a section of rim joist above a crawlspace that never dries out.

Moisture ants are an even more direct signal. Finding them is close to a
guarantee that there is a genuine water problem in that timber, because they need
consistently damp wood to nest in at all.

Treating either without dealing with the water is temporary by definition. A
colony removed from wet wood is replaced by another colony in the same wet wood,
usually within a season or two. On a property this far out, where a soft sill can
sit unnoticed behind a workbench for years, finding the water is most of the job.

## Wasps on a rural property

Wasps are worse on acreage than in town, for two straightforward reasons.

There is more building surface per property — house, barn, shop, pump house,
carport — and more of it is unpainted, unsealed or open-sided, which is exactly
the sheltered structure a colony wants. And there is far more forage, so colonies
get larger before anybody notices them.

Yellowjackets that nest in the ground are the ones that cause injuries here,
because they are found by mowing rather than by looking. A nest in a bank or an
old rodent burrow gives no warning until a machine goes over it. If there is
regular mowing on the property, it is worth knowing where the activity is before
August rather than discovering it at speed.

We do not treat honey bees, and we will say so plainly. A swarm is a beekeeper's
job and there are people locally who will collect one.

## Spiders and the rest of the domestic list

Spiders are the most common thing people ask about and among the least dangerous.
There are no medically significant spiders established in Whatcom County. Giant
house spiders and cross orbweavers account for most of what turns up, and the
autumn surge indoors is males wandering to find females rather than an invasion.

The rest of the residential list here is ordinary: sowbugs and pillbugs around
damp foundations, earwigs in wet ground cover, silverfish in bathrooms and older
crawlspaces, and cluster flies as described above. Most of it responds to
moisture management and sealing rather than to repeated spraying.

## Feed rooms, coolers and the small commercial side

A fair share of the work around Nooksack is not residential at all. Berry and
dairy operations run buildings that concentrate exactly what rodents want: feed
in bulk, water, warmth and constant door movement.

A feed room is the clearest case. Grain and pellets in volume, spillage that is
difficult to eliminate entirely, and a door that opens many times a day. No amount
of treatment outcompetes an open food supply, so the work there is sequencing:
storage in sealed containers first, spill discipline second, the building envelope
third, and control measures last rather than first.

Cooler and packing buildings have the opposite problem. They are cleaner, but they
run doors open through harvest and carry a great deal of vehicle traffic, which is
how populations move onto a site rather than build up on it. On agricultural
accounts we plan around the operation's calendar. Turning up in the middle of
harvest to do exclusion work is not useful to anybody.

## What actually holds on a rural building

Exclusion on acreage fails in predictable ways, and most of them come down to
material choice.

Expanding foam alone does not hold. Rodents chew through it, and on a building
this exposed it degrades in sunlight at the edges. Hardware cloth stapled over the
outside of a vent works until the staples rust, which in this climate is a few
seasons. Steel wool packed into a gap is a stopgap and rusts into a stain down the
siding.

What holds is metal that is fastened rather than pressed in: screened covers cut
and folded to fit the opening, mortar or backing rod and sealant where the gap is
in masonry, and sheet metal over chewed edges on a door or a sill. It costs more
on the day and it is still there in ten years, which on a property with a dozen
openings across five buildings is the only economics that works.

The other thing that holds is doing the outbuildings at the same time. Sealing a
house properly while the shop stays open produces a house that is fine and a shop
that is worse, because the population that was spread across two buildings is now
concentrated in one.

## Rodents in the attic, and what you are actually hearing

Most rural rodent calls start with a noise, and the noise tells you more than
people expect.

Scratching and scurrying overhead after dark, in short bursts, is usually roof
rats or mice in the attic or a ceiling void. Heavier, slower movement lower in the
building is more often Norway rats in a crawlspace or wall base. Rapid activity
at dusk and again before dawn fits rodents; something moving during the middle of
the day is more likely a squirrel, and that is a different job.

What matters is that the noise is a late signal rather than an early one. By the
time an animal is audible from a bedroom, it is established, it has a route in and
out, and it has almost certainly been there a while. That is why the first thing
we do is find the entry rather than set traps in the roof space. Trapping without
sealing removes the individuals and leaves the door open behind them.

Insulation is the part people underestimate. Rodents tunnel through blown-in
material, contaminate it and flatten it, and once that has happened the insulation
has stopped doing the job it was installed for. Where it is bad enough, removing
and replacing it is part of the repair rather than an upsell — and everyone on
staff has completed installer training for that reason.

## The Nooksack year

**January to March.** Overwintering insects waking in wall voids and appearing
indoors on warm days. Rodents already established and now breeding. This is
cleanup and exclusion season rather than prevention season.

**April to June.** Ants become active as ground warms. Wasp queens start nests —
the cheapest time all year to deal with one, when it is the size of a golf ball
and holds a single insect.

**July and August.** Wasp colonies reach the size that gets noticed. Berry harvest
begins and the first field-displaced rodents start showing up in outbuildings.
This is also the window for overwintering treatment on south and west walls.

**September and October.** The heaviest weeks. Fields cut, cover gone, rodent
pressure at its peak, and cluster flies, stink bugs and lady beetles massing on
warm walls at the same time.

**November and December.** Whatever got in is in. Cold weather concentrates
activity in the warm parts of a building, which is when people hear it overhead
at night.

## What we will not sell you

**A monthly contract for a house with no ongoing pressure.** Plenty of properties
here have a real, continuous problem because of what surrounds them. Plenty do
not. If a one-time treatment and some sealing will hold, we will say so.

**Repeated interior fogging for overwintering insects.** They are dormant in a
void, they cannot reproduce there, and treating the room they emerged into does
nothing about the several hundred still in the wall. Exterior timing and exclusion
are the work.

**Treatment for a wet-wood ant problem without addressing the wet wood.** We will
do the treatment, but we will tell you it is temporary, and we would rather quote
the repair.
