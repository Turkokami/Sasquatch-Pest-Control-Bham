## The county seat, and three towns inside it

Mount Vernon is the largest place we work south of the county line, and like
most towns of its age it is really several places sharing a name. From our side
of the work there are three, and the address tells you which one you are in
before anything else does.

There is the historic downtown, with commercial buildings on shared walls. There
is the older residential ground, much of it low and close to the river. And
there is the newer development on the edges, built outward across what was
agricultural land within living memory.

Each of those fails differently, and the mistake we see most often is a program
designed for one being applied to another.

## Downtown, where the problem belongs to the block

Mount Vernon has a real historic core — masonry and timber commercial buildings,
many over a century old, built shoulder to shoulder.

That construction has a consequence people find genuinely surprising. Buildings
in a block like this are connected. Shared party walls, common basements,
utility chases and service runs that pass from one building into the next, and
old drainage that was extended and re-extended as tenants changed. A rodent
population in that block is a single population using the whole structure, and
it does not recognize the property lines.

Which means treating one tenant while the buildings either side do nothing
produces a temporary result at best. The animals move, wait, and come back. We
have watched businesses spend years paying for monthly service against a
population that was never theirs alone.

Where we can get a building owner, or several tenants, working to one plan, the
outcome is different in kind — not incrementally better but actually resolved.
That conversation is harder than selling one contract, and it is the honest one.

The other downtown feature is age itself. Old buildings have old penetrations:
abandoned pipe runs, a filled-in coal chute, a service entry from a utility that
no longer exists, gaps behind a suspended ceiling that nobody has looked above
in decades. Finding those is most of the work, and it is why a proper first
visit downtown takes longer than people expect.

## Food service in old buildings

A good share of the downtown is food service, and it is a specific job.

The building is the first constraint. Older drainage, floors that have been
resurfaced repeatedly, equipment fitted into spaces not designed for it, and
voids behind and beneath fixtures that cannot easily be reached. Those voids are
where German cockroaches persist, and they are why a kitchen can look immaculate
and still have a problem.

Waste is the second and it is where most of the genuine progress sits. A
dumpster with a broken lid, an enclosure that cannot be closed, or bins staged
in an alley overnight will support a rodent population that no service schedule
overcomes. We put that in the report. Quietly servicing around a waste problem
while billing monthly would be dishonest, and it also does not work.

Deliveries are the third. Cardboard and pallets arriving from elsewhere bring
what traveled with them, and in a shared block one tenant's receiving practice
becomes everybody's problem.

## The river and the low ground

The Skagit is a serious river with a long flood history, and Mount Vernon sits
against it behind a dike and floodwall system.

From our point of view a dike is a linear habitat corridor with excellent cover,
running for miles, immediately adjacent to buildings. The sloughs, drainage
ditches and vegetated margins between fields do the same thing on a smaller
scale. The result is continuous connectivity — rodents move the length of the
valley without ever crossing open ground.

You cannot treat a corridor and it would be wrong to try. What you can do is
defend the specific building and accept that the pressure against it is constant
rather than seasonal.

The low ground also saturates early. When the water table comes up and burrows
flood, everything living in them moves toward buildings, and riverfront and
low-lying properties here see that weeks before the higher ground does. If you
live down near the water, your autumn starts earlier than your neighbor's on
the hill.

For any building that has taken water at some point, the sequence matters more
than the product. Wet insulation comes out rather than being treated around — it
holds moisture, accelerates decay in the framing above it and makes excellent
nesting material. The space gets dried properly. The vapor barrier is
re-established over clean ground. Only then does exclusion hold.

## The older residential stock

Away from downtown, the older Mount Vernon housing carries the standard Pacific
Northwest pattern and it is the same pattern we deal with in Bellingham's
prewar neighborhoods.

Shallow crawlspaces over bare earth. Post-and-pier foundations or early stem
walls. Foundation vent screens that rusted through decades ago and have been
open ever since. Board sheathing that has shrunk. Stud cavities that run from
the sill plate to the top plate without a firestop, so something entering at
grade has an uninterrupted route to the attic. And a century of accumulated
utility penetrations, each drilled oversize and none of them ever sealed.

The pests follow directly. Norway rats and house mice in the crawl and the
walls. Carpenter and moisture ants in any framing that has taken water. The
damp-crawl company underneath — sowbugs, millipedes, springtails, silverfish and
Oriental cockroaches — all of which are reporting a moisture condition rather
than being a problem in their own right.

The correct work is structural: sealing the envelope, screening the vents
properly with material that will last, and closing the penetrations. Treatment
handles what is already inside. Without the first part, you are buying the same
service again next year.

## The new edges, and the farmland behind them

Mount Vernon has grown outward, and the newer subdivisions sit against
agricultural ground.

That produces a specific and initially baffling situation: brand-new houses with
rodent problems. Owners find it hard to credit, because people reasonably expect
a new building to be sealed. The newer stock genuinely is tighter — better
sheathing, continuous housewrap, sealed sill plates, and in some cases a
conditioned crawlspace rather than a vented one.

But tightness is not the same as sealed, and the failure points are boringly
consistent. The dryer vent where the duct crosses the rim joist and the exterior
flap has warped. The hose bib and irrigation supply, drilled generously. The
heat pump line set, which enters at an angle and leaves a crescent gap at the
top of the hole. The electrical and low-voltage entries, often clustered behind
a panel in one oversized opening. And the gap under a garage door seal on a slab
that has settled slightly, which is on nearly every house we look at.

Each of those was closed with expanding foam by a subcontractor working at
speed. Foam stops drafts. It does not stop a rodent, which opens it in an
evening.

Behind the fence is the actual driver. Open agricultural ground supports a large
permanent population, and that population moves twice a year for reasons that
have nothing to do with cold. Harvest removes cover overnight — a field that was
habitat in the morning is bare dirt by evening — and everything living in it
looks for the nearest structure. Then the autumn rain floods the low ground and
does the same thing again, harder.

The storage that goes with valley ground matters as much as the fields do. Bulb
sheds, seed and produce storage, feed rooms and equipment barns hold something
edible and something soft to nest in right through the winter, and on this edge
of town they sit closer to the new streets than most buyers realize when they
look at the view. A population wintering in a shed two fields away is a
population with a very short walk to your garage in February, which is why we
would rather scope the outbuilding alongside the house where the owner of one
will talk to the owner of the other.

## Ants, and what they are telling you

Two ants matter here and they get confused constantly.

Moisture ants only establish where the wood has already taken water and started to break down. Finding
them is useful information rather than merely a problem: the water is the
disease and the ants are the symptom. Treating them without correcting the
source produces a new colony in the same timber, which on low-lying Mount Vernon
property is a common and expensive outcome.

Carpenter ants excavate galleries to nest and prefer wood that has begun to
soften. A mature colony almost always keeps its parent nest outdoors — a stump,
a fence post, a landscape timber, a woodpile — with satellite nests inside the
structure. The satellites are what people see, and treating them while the
parent sits twenty feet away is why these jobs develop a reputation for
returning.

In spring, winged carpenter ants get mistaken for termite swarmers regularly.
Ants have an elbowed antenna, a pinched waist and wings of unequal length;
termites have a straight antenna, a uniform body and four wings of the same
size. Identification is the cheapest thing we do and it changes everything after
it.

## The year in Mount Vernon

**Winter.** Rodent activity indoors at its peak, because everything that came in
during the autumn is still there. Damage becomes visible — soiled insulation,
chewed ducting, a stain in a ceiling, a smell in a room that turns out to be
something dead in a cavity.

**Late winter.** Cluster flies and other overwintering insects wake on the first
mild afternoon and appear at south-facing windows. More pronounced on the
properties with open ground around them than in town.

**Spring.** Ants become active, and because they have wintered in damp framing
the first sighting is usually indoors. Swarmers appear on windowsills. Wasp
queens found nests, which is the cheapest possible moment to deal with them and
the moment almost nobody is looking.

**Summer.** Wasp season, and the correct window for everything structural. Dry
ground, dry crawlspaces, easy access to the exterior. Anything preventive should
be scheduled here rather than reacted to in November.

**Harvest and autumn.** The decisive stretch, sharper here than anywhere in our
home county because of the sheer acreage. Fields are cut, the rain returns, the
low ground saturates, and a very large displaced population moves toward
buildings within a few weeks of each other.

## Spiders, and the autumn phone calls

Every September the calls start about large fast spiders indoors, and the answer
is reassuring rather than alarming.

These are almost always giant house spiders. They are harmless, long established
throughout western Washington, and they are not coming inside because it is
getting cold — adult males are wandering in search of mates, and the ones found
stranded in a bathtub are males that got in and could not climb back out.

There is no brown recluse in this corner of the state and there never has been.
Black widows exist in Washington but are associated with the drier country east
and south of the Cascades and are uncommon here. Anyone telling you otherwise
while holding a quote is selling fear rather than a service.

What genuinely reduces the population is unglamorous: knocking down exterior
webbing, dealing with the harborage under decks and in crawl vents, correcting
exterior lighting that concentrates the insects the spiders are eating, and
sealing the door sweeps and thresholds most of them use to get in.

## Cockroaches, and which one you have

Two very different insects get the same name, and the treatments are not
interchangeable.

German cockroaches are indoor and human-associated. They travel in cardboard,
appliances and kitchen equipment, they breed quickly, and they are concentrated
in food service and multi-unit housing. In a building with shared structure they
need a coordinated, building-level plan — treating one unit while the neighbors
go untreated moves the population rather than reducing it.

Oriental cockroaches are a damp-crawlspace insect. They live outdoors and under
buildings, come up through drains and crawl vents, and are reporting a moisture
condition rather than a hygiene one. In the older low-lying housing here they
are common for exactly that reason, and the durable answer is drainage and a
sound vapor barrier rather than repeated interior treatment.

Getting the identification right first is the cheapest thing we do on the visit.

## What we will not sell you

**Anything that is not an insect or a rodent.** Birds on a downtown parapet, an animal in a ceiling void, a bat in an attic — none of that is ours. Sasquatch does pest control and exclusion, and we would rather send you to the right operator on the first call than take a booking we cannot serve.

**Mole trapping.** The methods that actually work are restrictively regulated in
Washington, and most of what is sold to homeowners is theatre.

**A recurring program over an unresolved water problem.** On low-lying and
flood-affected property this is the most common way to be overcharged. Drying,
removal and drainage come first; quarterly service laid over a wet crawlspace is
an indefinite cost rather than a solution.

**Treatment for an inactive infestation.** Old galleries and old exit holes are
evidence of history, not of current activity.

## Multi-unit and rental housing

Mount Vernon has a substantial rental and apartment stock, and multi-unit work
follows different rules from a single house.

The governing fact is that in a building with shared structure the pests are a
building-level population rather than a unit-level one. Wall voids, plumbing
chases, shared attics and common utility runs connect units, and an animal or an
insect moves through those without regard to who pays which lease.

So treating one unit while the ones either side go untreated relocates the
problem, usually next door, and often back again a month later. Tenants
reasonably conclude the treatment failed. It did not fail; it was scoped wrong.

What works is a building plan: mapping which units are affected, identifying the
source, treating in a coordinated pass, and following up on an interval matched
to the life cycle rather than to the billing cycle. Landlords who resist that
frequently end up paying for the same problem three times.

Bed bugs and German cockroaches are the two that most reward this approach and
punish the alternative most severely. Both travel between units readily, and
both are far cheaper to resolve early and thoroughly than late and piecemeal.

A visit starts outside. Grade and drainage, the siding-to-foundation transition,

crawlspace, properly and with a light. Then the attic and the interior.
