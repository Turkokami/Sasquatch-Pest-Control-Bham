## A mill town, and what that left behind

Sedro-Woolley grew as a mill and railroad town, and the housing stock reflects
exactly that history. A dense core of worker housing built for people employed
within walking distance, laid out on small lots, much of it going up between
roughly 1900 and 1950, with a compact commercial downtown at the center and the
valley opening out around it.

That is a specific inheritance from our side of the work, and it makes this town
behave much more like Bellingham's prewar neighborhoods than like the newer
parts of Skagit County a few miles west.

The other two facts that matter are geographic. The Skagit runs along one side
of town, and the valley begins climbing toward the Cascades on the other. So a
single small town sits between a major river corridor and a forest edge, and
gets pressure off both.

## The housing, and how it fails

Older Sedro-Woolley housing shares a set of characteristics that were entirely
sensible when they were built and are a straightforward invitation now.

**Shallow crawlspaces over bare earth.** Very often no vapor barrier at all, or
one that was laid decades ago and has since been pushed aside by whoever last
ran a pipe. Bare soil puts ground moisture directly into the space and into the
joists above it, continuously, in a climate that supplies plenty of it.

**Post-and-pier foundations and early stem walls.** Frequently with skirting
rather than a continuous closed perimeter, and access hatches that no longer
seat properly.

**Foundation vents that failed long ago.** The original screen was light
material, it has rusted through at a corner, and it has been open for years. A
vent screen is one of the cheapest and highest-value things to replace properly
on a house of this era.

**Balloon or near-balloon framing.** Stud cavities that run from the sill plate
to the top plate without a firestop. Something entering at grade can be in the
attic without ever crossing an occupied room, which is why people hear
scratching upstairs and cannot understand how anything got there.

**A century of accumulated penetrations.** Gas, water, waste, electrical, three
generations of telephone and cable, a redundant oil line, an abandoned chimney
thimble. Each drilled generously by whoever was doing that job on that day, and
essentially none of them sealed.

The pests follow from that list without much mystery. Norway rats and house mice
in the crawl and the walls. Carpenter and moisture ants in framing that has
taken water. Sowbugs, millipedes, springtails, silverfish and Oriental
cockroaches in the damp space underneath, every one of which is reporting a
moisture condition rather than being a problem in its own right.

The correct work is structural. Seal the envelope, screen the vents with
material that will still be there in ten years, close the penetrations, and get
a proper barrier over clean ground. Treatment handles what is already inside.
Without the first part you are buying the same service again next autumn.

## Carpenter ants, the local signature

If there is one insect that defines older Sedro-Woolley property, this is it,
and the reason is simply age.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in it to nest, and they
strongly prefer wood that has already begun to soften. A house that has stood
for a hundred years in this climate has almost certainly had water into the
framing somewhere — a gutter that overflowed for a decade, a window flashing
that failed, a porch roof that leaked quietly, a sill plate close to grade.
Those areas are what a colony wants.

The part most treatment gets wrong is the colony structure. A mature population
here keeps a **parent nest outdoors** — in a stump, a fallen log, a fence post, a
landscape timber, a woodpile — with **satellite nests** inside the building. The
satellites are what people see: workers on a counter, coarse frass below a
window, a rustling in a wall at night.

Treat the satellite while the parent sits twenty feet away and you get a quiet
couple of months. That is the entire reason carpenter ant work has a reputation
for coming back, and it is why our first visit here is investigative before it
is anything else. We are looking for the parent: the stump nobody thinks about,
the section of deck framing that stays wet, the old timber retaining wall, the
woodpile against the garage.

Where we find it, the result holds. Where it sits on a neighboring property we
will say so plainly and plan around it rather than implying we have solved
something we have not.

Frass is the useful field diagnostic. Carpenter ants push shavings out, so the
debris below a gallery is fibrous and often contains insect body parts. That is
different from the hard pellets a dampwood termite produces and different again
from the fine powder below a wood-boring beetle exit hole, and the distinction
changes the treatment entirely.

## Moisture ants, and reading them correctly

Moisture ants get confused with carpenter ants constantly and the message they
carry is different.

They colonize wood that is already wet and already decaying. They are not
capable of establishing in sound dry lumber, so finding them is genuinely useful
information: the water is the disease and the ants are the symptom.

On this housing stock the source is usually on a short list. A gutter that has
been overflowing down a wall. A downspout discharging at the foundation. A
crawlspace with no working barrier and standing groundwater. Plumbing weeping
slowly inside a wall. Siding in contact with soil or bark mulch. Window flashing
that failed years ago. A porch or deck ledger flashed badly or not at all.

Kill the ants without correcting any of that and you get a new colony in the
same timber within a season or two. Which is why, on a good share of these
visits, the most valuable thing we do is point at a gutter and tell you the
person you need next is a roofer rather than us. That costs us work regularly
and it is the correct answer.

## Wood-boring beetles and decay

Damp crawlspaces in old housing produce two things people find alarming and
frequently misread.

**Anobiid beetles** are genuinely common in this region's older crawlspaces. The
signature is small round exit holes with fine powder below them, in joists,
subfloor and sill. The question that decides everything is whether the
infestation is active or historic, because damage persists for decades after the
insects have gone and exit holes in a joist are not by themselves evidence of
anything current. We would far rather establish that than treat a museum piece,
and if it is old and inactive we will tell you so even though it costs us the
job.

**Decay fungus** is not an insect and is frequently the more consequential
finding. Sustained moisture in framing produces decay that continues
indefinitely, and decay rather than insects is what actually costs structural
capacity in this climate. Where we find it, the honest answer often involves a
contractor, and the pest treatment is the small part of the project.

Both trace back to the same condition, which is why we spend so much of a visit
here on water rather than on insects.

## Downtown and the connected block

Sedro-Woolley has a compact commercial core of older buildings, many sharing
walls.

The consequence is one that surprises tenants: those buildings are connected.
Party walls, common basements, utility chases and service runs pass from one
into the next, along with drainage that has been extended repeatedly as tenants
changed. A rodent population in that block is a single population using the
whole structure, and it has no interest in the property lines.

So treating one unit while the buildings either side do nothing produces a
temporary result. The animals move, wait, and return. Businesses can spend years
paying for monthly service against a population that was never theirs alone.

Where a building owner or several tenants will work to one plan, the result is
different in kind rather than incrementally better. That is a harder
conversation than selling one contract, and it is the honest one.

Old commercial buildings also carry old openings — an abandoned pipe run, a
filled coal chute, a service entry from a utility that no longer exists, a void
above a suspended ceiling nobody has looked into in decades. Finding those is
most of the work, and it is why a proper first visit downtown takes longer than
people expect.

## The river corridor

The Skagit runs along the town and the dike and drainage system that manages it
runs with it.

A dike, from our point of view, is a linear habitat corridor with excellent
cover, running for miles, immediately adjacent to buildings. Sloughs, drainage
ditches and the vegetated margins between fields do the same on a smaller scale.
The result is continuous connectivity: rodents move the length of the valley
without ever crossing open ground.

You cannot treat a corridor and it would be wrong to try. What you can do is
defend the specific building, and accept that the pressure against it is
constant rather than seasonal.

The low ground also saturates first. When burrows flood, everything living in
them moves toward buildings, and river-adjacent property here sees that two or
three weeks before the higher ground does.

Any building that has taken water needs the sequence respected: wet insulation
out rather than treated around, the space dried properly, the barrier
re-established over clean ground. Only then does exclusion hold. Treating a wet
insulated crawl produces a building that is still damp, still attractive and
still deteriorating, with an invoice attached.

## The eastern edge and the forest

East of town the valley climbs and the character changes to forest edge, and a
good share of the housing out that way is cedar against standing timber.

That brings its own set: carpenter ants in softened cedar trim and fascia,
rodents pushing in off the treeline through crawl vents and skirting, and the
heaviest spider and centipede pressure in the area, all of it driven by abundant
insect production in damp wooded ground.

The fix is at the interface rather than in the woods. Trim the branch bridging
to the roof. Screen the vent properly. Close the gap where a deck meets the
house. Move the woodpile off the wall. Correct where landscaping runs straight
up against the siding. Trapping alone against a treeline is a subscription
rather than a result, and we would rather say that at the first visit than at
the fourth.

Our routes do thin out as you go further east up the valley. For an address well
beyond town, call and ask rather than assuming — we will tell you honestly
whether we run there.

## Firewood, and why it matters here

Wood heat is common in this part of the county and firewood storage is one of
the most reliably overlooked pest factors on a property.

A stack against the house wall is harborage directly against the structure. It
holds moisture, it shelters rodents, it is exactly the sort of decaying timber
carpenter ants nest in, and it brings spiders, beetles and other insects to
within inches of the siding. Wood carried inside brings whatever is in it into a
warm room, which is where the alarming-looking spider on the hearth in January
comes from.

The advice is simple and free. Stack it off the ground, away from the building,
and bring in only what you will burn that day. That single change resolves more
problems on a rural property than most of what we could sell you.

## The year here

**Winter.** Rodent activity indoors at its height, and the season when the
autumn's damage becomes obvious: soiled insulation, chewed ducting, a stain in a
ceiling, a smell that turns out to be something dead in a cavity. Firewood
brings the occasional startling spider indoors.

**Late winter.** Overwintering insects wake on the first mild afternoon and
appear at south-facing windows.

**Spring.** Carpenter and moisture ants become active, and because they have
wintered inside damp framing the first sighting is usually indoors rather than
out. Swarmers appear on windowsills and get mistaken for termites. Wasp queens
found nests, which is the cheapest possible moment to deal with them.

**Summer.** Wasp season, and the correct window for everything structural: dry
ground, dry crawlspaces, easy access to the exterior. Anything preventive should
happen now.

**Autumn.** The decisive stretch. The rain returns, the low ground saturates,
and the population outside moves toward buildings within a few weeks. Work done
before this prevents the winter problem entirely; work deferred becomes
remediation.

## What we will not sell you

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Birds, bats, squirrels, raccoons and other
wildlife are outside what we do. We handle insects and rodents, and we will tell
you which you have and who to call for the rest.

**Mole trapping.** The methods that actually work are restrictively regulated in
Washington and most of what is sold to homeowners does not work.

**A recurring program over a wet crawlspace.** On this housing stock that is the
easiest way to be overcharged. Drying, removal and drainage come first.

**Treatment for a beetle infestation with no evidence of current activity.** Old
exit holes are history rather than a problem.

## Spiders, and the reassuring answer

Every autumn the calls start about large fast spiders indoors, and in a town
with this much old housing and this much wooded ground nearby the load is
genuinely heavy.

They are almost always giant house spiders. Harmless, long established
throughout western Washington, and the large ones people find are wandering
males looking for mates rather than an infestation responding to cold. The ones
stranded in a bathtub are males that got in and could not climb back out.

There is no brown recluse in this corner of the state and there never has been.
Black widows exist in Washington but belong to the drier country east and south
of the Cascades. Anyone telling you otherwise while holding a quote is selling
fear rather than a service, and on old housing there is usually plenty of real
work to do without inventing any.

What actually reduces the population is unglamorous: clearing exterior webbing,
dealing with harborage under porches and in crawl vents, moving the firewood off
the wall, correcting exterior lighting that concentrates the insects they feed
on, and sealing the door sweeps and thresholds most of them use to get in.
