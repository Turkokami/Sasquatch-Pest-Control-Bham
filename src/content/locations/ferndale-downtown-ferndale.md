## The oldest part of the city

Downtown Ferndale is the original town — a compact commercial core with the
oldest residential stock in the city around and behind it, on low ground beside
the Nooksack.

Nearly all of the pest work here comes from two facts that have nothing to do
with pests. The buildings are old, and they are close to the river.

Everything the newer parts of Ferndale get — the subdivisions on higher ground,
the townhome developments, the estates on the edges — is a different job with
different failure points. A technician who has spent the morning in Pacific
Highlands and comes downtown in the afternoon is looking for entirely different
things.

## What a century of building does to a house

Age by itself is not a verdict. Plenty of the best-maintained buildings we work
on downtown are the oldest ones. What age changes is the number of openings and
how long it takes to find them all.

A house here has typically been through several generations of services. Knob
and tube, then rewiring. A coal or wood system, then oil, then gas or electric.
Galvanized supply, then copper, then possibly plastic. Waste lines replaced in
sections. A phone line, then cable, then internet. A dryer vent cut through a
wall in the seventies. An added bathroom, a kitchen moved, a porch enclosed, a
window replaced with something a different size.

Every one of those was cut into the building by somebody focused on making the
new thing work rather than on closing up behind it. Most were never sealed.
Some were sealed with something that has since perished, and a few were stuffed
with material that a rodent regards as an invitation rather than a barrier.

The result is a building with a great many small routes rather than one obvious
one, and a survey that finds three of them has not finished.

## Post-and-pier, and the space underneath

A large share of the older housing here sits on post-and-pier foundations —
individual piers carrying beams, with a skirt of some kind closing the
perimeter — rather than on a continuous foundation wall.

That construction has two consequences that matter enormously.

The first is that the perimeter is not structural. A skirt is a screen, and it
is built and maintained accordingly: thin boards, lattice, sheet material,
sometimes a partial course of blocks. It rots at the base where it meets the
ground, it gets damaged by mowers and vehicles, and it gets opened up by
tradespeople who never close it again.

The second is that the space underneath is generous. Unlike a shallow modern
crawl, an old post-and-pier under-floor space is often tall enough to move
around in comfortably. That is convenient for us and equally convenient for
everything else.

The good news is that this is very fixable. Repairing the skirt properly,
screening it with quarter-inch hardware cloth fixed rather than stapled, fitting
sound vent units and closing the penetrations is a day's work on most of these
buildings, and it ends routes that have been open for thirty years.

## The river corridor at the back of it

The Nooksack runs past this part of town, and the vegetated corridor along it
is one of the best rodent habitats in the county: continuous cover, food, water
and shelter, running for miles, with almost no disturbance.

It is worth separating the source from the route, because only one of them can
be changed. The river is not going to stop being a river, and the corridor is
not going anywhere. What can be changed is whether the animals living in it can
get into your building, and that is always an ordinary gap.

The seasonal pattern is worth knowing too. High water displaces whatever lives
in the bank, and the animals move to dry ground, which is where the buildings
are. A property with no activity in the summer can have a genuine problem
within days of the river coming up, and it has nothing to do with how the place
is kept.

## Moisture, and the ants that report it

Low ground near a river means a water table closer to the surface than it is on
the ridges, and an old under-floor space over bare soil will hold moisture
accordingly.

That matters because of what damp wood permits. Carpenter ants excavate
galleries far more readily in softened timber than in sound timber. Wood-boring
beetles and decay fungi need moisture content that dry framing never reaches.

And moisture ants — which most people have never heard of and which we find
constantly in this stock — are not really a pest problem at all. They nest in
wood that is already wet and decaying. Finding them is useful information: it
means there is a genuine moisture problem in that timber, and the ants are the
symptom rather than the cause. Treating them and leaving the wet wood is
treating a smoke alarm.

Getting a vapor barrier onto the soil and sorting out the obvious drainage is
the highest-value work available on most of these houses, and a fair proportion
of our downtown jobs involve no product at all.

## The commercial core and the buildings that do two jobs

The center of town has attached commercial buildings, several of them with
residential space above or behind, and those work differently from a detached
house on the next street.

Behind the finished surfaces, an attached building is largely continuous — wall
cavities that run past party walls, chases that pass vertically between floors,
ceiling voids that span a whole floor plate, roof spaces that run the length of
a terrace. A population living in that shared volume treats an individual unit
as one room of a larger territory.

That is why a treatment in one unit can work and then stop working. It cleared
what was inside; the source was never inside. The occupant reasonably concludes
the treatment failed, and it did not — it was scoped to a unit when the problem
was scoped to a building.

Where there is food service on the ground floor and residences above, the
routes connecting them are almost always open: risers, extract runs, and the
penetrations cut for gas, water, waste and power. Sealing those protects
everyone in the building at once, and it is the cheapest thing available.

## Rodents, and the ways in that actually get used

The openings downtown are ordinary and they are consistent.

At ground level: damaged skirting, a crawl hatch that is a board leaning
against a hole, corroded vent screens, siding that stops short of the
foundation, and the gap where a porch or a deck was built up against the house
without screening underneath.

Higher up: where a roof meets a wall on a building that has settled, gable and
attic vents with no screen behind them, and the point where a chimney passes
the roofline on an old masonry stack.

And the one people never expect on older stock, which is the drainage. Disused
or broken drain runs under and beside old buildings are a genuine route into a
structure, and a rat problem that resists everything sensible at ground level is
occasionally coming up rather than in.

## Chimneys, and what lives in a disused one

Older housing here still carries masonry chimneys, and a good number of them
have been out of service for years since the heating was changed.

A disused chimney is an open shaft from the roof into the building, and unless
it was properly capped when it was decommissioned it is exactly that to
anything that can climb or fly. Birds nest in them. Wasps nest in them. Rats
and squirrels use them as a route into a roof space, and bats occasionally
roost in them, which is a legally different matter that we will identify rather
than treat.

The pest consequence is often indirect, which is what confuses people. An old
bird nest in a flue holds mites and carpet beetles, and when the nest is
abandoned those move inward, turning up in a bedroom weeks later with no
apparent connection to anything.

Capping a decommissioned flue properly, with a cowl that still lets the stack
breathe, closes all of that permanently. It is a roofer's job rather than ours,
and it is one we recommend regularly.

## Basements, part-basements and the ground beneath

Some of the older houses here have a basement or a partial basement rather than
a crawlspace, and they behave differently enough to be worth separating.

A basement on low ground near a river is a below-grade room in wet soil.
Moisture pushes through masonry under pressure, older walls are rarely tanked
to any modern standard, and drainage that was adequate in 1950 is frequently
not adequate now that the ground outside has been landscaped and built up.

What that produces is a permanently humid space, usually with stored material
in it, at the bottom of the building. Silverfish and firebrats do well there.
Spiders do well on what they attract. Centipedes and pillbugs come in from the
soil. And any wood in contact with a damp wall, from a stair stringer to an old
workbench, is candidate material for wood-boring insects.

Pest work in a wet basement is nearly always second to the moisture work, and
we will say so.

## Spiders, and the fall wandering season

Old houses with generous under-floor spaces and a lot of ground-level openings
produce more spiders indoors than new houses do, and downtown gets a noticeable
wave of them in the autumn.

Most of what people find is giant house spiders, which look alarming and are
not. The fall activity is males moving to find females rather than an
infestation arriving, which is why they turn up singly, at speed, in the middle
of a floor, in September and October.

Reducing them is largely a matter of reducing what they eat and where they get
in — the same exclusion work that handles everything else, plus attention to
exterior lighting, which concentrates the insects they are hunting.

## Wasps on an old building

Old buildings offer more aerial nesting opportunities than new ones: open
soffits, gaps at the roofline, disused chimneys, porch roofs, and the voids in
outbuildings that come with older lots.

The complication downtown is access and the public. A nest in the fascia above a
shop entrance or a shared walkway is a hazard to people who never chose to be
near it, and dealing with it needs equipment and a time when there is nobody
underneath.

Honey bees are a separate matter and we do not treat them. An old chimney or
wall cavity with a colony in it needs somebody who removes them properly, and
we will point you at one.

## Storage, outbuildings and the back of the lot

Older lots downtown often carry a shed, a detached garage, or an outbuilding
that predates the house's last three owners, and these are consistently where a
population is actually based.

They are closer to cover, sealed to a much lower standard, and full of exactly
the soft, undisturbed material that makes good nesting. A house forty feet from
a full outbuilding is being supplied continuously, and sealing the house alone
concentrates the problem rather than reducing it.

Firewood against a wall does the same thing on a smaller scale, and nearly
every old house here has some.

## Bed bugs in older rentals

A share of the older housing downtown is rented, sometimes divided into units,
and bed bugs follow that pattern rather than any question of how a home is
kept.

They arrive in luggage and on secondhand furniture. In a divided building they
move between units along shared voids and service runs when pressure rises in
one of them. So a single treated unit in an untreated building gets reinfested,
and a single untreated unit undoes work everywhere else.

The obstacle is usually reporting rather than treatment. A tenant who expects to
be blamed waits, and by the time it is raised the neighbors have it. A building
where reporting early is treated as helpful costs nothing and saves a great
deal.

We charge to verify bed bugs and credit it against treatment, and on a divided
building verification is worth having on its own — it establishes what is
actually there before several units get treated on an assumption.

## What is worth doing first

On this housing stock the ranking is unusually clear.

Vapor barrier and drainage first, because the moisture is what several other
problems depend on. Then the perimeter — skirting, vents, hatch, penetrations —
because that ends the routes rather than the individuals using them. Then the
outbuildings, because that is where the supply is. Product, where it is needed
at all, comes after those three rather than instead of them.

A recurring treatment on an old house with an open skirt and a wet crawlspace
will produce a visible result every time it is applied and will never finish.
We would rather quote the day of hardware.

## Alleys, back lots and the ground behind the buildings

The older grid downtown has back lanes, service accesses and awkward strips of
ground behind buildings, and collectively they are the most productive place to
look on this side of town.

They are out of sight, so they collect. Stored material, waste, disused
equipment, timber from a project, and vegetation nobody manages because nobody
is quite sure whose it is. They run continuously behind whole blocks, connecting
properties that never share a boundary at the front.

For a rodent population that is a corridor with harborage every few yards, and
it is why activity downtown tends to appear along a run of addresses rather
than at one.

Almost none of that requires a technician to fix. Clearing a back lot, getting
stored material up off the ground, and making sure waste is contained does more
than anything applied to a wall — and where several neighbors do it at once, it
removes the corridor rather than moving it along.

## How a visit works downtown

We start with the perimeter and the ground: skirting, vents, hatch, drainage,
what is stored against the walls, and how close the cover gets.

Then underneath, properly, with a light, through the whole under-floor space.
On post-and-pier that is a real inspection rather than a look from the opening,
and it is where most of the answers are.

Then the interior and the roof space, and in an attached or mixed-use building
the shared routes — risers, chases and ceiling voids — because that is what
turns one unit's problem into a building's problem.

You get a plain account of what is active, what is old, what is driving it,
which items are building work rather than pest work, and what belongs to a
landlord or a neighbor rather than to you. The survey costs nothing. Bed bugs are the single exception, billed for verification and credited back against treatment, and no contract is involved at any point.
