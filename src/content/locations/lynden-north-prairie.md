## Working ground, not a neighborhood

North Prairie is farm country. It runs along North Prairie Road into the
agricultural ground north and east of Lynden, and what sits on it is dairies,
berry operations, hay ground, equipment yards and the houses that belong to
them.

That makes it different from every other page on this site about a Lynden
address. This is not residential pest control with a rural view. It is
agricultural pest work, and the priorities, the products, the regulations and
the standard of success are all different.

The single most useful thing to say at the start is that a farm cannot be
treated like a house and should not be sold like one. A house can be sealed. A
barn cannot. What can be done on farm ground is to protect the buildings that
matter, manage the material that produces the problem, and be honest about the
rest.

## Two kinds of building, and only one can be closed

Every operation out here has both, and separating them is the first thing we
do.

**The buildings that can be closed** are the feed room, the milk house, the
shop, the pump house, the office, the grain or supplement store, and the
house. These have doors, walls and a reason to be sealed, and exclusion work on
them is worth doing properly because it holds.

**The buildings that cannot** are the open barn, the loafing shed, the hay
store, the equipment shelter and the machinery bay. They are open by design,
they have to be, and no amount of money will make them rodent-proof. Treating
them as sealable is how people end up paying for work that never had a chance.

Once that split is made, a plan gets much simpler. Concentrate the exclusion
where it holds. Manage the open structures as habitat rather than as buildings
— placements, monitoring, and attention to what is stored in them. Accept that
the open buildings will always carry some pressure and make sure the closed
ones do not.

## The feed room is the whole game

If there is one place on a dairy where money spent returns most, it is the feed
room, and it is regularly the least defended building on the property.

Concentrate, supplement, grain and mineral in one warm dry space is the best
food source for miles. It is usually accessible, frequently propped open
during working hours, and often has a door that has not closed properly since
before the current herd.

Three things change it, and none is exotic. A door that actually shuts, with a
sweep that meets the threshold. Feed in closed bins rather than open sacks or
spilled on the floor. And a genuine gap kept around the walls so the perimeter
can be seen — a feed room stacked to the walls cannot be inspected by anybody,
including us.

Spillage is the part people underestimate. A few pounds a day swept up rather
than left is the difference between a room that supports a population and one
that does not.

## Silage, bedding and the material that breeds flies

Fly control on a dairy is a material-handling problem before it is a treatment
problem, and any company that starts with adult flies has started in the wrong
place.

House flies and stable flies breed in moist organic material: manure packs,
soiled bedding, silage face spoilage, feed waste at the bunk, and the wet
margins around waterers and drains. Development starts when the material warms
in spring, well ahead of anybody noticing adults, and each generation
compounds.

By the time flies are obvious in a parlor, there are several generations of
larvae behind them in material nobody has moved. Fogging the adults reduces
what is visible for a day or two and does nothing whatever to the supply.

What works is boring and it is genuinely effective. Keeping breeding material
dry, moving it before it warms through, cleaning up the wet edges around
waterers and along bunk lines, managing the silage face so spoilage does not
sit, and using larvicide where the material genuinely cannot be moved. Adult
control has a place, and its place is on top of that rather than instead of it.

The reason to care beyond comfort is production. Stable flies in particular
affect animal behavior and intake, so this is a farm-performance question
rather than a nuisance one.

## Rats and mice, and why the distinction matters here

Most residential rodent work in this county is house mice. On farm ground it is
routinely both species, and the difference decides the approach.

**Mice** occupy small spaces, breed quickly and range very little. A mouse
population is usually local to one building and one food source, and it grows
fast once established.

**Rats** range considerably further, are markedly cautious about anything new
in a familiar run, and cause a different order of damage. Chewed wiring in a
shop or a pump house. Insulation pulled out of a wall. Damage to a control
panel. Gnawing that has gone through something structural rather than
cosmetic.

That caution is the practical difference. A rat will avoid an unfamiliar object
in its run for days, which means a placement strategy that works immediately on
mice reads as a total failure on rats for the first week. Knowing which animal
is present before anything is set saves a fortnight and a good deal of
frustration.

We work it out from the droppings, the size and character of the gnawing, the
runs, the smell and the damage pattern, before anything goes down.

## Where product can and cannot go

A working farm has more restrictions on it than any house does, and they are
label restrictions rather than preferences.

A pesticide label is a legal document and it is an offense under federal law to
use a product in a manner inconsistent with it. On agricultural premises the
labels are specific about food and feed areas, about animal contact, and about
proximity to feed, water and milk handling. A product cleared for a shop
perimeter is frequently not cleared anywhere near a feed bunk or a milk house.

We work from the label, we will show you the label, and every product this
company carries is published on this site with its EPA registration number, its
full label and its safety data sheet. If somebody proposes to apply something
in your feed room, ask them for that document before they open the container.

Where the honest answer is that nothing may be applied in a given area, we will
say so and reach for exclusion, trapping and material management instead.

## Rodenticides, raptors and farm cats

Three of the products this company carries are rodenticides, and on farm ground
they carry a risk that the insect products do not.

An animal that has eaten bait can be eaten by something else. That is how a
barn owl, a hawk or a farm cat ends up poisoned by a product nobody applied
anywhere near it, and on a property that actively benefits from having raptors
around, it is a real cost rather than a theoretical one.

The risk is managed by placement rather than by choice of bait. Exterior
placements go in tamper-resistant stations, secured, on a documented map of the
property. Interior work in a building that people and animals use leans on
trapping wherever traps will do the job, because a rodent that dies in a wall
void is a problem in its own right.

And the reason exclusion is the larger half of this business is that sealing a
building is the only rodent work that does not have to be repeated.

## Harvest, cutting and the week everything moves

The most predictable event on this ground is a sudden influx after the cover
goes.

A field that has supported a large outdoor population all summer is cut,
worked or harvested over a few days. Several hundred acres of shelter becomes
bare ground within a week, and every animal living in it has to be somewhere
else immediately.

The nearest buildings with a gap are where a good share of it goes. That is why
a property with no visible problem in July has a genuine one by late August,
weeks before there is any cold weather to blame.

Because it is event-driven, it is predictable, and that is the useful part. The
closing-up work on the feed room, the shop and the house is worth doing before
the ground goes rather than after the animals are already inside.

## Equipment sheds and what winters in machinery

A machine standing still for the winter is a building as far as a rodent is
concerned, and it is a better one than most.

Combines, balers, tractors, sprayers and trailers hold insulation, wiring
looms, upholstery and enclosed cavities, sitting undisturbed for months in a
sheltered structure surrounded by cover. Nesting in a cab or an engine bay is
routine, and the damage found at start-up in spring is regularly to a wiring
harness, which is an expensive and badly timed discovery.

Ten minutes per machine before lay-up — removing anything edible, opening cabs,
checking enclosed spaces — plus stations around the shed rather than in the
machines is most of the answer.

## Grain, supplement and stored-product insects

Where there is stored grain or supplement there are stored-product insects, and
they are a different problem from rodents with a different solution.

Sawtoothed grain beetles, weevils and Indianmeal moths live in the product
itself, and they frequently arrive in it rather than finding it. Temperature
and moisture in storage decide whether a small population becomes a large one.

The controls are storage hygiene, rotation, cleaning between loads — residue in
the corners of a bin is where the next infestation starts — and monitoring so a
problem is caught while it is still small. Fumigation is somebody else's trade
and we will say so rather than improvise.

## Wasps around livestock and equipment

Yellowjackets are a late-summer problem out here and the ground nests are the
ones that matter.

They get found by machine rather than by eye — a mower, a rake, a tractor
working a headland — and on ground where people are working around animals,
being stung is a hazard beyond the sting itself. Knowing where the activity is
before August is worth having, and it costs nothing to have somebody look while
they are on the property.

Aerial nests in barn eaves, equipment sheds and around doorways are more
visible and generally easier. Bald-faced hornets build the large gray paper
nests people photograph; European paper wasps build small open combs under
rails and are markedly less defensive.

Honey bees are not our work. On a farm with berry ground, pollinators are worth
more than we are, and if there is a colony in a structure we will identify it
and point you at somebody who removes them properly.

## Berry ground, and the boundary between our work and an agronomist's

Berry operations here have their own pest programs and their own advisers, and
we are careful about the line.

Field pests — spotted wing drosophila and the rest of what affects the crop —
are an agronomic matter with its own products, timings and regulations, and
that is not this company's trade. What we handle is the structures: the coolers,
the pack area, the storage, the shop, the office and the housing.

Where the two meet is worth attention, because a pack house or a cooler is a
food premises with everything that implies, and fruit residue plus warmth plus
shelter produces both flies and rodents very quickly.

If somebody offers you both field and structural work, ask which licenses and
which labels are being used for which.

## Birds in farm buildings

Open agricultural buildings attract nesting birds, and on a working farm the
consequences go beyond mess.

Starlings and swallows both use barns, and the difference matters. Swallows are
protected and are largely beneficial, taking insects around the buildings; the
answer with them is patience and, where it is genuinely necessary, exclusion
outside the nesting season rather than anything else. Starlings in numbers are
a different matter — they consume and foul stored feed, and fouling in a feed
area is a contamination issue rather than an aesthetic one.

What we can usefully address is the harborage and the openings: netting or
screening on the parts of a building that can be closed, and attention to where
nesting material accumulates in gutters, ledges and equipment.

The indirect consequence is the one that surprises people. Old nests hold mites
and beetles, and when a nest is abandoned those move outward and inward — into
a feed room, an office or the house — and get diagnosed as something else
entirely because nobody connects them to a nest in a rafter twenty feet up.

## The house on the farm

The farmhouse gets the same problems as any rural house and one extra: it sits
at the center of the whole operation.

Everything above — the feed room, the sheds, the machinery, the cut ground, the
open barns — is generating pressure a short distance away, and the house takes
the overflow. Which means that sealing the house is worth doing and will never
be sufficient on its own.

The order that works is the buildings first, the house alongside. Vent screens,
sweeps, penetrations, crawl hatch and garage door corners on the house; feed
room, shop and pump house closed properly; open structures managed rather than
sealed.

## What we would rather not sell you

We will not put a farm on a treatment schedule that leaves the feed room door
broken and the spillage on the floor. That is selling a recurring problem back
to the person who has it.

We will not fog for adult flies while the breeding material sits where it is.
It looks like action, it costs money every visit, and it changes nothing.

And we will not tell anybody that an open barn can be rodent-proofed. It cannot,
and a quote that implies it can is either a misunderstanding or worse.
