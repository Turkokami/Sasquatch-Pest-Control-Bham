## Where the city runs out

East Blaine is the part of town that stops being town. Established residential
streets run east from the older core, and within a few blocks the lots get
bigger, the gaps between houses widen, outbuildings appear, and then it is open
county.

That transition is short — a matter of blocks rather than miles — and it is the
whole story of the pest work here. The housing is ordinary suburban housing.
The pressure arriving at it is agricultural and forested. Those two things do
not usually sit this close together.

The practical effect is that two houses six streets apart can need genuinely
different jobs, and neither the address nor the city limit tells you which is
which. What tells you is how much cover is within a couple of hundred yards of
the back door.

## Pests do not know where the city ends

It is worth stating directly because it comes up on nearly every first visit
out here: a municipal boundary means nothing to a mouse.

People inside the city limits are frequently surprised to be dealing with field
rodents, and they should not be. A house that is three blocks from a cut field
is three blocks from a cut field. The line on the map does not sit between them
and neither does anything else.

What actually varies across East Blaine is the distance to cover and the amount
of it. On the western streets, backing onto other houses, the pressure is
ordinary residential pressure — a neighbor's woodpile, an untended hedge, a
compost heap. On the eastern edge, backing onto open ground or a treeline, it
is the full rural pattern arriving at a suburban building that was never
detailed for it.

We read that off the property rather than off the street name.

## The late-summer arrival

The most reliable event in the East Blaine calendar is a sudden rodent problem
in late summer or early autumn, and its cause is misunderstood almost
universally.

People assume cold weather drives animals indoors. Cold is a factor, but it is
rarely the trigger, and the timing gives it away: the arrivals usually start
weeks before there is any cold to speak of.

What actually happens is that cover disappears. A field that has supported a
substantial population all summer is cut or worked over the course of a few
days. Several hundred acres of shelter becomes bare ground in a week. Every
animal living in it has to be somewhere else immediately, and the nearest
structure with a gap in it is where a good number of them go.

Because it is driven by an event rather than a season, it arrives suddenly and
in numbers. That is why a property with no activity at all in July has a
genuine problem by the middle of August, and why the householder is convinced
something must have changed about their house. Nothing changed about the house.
Something changed about the field.

The useful consequence is that this is predictable. If the ground behind you is
worked, the work to close the building should be done before it is worked
rather than after the animals are already in.

## Suburban buildings meeting rural pressure

The buildings here were, for the most part, detailed as town houses — and there
is nothing wrong with that until the pressure on them stops being town
pressure.

The openings we find are the standard residential ones, and there is nothing
exotic about any of them. Crawlspace vents with screening that has corroded
from the ground side. Service penetrations for water, waste, power and dryer
vents, drilled oversize and never closed. The bottom corners of a garage door,
which show daylight from inside if you stand in there with the door shut. A
crawl hatch that is a board leaning against a hole. The gap where siding stops
short of the foundation.

None of that matters much on an interior lot in a subdivision. All of it
matters a great deal when there are field rodents arriving in numbers a hundred
yards away.

The work is unglamorous and it is permanent. Quarter-inch hardware cloth
correctly fixed rather than stapled over, proper vent units rather than
patched screening, sweeps that actually meet the ground, and penetrations
closed with something that will not be chewed through. That is a morning's work
per building and it ends the route rather than the individuals using it.

## Outbuildings, and the order things fill up in

Once lots get to a certain size out here, there is a shed, a shop, a barn, a
carport or all four, and they change the shape of the job.

Outbuildings sit closer to cover and are built and sealed to a lower standard
than the house. They fill first, every time. And a full shop forty feet from a
house is a permanent supply — the house is not being colonized from the field,
it is being colonized from the shop.

That is why sealing a house on a property whose outbuildings are open does not
work as expected. It does not remove a population. It concentrates it, and
concentrates it right next to the building you care about.

On a property with several buildings the honest unit of the job is the site.
Where somebody only wants the house done, we will do it and we will say plainly
what it will achieve and what it will not, so that the result is not a surprise
in six weeks.

## The treeline and the carpenter ants

Where East Blaine backs onto standing timber and mature hedgerow, carpenter
ants become a spring and summer story in their own right.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in it to nest, and
their natural habitat is stumps, fallen logs, standing deadwood and the soft
punky wood at the base of living trees. A treeline behind a property supports a
number of colonies that have nothing at all to do with the house.

The way they reach a building is the part most people have never been told. A
mature colony maintains a parent nest outdoors and then pushes satellite
colonies out from it. Satellites contain workers and older larvae but not the
queen, and a wall void, a rim joist or the timber around a skylight suits one
as well as a log does.

That is why a house on the edge of cover gets carpenter ants when a house six
streets west does not. The colony is already there. It only has to move part of
itself indoors.

The signs come in a sequence. Large black ants indoors in March or April, one
or two at a time, usually near a window or a sink — foraging, and an indication
that something nearby is active. Winged ants emerging indoors later, which mean
a mature reproducing colony and, if they are coming out inside rather than
flying in, a colony in the structure. And frass, the sawdust-like material
pushed out of galleries, which means an active nest directly above wherever it
is landing.

Finding the parent colony is the work. Killing the individuals crossing a
kitchen floor is not.

## Firewood, and the thing everyone does

Nearly every property out here stores firewood, and nearly all of it is stored
against the house because that is convenient in February.

Stacked wood against a wall is a covered, dry, undisturbed harborage directly
against a building, and it holds carpenter ants, spiders, beetles and rodents
in whatever proportion the season provides. Bringing an armful inside brings
whatever is in it inside.

The fix is small and nobody wants to hear it: get the stack off the wall and off
the ground, keep it away from the building, and bring in what you will burn
rather than a week's supply to sit by the stove. That change alone accounts for
a measurable share of the winter callouts on this side of town.

## Voles, moles and the things that ruin the ground

On the larger lots out here two animals get blamed for each other constantly,
and the confusion costs people a season.

**Voles** work at the surface. They cut shallow runways through long grass and
ground cover, leave small clean openings, and eat plant material — roots,
bulbs, and the bark at the base of young trees and shrubs over winter. The
damage that matters is the last of those: a ring of bark stripped at ground
level will kill a young fruit tree outright, and it happens under snow or long
grass where nobody sees it until spring.

**Moles** work below and eat almost no plant material at all. They tunnel after
worms and larvae and push spoil up in conical heaps. The damage is to the
surface of a lawn and to anything whose roots get disturbed, not to the plants
themselves.

The tell is in the workings. Winding surface trails with clipped vegetation,
appearing when long grass is cut short, means voles. Mounds of pushed-up soil
and a lawn that lifts underfoot means moles. A property can have both, and
frequently does.

Neither of them is much of a building problem — voles occasionally get into a
crawlspace or a garage, moles essentially never — which is worth saying because
the alarm about them is usually out of proportion to what they actually do. On
a place with fruit trees or ornamental planting the vole damage is real and
worth acting on; the mole hills are a groundskeeping annoyance.

What actually reduces vole pressure is removing the cover they depend on:
keeping grass short around the base of young trees, pulling mulch back from
trunks rather than mounding it against them, and guarding the stems of anything
newly planted. That is work anybody can do in an afternoon and it is more
effective than anything we would apply.

## Wells, septic systems and where things can go

As lots get larger toward the county, properties move onto wells and septic
systems, and that shapes placement rather than limiting what can be achieved.

Nothing goes near a wellhead. Nothing goes over a drain field. Where work is
needed close to either, we will say so before we do it and explain what is being
used and why. Out here that is a normal part of the conversation rather than an
obstacle, and a company that does not raise it is not paying attention.

## Wasps on a larger lot

Yellowjackets here are a late-summer problem and the dangerous ones are in the
ground.

Ground nests get found by machine — a mower, a strimmer, a tractor working a
boundary — rather than by eye, and that is how people end up needing a hospital.
On a larger lot with rough edges and banks, knowing where the activity is before
August is genuinely worth something, and it costs nothing to have somebody look
while they are already on the property.

Aerial nests in eaves, sheds and outbuildings are more visible and less
dangerous, though a nest in a shop doorway is still a problem. Bald-faced
hornets build the large gray paper nests people photograph; European paper
wasps build small open combs under railings and are far less inclined to defend
them.

Honey bees are not our work. If a colony is in a wall we will say so and point
you at somebody who removes them properly.

## The autumn crowd on the sunny wall

From late September onward the eastern streets get a set of visitors that
belongs specifically to the rural edge, and they turn up together on the first
genuinely cold afternoons.

Multicolored Asian lady beetles gather on sun-warmed siding, working whichever
elevation gets the low afternoon light, and then push into gaps around trim,
siding laps, window frames and attic vents. Brown marmorated stink bugs do
much the same thing on much the same walls. Cluster flies accumulate in upstairs
rooms and window channels in numbers that seem out of all proportion to any
opening anybody can find. Spiders come in for reasons of their own, and this is
when people notice them.

The reason this is worse on the edge of open ground than three streets west is
supply. All of these overwinter in numbers determined by the surrounding
landscape rather than by the building, and a house with fields, rough margins
and a treeline behind it simply has more of them arriving at it.

None of them bite. None of them breed indoors. None of them damage the
structure. That is not a reason to dismiss the problem — a bedroom ceiling
covered in beetles is genuinely miserable, and a stink bug crushed underfoot
announces itself for an hour — but it does decide what a sensible response looks
like.

Because they do not reproduce inside, the number indoors is entirely a function
of how many got in. Spraying indoors repeatedly through the winter kills what
is already present and does nothing at all about the supply. Sealing the
openings and treating the exterior at the right moment in early autumn
addresses the supply. That difference is the whole gap between a house that has
this every single year and one that does not.

Timing is the part that is easy to get wrong. Exterior work done too early has
faded before the wave arrives; done after they are already on the wall, most of
the benefit has gone. It is a narrow window and it moves with the weather,
which is why it is worth booking rather than reacting.

## What is worth paying for and what is not

The most valuable work out here is physical and it is finished when it is
finished: vents, sweeps, penetrations, hardware cloth, and the outbuildings done
at the same time as the house.

The least valuable is a recurring perimeter treatment on a property whose real
problem is a quarter-inch gap and an open shop. It will produce a visible
result, it will need repeating forever, and it will not close the route.

Where the answer to a rodent problem is a morning with a drill rather than a
schedule of visits, we will say so, and we will quote the morning.

## How a visit works here

We start with the boundary and the cover — what is behind the property, how
close it is, whether it gets worked, and what is stored between it and the
building. That sets the expectation for everything else.

Then the outbuildings, because on a lot with any of them that is usually where
the population actually is.

Then the house at ground level: foundation line, vents, sweeps, penetrations,
garage door corners, and where planting or stored material meets siding. Then
underneath, with a light, through the whole crawlspace rather than a look from
the hatch.

You get a plain account of what is active, what is old, what is driving it,
what should be done before the ground behind you is next cut, and which items
are building work rather than pest work. Assessment is free for everything we
handle other than bed bug verification, which is charged and credited against
treatment, and there is no term agreement.
