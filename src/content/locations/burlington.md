## A commercial town

Burlington is where Skagit County shops, and the built environment reflects it.
Large-format retail, distribution and warehousing, restaurants and drive-through
food service, hotels, and the road and parking infrastructure that ties them
together, concentrated around the freeway interchange.

That makes our Burlington work predominantly commercial, and commercial pest
control is a genuinely different discipline from residential. It runs on
documentation, on realistic goals for buildings that cannot be sealed, and on a
willingness to tell a client that the reason their program is not working has
nothing to do with the program.

## The buildings, and why they behave as they do

Large-format commercial construction has a consistent set of features and each
one is a pest consideration.

**Dock doors and receiving.** Open for much of the working day out of
operational necessity, with a leveling plate, a gap under the seal when a
trailer is not docked, and constant traffic across the threshold. This is the
single largest opening in the building and it cannot be closed.

**Flat and low-slope roofs.** Large expanses of membrane with parapets,
mechanical curbs, drains and roof penetrations for every service. Ponding water
where drainage is imperfect. Roof-level access to soffits and mechanical spaces.

**Suspended ceilings and service voids.** A continuous concealed space above the
sales or work floor, running the full footprint, carrying every service. Once
something is up there it has unimpeded access to the whole building and nobody
looks until a tile stains.

**Waste enclosures.** Usually a masonry corral with gates that no longer close
against the ground, compactors with a leaking seal, and cardboard staged outside
because the baler is full. This is very often the real driver of everything
else, and it is the thing most likely to be someone else's contract to fix.

**Landscaping and yard margins.** Islands, berms, the vegetated strip along a
fence line, the untended ground behind a building. All of it is harborage, all
of it is immediately adjacent to the structure, and almost none of it is
anybody's explicit responsibility.

## The exterior is usually the answer

The most useful thing we bring to a Burlington commercial account is a habit of
looking outward before looking in.

An interior rodent sighting on a retail floor is nearly always the visible end
of an exterior population. Something is supporting that population — a waste
enclosure that cannot be closed, a landscaped bed against a warm wall, a
drainage low point that never dries, an adjacent vacant lot, a neighboring
tenant's receiving practice. Placing more devices inside without changing any of
that is an indefinite cost with no end date.

So a proper first visit here means walking the full perimeter and the yard,
looking at the waste situation honestly, checking the dock aprons and thresholds
and the exterior of every penetration, and reading the landscaping as habitat.
Then the interior, the ceiling void and the receiving area.

You will get told which findings are ours to fix and which are a housekeeping,
landscaping or building-maintenance issue on your side. Some clients do not
enjoy that conversation. The ones who act on it stop paying us to manage a
symptom.

## Stored-product insects

A distribution town sees these constantly, and they are routinely misdiagnosed
as a building problem when they are a product problem.

Beetles and moths that infest grain, flour, seed, pet food, dried goods and
animal feed arrive **inside the product**. That single fact changes the entire
response: treating a building perimeter for an insect that came in on a pallet
accomplishes nothing at all, and a great deal of money gets spent that way.

The work is finding the source lot. That means tracing back through stock,
starting with whatever has been sitting longest and furthest back, checking
spillage in racking seams, under pallets and in the corners of a storage area,
and being systematic rather than hopeful. In most warehouses the source is a
single damaged sack or a spill in a corner that has been quietly producing
adults for months.

After that it is stock rotation, sealed containers where practical, cleaning
residue out of racking and floor seams, and monitoring long enough to confirm it
is genuinely finished. On a commercial account we would rather write that up
than sell a treatment schedule that leaves the source sitting where it was.

## Food service on the corridor

Restaurants and drive-throughs on a busy retail corridor have their own shape.

Volume and hours are the first factor. High throughput, long service days and
staff turnover mean housekeeping standards vary more than in a smaller
operation, and the gap between the closing checklist and what actually happens
at eleven at night is where problems start.

Drive-through service adds a specific issue: a service window that opens
repeatedly all day, usually into a warm kitchen, with a queue of vehicles and
frequently a bin nearby. Flies are the obvious consequence and they are won on
breeding sites and waste timing rather than on adults.

Grease is the third. Interceptors, the ground around them, and the drain lines
that feed them support drain flies and cockroaches, and no amount of surface
treatment substitutes for cleaning them properly.

German cockroaches are the species that matters in a commercial kitchen. They
are indoor and human-associated, they travel in cardboard, appliances and
equipment, and they breed faster than an unplanned treatment schedule can knock
back. Getting on top of them needs a coordinated pass, sealed harborage in
equipment voids, and follow-up on an interval that matches the life cycle rather
than the invoice.

## Hotels and lodging

The interchange supports a cluster of lodging, and that means bed bugs.

A bed bug problem in lodging is not a hygiene failure and it is not a reflection
on housekeeping. It is a transport problem, and any property with regular guest
turnover is exposed regardless of how well it is kept. The operators who handle
it best accept that and build detection into the routine; the ones who assume it
cannot happen to them find out late.

Early detection is everything. A problem caught in one room while it is still
one room is a contained job. The same problem discovered three months later,
after housekeeping carts and guests have moved between floors, is a
property-wide treatment at several times the cost.

Bed bugs are the one service where our assessment is not free — there is a
verification fee, credited against treatment if you proceed. Everything else we
handle costs nothing to have us look at.

## The residential side

Away from the corridor, Burlington is mostly newer housing, and it is a
straightforward job compared to the commercial work.

Modern envelopes here are genuinely good until a trade puts a hole in one. The
recurring failures are the same five we find everywhere in newer construction:
the dryer vent at the rim joist, the hose bib and irrigation supply, the heat
pump line set with its crescent gap, the clustered electrical entry, and the
garage door bottom seal on a settled slab. Each closed with expanding foam,
which stops drafts and does not stop a rodent.

What sharpens it here is what sits behind the fence. Much of this housing was
built on former farm ground and open agricultural land is still adjacent. When a
field is cut the resident population loses its cover in an afternoon; when the
autumn rain floods the low ground it happens again. Harvest drives rodent calls
in Burlington as reliably as rainfall does.

Wasps are the other residential constant. Newer construction offers vented
ridges, continuous soffit venting and generous eave overhangs — sheltered
cavities with easy access — and landscaping that has not matured enough to
compete for the space. Yellowjackets also nest in the ground along lawn edges
backing onto open ground, which describes a lot of properties here.

## Ants and the damp list

Odorous house ants and pavement ants come in along the slab edge, the sill and
service penetrations, drawn by whatever is available in a kitchen. In the newer
subdivisions an irrigation system keeps the bed against the house permanently
damp and the bark mulch never dries, which is most of the reason they are there.

Moisture ants are a different message. They colonize wood that is already wet, so
finding them means finding a water problem, and treating without correcting it
produces a new colony in the same timber.

Carpenter ants take the middle ground and keep a parent colony outdoors. On
newer lots that parent is frequently a landscape timber, a fence post or a
stump left from clearing.

## Flies, and where they actually come from

Fly pressure on a commercial site is won or lost on breeding sites rather than
on adults, and the sites are usually a short and predictable list.

House flies breed in moist decaying organic material. On a retail or food
corridor that means the residue in and under a waste enclosure, the ground
around a compactor, a grease interceptor and the soil near it, spillage in a
receiving area that gets swept rather than washed, and any drainage low point
that never dries out.

Drain flies are a specific and commonly misread case. They breed in the organic
film lining drains, floor sinks and the underside of drain covers, so surface
spraying does nothing and the population continues. Cleaning the film out
mechanically is the only thing that ends it, and where a client does that the
problem stops within a fortnight.

Both develop fast enough in warm weather that a site missed on Monday is
producing adults before the weekend. That short cycle is why a fly program built
on adult knockdown alone quietly fails every summer, and why we spend the first
visit looking for sources rather than spraying.

## Roof and mechanical spaces

Worth its own note because on large-format buildings this is where problems
originate and nobody looks.

A flat roof carries every mechanical service, and each one is a penetration.
Curbs, conduit entries, refrigeration lines, exhaust fans and drainage all pass
through the membrane, and the sealing around them degrades on a much shorter
cycle than the roof itself. Ponding water where drainage is imperfect keeps
those areas wet and accelerates it.

Once something is through the roof plane it is into the ceiling void, which on
these buildings runs the full footprint and connects everything. That is how a
problem that started at a rooftop unit shows up as droppings above a sales floor
two hundred feet away, and why chasing it at the point of sighting rarely
resolves anything.

Roof-level inspection is part of a proper commercial program here, and it is
the part most often skipped.

## Hotels, and why detection beats reaction

The interchange supports a cluster of lodging and it is worth being specific
about how that work actually goes.

Detection is the whole game. A bed bug problem caught in one room while it is
still one room is a contained job with a predictable cost and no reputational
consequence. The same problem three months later, after housekeeping carts and
guests have circulated, is a floor or property-wide treatment at several times
the price, with rooms out of service during the season you most need them.

That means the useful investment is not a bigger treatment, it is a routine that
finds things early: briefing housekeeping on what to look for and making it safe
to report, encasements on mattresses and box springs as standard because they
both reduce establishment and make evidence obvious, and inspecting the rooms
adjacent to any confirmed one rather than only the reported room.

Guests very often do not report. They leave a review instead. So the operators
who do best are the ones who assume exposure is normal for any property with
turnover and build detection into the week, rather than treating each incident
as an anomaly.

## The residential edges and the ground behind them

One thing worth spelling out for anyone living on the newer edges of town.

The population outside your fence is permanent and it is not going to be
reduced. Open agricultural ground supports it regardless of anything done to
your property, and that is simply what living beside farmland means.

What is achievable is making your building genuinely unattractive and genuinely
sealed. That is a finite piece of work with an end point — the penetrations, the
vents, the garage seal, the grade against the siding, the landscaping held off
the foundation — and once it is done properly it stays done for years.

The timing that matters is August. A house sealed before the autumn movement
does not have the interior problem at all. A house sealed in December has an
interior problem plus whatever is now sealed inside with you, and the order of
operations gets considerably more complicated.

## What we will not sell you

**Anything with feathers or fur.** Property managers ask about roof-nesting birds and about animals in a ceiling void often enough that it is worth stating: we do pest control and exclusion, and that scope stops at birds, bats, squirrels and other wildlife. We will tell you on the phone and hand you a better number than ours.

**A sealed warehouse.** Repeating it because it is the promise most often made
on this corridor. A building with dock doors open through the working day cannot
be made rodent-proof, and quoting as though it could be is how a client ends up
disappointed by a program that was never capable of the result.

**More devices in place of a fix.** Where the driver is a waste enclosure that
will not close, a landscaped bed against a warm wall or an untended yard margin,
adding interior stations is an indefinite cost. We would rather write the
finding and lose the upsell.

**Perimeter treatment for stored-product insects.** They came in on a pallet.
Treating the outside of the building for them is money spent on the wrong
problem, and it is one of the most common misdiagnoses we see in distribution.

**Mole trapping.** The methods that work are restrictively regulated in
Washington and most of what is sold to property managers does not work.

## Ants on commercial landscaping

Worth a note because it is the most common insect call we get from property
managers on this corridor, and the cause is nearly always the same.

Commercial landscaping is irrigated, mulched and planted tight against warm
building walls. That combination keeps the bed against the structure permanently
damp and the bark never dries, which is close to ideal for odorous house ants
and pavement ants. They then enter along the slab edge, through the sill, or via
a service penetration, and appear inside a break room or a customer area.

The durable corrections are landscaping decisions rather than pest ones: pulling
mulch back off the foundation, adjusting irrigation heads that spray the
building, and leaving a dry band at the base of the wall. Where a client makes
those changes the treatment holds. Where they do not, we are back next spring.
