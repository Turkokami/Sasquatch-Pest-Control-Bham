## A private development, not a neighborhood

Birch Bay Village is a gated residential community with a Blaine address, its
own marina, its own golf course, private roads and a substantial amount of
common ground held by the association.

That structure, rather than the housing, is what shapes pest control here. In
an ordinary neighborhood a property line is a legal detail and a pest problem
is entirely the householder's. Here a great deal of the ground around your
house is not yours, the vegetation on it is not managed by you, and a
significant share of what turns up at your door came from it.

Getting a good result depends on being clear about that boundary early, and it
is the first thing we work out on site.

## First, it is not Birch Bay

The names cause real confusion and it is worth separating them.

**Birch Bay** is the unincorporated shoreline community — the beach, the state
park, the seasonal cabins and the drive along the bay. It has its own page on
this site and its own set of problems.

**Birch Bay Village** is a private gated development inland and north of it,
with a Blaine mailing address, controlled access, and its own amenities. Almost
nothing about the two is the same from our side of the work: different housing
eras, different access, different ownership of the ground, different pressures.

If somebody quotes you for one thinking of the other, the quote will be wrong.

## Two jobs, one property

Practically, every call here divides into work on your lot and pressure
arriving from common ground, and the division decides what a treatment can
achieve.

**On your lot** is everything you control: the building envelope, the
landscaping against the walls, the garage, the crawl space, what is stored
outside, drainage close to the foundation. All of that is ours to work on
directly and it is where most of the durable improvement is.

**Off your lot** is the rest: common landscaping, the golf course and its
irrigation, the lagoon and marina margins, the road verges, the greenbelt
strips and whatever is stored in shared areas. That ground supports
populations, and those populations do not stop at a boundary they cannot see.

The reason this matters is not to duck work. It is that a homeowner who pays
for a thorough job on their own lot, next to an untouched source thirty feet
away on common ground, will get a partial result and feel misled. We would
rather say at the start which of the two we are dealing with, and where the
answer involves the association we will put it in writing so it can be taken to
them.

## Irrigated turf, voles and moles

A golf course, common lawns and irrigated planting produce one of the most
reliable pest patterns in the county, and residents here see it constantly.

Watered turf supports a heavy invertebrate population — worms, larvae, grubs —
and dense grass gives continuous cover at ground level. Two very different
animals exploit that, and they get confused with each other routinely.

**Voles** work at the surface. They cut shallow runways through the thatch,
leave small neat openings, and eat plant material — roots, bulbs, bark at the
base of young shrubs. What people notice is winding surface trails appearing in
the grass, especially when it is cut short after being long.

**Moles** work below. They tunnel for invertebrates and push spoil to the
surface in conical mounds, and they eat almost no plant material at all. What
people notice is the mounds and a spongy, lifting lawn.

They need different handling, and treating for the wrong one wastes a season.
The clue is in the workings: surface runways with clipped vegetation means
voles, mounds of pushed-up soil means moles, and a lawn can genuinely have
both.

Neither of them enters buildings often, which is worth saying because the panic
about them is usually disproportionate. Voles occasionally get into a crawl
space or a garage; moles essentially never. The damage is to the ground rather
than to the house.

## The lagoon, the marina and where product cannot go

Open water inside a residential development changes the toolkit, and it changes
it for good reasons.

Work near the lagoon edge, along the marina and around drainage that reaches
either has to respect the water, which means the sprayed residual that would be
routine around a foundation two hundred yards inland is not the right approach
here. Placements move to enclosed stations and to trapping, and physical
exclusion takes on more of the load.

That is not a lesser service. For rodents around water, exclusion and trapping
are the more durable answer anyway, because they deal with the route in rather
than with the individuals using it. What it does mean is that a job along the
water is slower and more mechanical, and it is worth understanding why before
somebody wonders what they are paying for.

The water edge also produces its own set of nuisance insects — midges,
non-biting flies emerging in numbers on still evenings — which are genuinely
unpleasant and are not a treatable structural problem. Where the honest answer
is screening and light management rather than treatment, we will say so.

## Fifty-year-old housing, and what that predicts

Much of the Village dates from a development era that had its own construction
habits, and those habits produce a fairly predictable set of entry points.

Crawlspaces are typically vented, with the original screening still in place —
which by now has had five decades of weather working on it from the ground side
where nobody inspects. Service penetrations for water, waste, electrical and
telephone were drilled generously and rarely closed up afterward. Siding often
runs down close to grade. Garage doors have been replaced once, usually, and
the seal at the bottom corners is where daylight shows from inside.

None of that is a defect. It is a building from its period, and it is why we
can walk up to a house here and have a fair idea of where to look before we
have looked.

The other era feature is the landscaping. Planting installed when these houses
were new is now mature, and a great deal of it is hard against the walls with
fifty years of accumulated duff underneath. That combination — permanent cover
touching siding, over damp organic material — is the single most productive
thing to fix on most lots here, and it costs nothing but an afternoon.

## Rodents, and the routes they actually use

The pressure here comes from the greenbelt strips, the water margins, the
irrigated ground and the general abundance of cover, and it is rats as well as
mice.

The routes in are ordinary. A corroded crawlspace vent. A gap where a service
line passes through the rim. The bottom corner of a garage door. A crawl hatch
that is a board resting against an opening. Where a deck or a patio has been
built up against a house, the space underneath is both a route and a place to
live, and it is nearly always unscreened.

The one route people under-rate here is the roof. Mature trees planted decades
ago now reach the roofline, and a branch touching a roof is a bridge that
bypasses everything sealed at ground level. Rats climb well and they will use
it. Trimming contact between vegetation and the building is worth as much as
most of what happens at the foundation.

## Carpenter ants and the wood around the house

Blaine carries carpenter ant pressure and the Village has its own version of
it: mature landscape timber, decades-old decks and fences, landscape ties, and
stored firewood, all of it now soft in the places that stay damp.

Carpenter ants excavate galleries rather than eating wood, and they take
softened timber far more readily than sound timber. A landscape tie that has
been in the ground since the seventies is ideal, and it is generally within
thirty feet of a building.

The sequence is the usual one — a parent colony outdoors, satellite colonies
pushed outward, and a rim joist or wall void serving a satellite as well as
anything natural. The signs are large black ants indoors in spring, winged ants
inside later on which mean something mature and reproducing, and frass
accumulating below a joint, which means an active nest above it.

The useful work is finding the parent colony rather than treating what walks
past a baseboard, and on these lots it is frequently in the landscaping rather
than in the woods.

One detail specific to a development of this age is the deck. A great many
of these houses gained a deck at some point in the last forty years, built up
close against the siding, and the space beneath it is almost never screened.
That space is dry, sheltered, invisible and directly against the building — it
is simultaneously a route in, a place to live, and the one part of the
foundation line that cannot be inspected without dismantling something. Where a
rodent problem here resists everything sensible, the deck is where we look
next.

## Wasps, and the amenity problem

Yellowjackets here are the standard late-summer story with one local
complication: a great deal of the ground is amenity ground that people walk
across in shorts.

Ground nests along verges, in bank slopes and at the edges of maintained turf
are the ones that cause injuries, and they are found by mower rather than by
eye. On common ground that is a maintenance-crew hazard as much as a resident
one, and it is worth reporting rather than stepping around.

Aerial nests under eaves, in soffits and inside outbuildings are more visible
and easier. Bald-faced hornets build the large gray paper nests people
photograph; European paper wasps build small open combs under railings and deck
rails and are markedly less defensive.

Honey bees we do not treat. If a colony has established in a wall we will
identify it and put you in touch with somebody who removes them properly.

## Getting in, and getting it scheduled

Access is the one genuinely different piece of logistics here.

Controlled entry means a plan made before the appointment rather than at the
gate. Some residents arrange it themselves, some route it through the office,
and the association's procedure is the association's to set — we will follow
whatever it is if you tell us what it is when you book. Turning up unannounced
wastes your slot and makes a nuisance of us at the gatehouse.

Private roads and shared parking are worth mentioning too, particularly if a
job needs a vehicle close to the building for equipment or for removing
material.

Where work spans a lot boundary onto common ground, it needs somebody at the
association to agree it before we do it. We are used to that conversation and
we can supply what is needed in writing.

## What is living in the turf itself

Underneath the vole and mole question sits a third one that nobody asks about
until the lawn starts dying in patches, and it is worth separating from the
other two because the animals are only the symptom.

Irrigated, fertilized turf grows a heavy population of soil-dwelling larvae.
European chafer and crane fly larvae are the two that matter in this county —
the first eats roots, the second eats roots and crowns, and both are at their
most damaging in spring when the grass is trying to grow and cannot.

What the homeowner sees is turf that lifts like a loose carpet, yellowing in
irregular patches, and birds working the lawn hard in the mornings. What they
often conclude is that something is burrowing.

The connection to moles is direct and it runs the other way from what people
assume. A lawn with a heavy larval population is a lawn worth tunneling under,
so the grub problem tends to arrive first and the mole arrives because of it.
Dealing with the animal while the food source stays put invites the next one.

This is turf management rather than pest control for the most part, and where
that is the honest answer we will say so and tell you what to ask a
groundskeeper. On common ground it is the association's turf and the
association's decision.

## Boats, trailers and the storage compound

A development with its own marina has boats, and a development of this kind
usually has somewhere to put them, along with trailers, RVs and the equipment
that comes with all three.

A storage compound is a concentrated version of the outbuilding problem.
Vehicles and vessels sit undisturbed for months, they contain upholstery,
insulation, wiring looms and often stored food, and they are parked in rows
close together with cover growing up around them.

Rodents find that combination extremely quickly, and the damage is
characteristic: chewed wiring, insulation pulled into nests, and fouling inside
enclosed spaces that nobody opens until spring. On a boat it is frequently a
wiring loom, which is an expensive discovery in April.

The work is straightforward — checking before lay-up rather than after,
removing anything edible, and using enclosed stations placed around a
compound rather than product applied to individual vehicles. The part that
needs organizing is that a compound is shared ground, so it works when it is
done for the compound and not much at all when it is done for one boat.

## What we would rather you did not pay for

If the lawn damage is moles, the honest position is that this is a grounds
problem rather than a building problem, it does not threaten the house, and it
does not warrant a treatment program.

If the source of a rodent problem is common ground, the honest position is that
a thorough job on your lot will reduce what reaches you and will not end it,
and you should know that before you commit rather than after.

And if the nuisance insects on a summer evening are coming off open water, no
amount of anything applied to your building is going to change that. Screens,
door discipline and lighting will do more, and none of that is billable.

We would rather tell you all three than sell around them.

## How a visit works here

We start on the boundary, working out what is yours and what is the
association's, because that decides everything downstream.

Then the exterior of the building at ground level: foundation line, vents,
screens, sweeps, penetrations, the garage door corners, and where planting and
duff meet siding. Then the roof line and any vegetation touching it.

Then underneath and inside, with a light, through the whole crawlspace rather
than a look from the hatch.

You get a plain account of what is active, what is historic, where it is coming
from, what we can do on your lot and what needs to go to the association — the
last of those in a form you can hand over. Looking costs nothing, bed bug verification excepted, and that fee is deducted from the treatment price. We do not tie the work to a contract.
