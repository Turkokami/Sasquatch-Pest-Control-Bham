## Living against the forest

Alger sits in the foothills where the freeway climbs away from the Samish
valley, and it is the top of our Skagit run — close enough to Bellingham to be
an easy part of a route we are already driving.

It is also unambiguously forest-edge country. The housing is rural and
scattered, frequently on acreage, commonly with a shop or a barn and a couple of
outbuildings, and very often placed deliberately close to standing timber
because the trees are the reason people live there.

That single fact — buildings against a working forest — sets almost everything
about the pest work here.

## Carpenter ants, which are the defining problem

There is more cedar and more weathered wood per property here than anywhere else
on our Skagit run, and carpenter ants follow it.

They do not eat wood. They excavate galleries in it to nest, and they strongly
prefer material that has begun to soften — which is precisely what cedar trim,
fascia, deck framing, fence posts and the bottom edge of siding become after a
few decades in this climate.

The critical part, and the part most treatment misses, is the colony structure.
A mature population here almost always has a **parent nest outdoors**: a stump
left from clearing, a standing dead tree, a fallen log, a landscape timber, a
fence post, a woodpile. Inside the building there are **satellite nests**, and
the satellites are what people see — workers crossing a counter, coarse frass
below a window, a rustling in a wall at night.

Treating the satellite while the parent sits untouched twenty feet away gets you
a quiet couple of months and a repeat call. That is the whole reason this work
has a reputation for coming back.

So a first visit here is investigative before it is anything else. We walk the
property looking for the parent colony: the stump nobody thinks about, the
section of deck framing that stays wet, the dead limb over the roof, the old
timber wall, the wood stacked against the shop. Where we find it, the result
holds. Where it turns out to be on a neighboring parcel or out in the trees
beyond the property line, we will say so plainly rather than implying we have
solved something we have not.

Frass is the useful field diagnostic. Carpenter ants push shavings out, so the
debris below a gallery is fibrous and often contains insect body parts —
different from the hard pellets a dampwood termite leaves and different from the
fine powder below a wood-boring beetle hole.

## Rodents off the treeline

The second constant, and the reason exclusion matters more here than treatment.

Wooded ground gives rodents continuous cover right up to the foundation. On an
open lot in town an animal has to cross exposed ground to reach a building; here
it does not, and the population outside is permanent regardless of anything done
to the property.

Deer mice come in off the wooded margins, along with Norway rats and house mice.
They find the crawl vents, the skirting, the gap where a deck meets the house,
the point where a service line enters, and the underfloor space of any outbuilding.

Where droppings need clearing in an enclosed space, we will tell you to
ventilate the area first and avoid sweeping or vacuuming dry material. That is
the sensible precaution regardless of what the risk turns out to be in any
particular building, and it costs nothing to do it properly.

The work that actually changes the outcome is at the interface rather than in
the woods. Trim the branch bridging to the roof. Screen the vents with material
that will still be there in ten years. Close the gap under the deck. Move the
woodpile off the wall. Correct where landscaping and brush run straight up
against the siding. Trapping alone against a treeline is a subscription rather
than a result, and we would rather tell you that at the first visit than at the
fourth.

## Outbuildings, and being honest about them

Nearly every property here has a shop, a barn, a garage or a run of storage
buildings, and the honest position on those is worth stating plainly.

A pole building with a gravel floor and a roll-up door is not going to be
rodent-proof. It never was. Quoting as though it could be sealed is how a client
ends up disappointed by a program that was never capable of the result.

What is achievable is a sensible hierarchy. Defend the buildings that can
genuinely be closed — the house first. Protect the things inside the ones that
cannot: the feed, the stored goods, and above all the wiring. Then monitor
rather than pretend.

Wiring is the priority that persuades people. Rodent damage to shop electrics,
control panels and the harness on machinery parked for the season is expensive
and occasionally dangerous, and a warm engine bay full of insulation is exactly
what a rat wants in November. Equipment stored over winter deserves a look in
February rather than a discovery in April.

Stored feed, seed and pet food are the other pivot. An open sack in a shed will
support a population indefinitely, and nothing we apply overcomes an unlimited
food supply. Metal bins with tight lids, spillage cleaned rather than swept
aside, and pallets kept off the wall so runways stay visible are worth more than
any product.

## Firewood, and why we always mention it

Wood heat is common out here and firewood storage is the single most overlooked
pest factor on a rural property.

A stack against the house is harborage directly against the structure. It holds
moisture against the siding. It is exactly the sort of decaying timber carpenter
ants nest in, so it frequently turns out to be the parent colony site we have
been looking for. And it brings spiders, beetles and other insects to within
inches of the wall.

Wood carried inside brings whatever is living in it into a warm room, which is
where the alarming spider on the hearth in January comes from.

The advice is free and it works: stack it off the ground, well away from any
building, and bring in only what you will burn that day.

## Spiders and centipedes

The heaviest pressure of anywhere on our Skagit run, and for a straightforward
reason — damp wooded ground produces insects in quantity, and spiders follow the
food.

Giant house spiders generate the autumn calls. They are harmless, long
established in western Washington, and the large fast ones people find are
wandering males seeking mates rather than an infestation. There is no brown
recluse in this corner of the state and never has been.

Exterior lighting matters far more than people expect. A bright white fixture on
a rural house on a warm night concentrates insects, and the spiders arrive to
eat them. Changing the fixture frequently does more than any treatment.

Centipedes belong to the same damp-ground story and turn up in crawlspaces and
basements. They are unsettling and essentially harmless, and they are reporting
a moisture condition rather than being a problem in their own right.

## Wasps on acreage

Worth its own note because on rural property this is a safety issue rather than
a nuisance one.

Yellowjackets nest in the ground along the edges of clearings, in banks and
root-bound slopes, under equipment that has sat for a few weeks, and in the wall
and soffit cavities of shops and outbuildings. Those nests are invisible until
something disturbs them, and a colony hit by a mower or a piece of machinery in
late summer puts somebody in hospital. The colony is at its largest and most
defensive in exactly the weeks when there is most outdoor work being done.

Bald-faced hornets build the gray paper nests people photograph, often in a tree
or under an eave, and on a wooded lot they are frequently not noticed until the
leaves come down.

Paper wasps under eaves, in equipment cabs and in the corners of outbuildings
are the mildest of the three, and where they are somewhere nobody goes we will
often say to leave them alone rather than treat for the sake of it.

The cheapest intervention by a wide margin is a walk of the eaves, outbuildings
and ground edges in late spring, when a founding nest is the size of a golf
ball.

## Wells, septic and the damp-building list

Most property out here is on a well and a septic system, and both are worth
mentioning.

Drain flies and fungus gnats around a slow drain, a wet spot in a yard or a
struggling system are a symptom to read rather than a pest to spray. Where we
find them the useful conversation is about the drainage and the system, not
about an application.

The wider damp-building list follows the same logic. Sowbugs, millipedes,
springtails, silverfish and Oriental cockroaches in a crawlspace are all
reporting moisture. Moisture ants in framing mean the wood is already wet and
already decaying, and treating them without correcting the water produces a new
colony in the same timber.

On a rural property the water is usually not hard to find: a downspout
discharging at the foundation, grade sloping toward the building, a crawlspace
with no sound vapor barrier over bare earth.

## The Alger year

**Winter.** Rodents indoors, and the damage from the autumn becoming visible.
Firewood brings the occasional startling spider into a warm room.

**Spring.** Carpenter ants become active and the first sighting is usually
indoors, because they have wintered in damp framing. Wasp queens found nests —
the cheapest possible moment to deal with them and the moment nobody is looking.

**Summer.** Wasp season, and the correct window for everything structural. Dry
ground, dry crawlspaces, easy access. Anything preventive should be scheduled
here, and on a wooded lot that means the vents, the skirting, the deck gap and
the branches over the roof.

**Autumn.** The decisive weeks. The rain returns and everything living in the
surrounding woods starts looking for somewhere dry. A building sealed in August
does not have the winter problem.

## What we will not sell you

**Anything living that is not an insect or a rodent.** On a wooded lot the
question comes up constantly — something in the attic, something under the deck,
something on the roof at night. Insects and rodents are ours; wildlife belongs
to a specialist, and we will tell you which one you have.

**Mole trapping.** Common request on acreage and not something we do. The
methods that work are restrictively regulated in Washington.

**Endless trapping against an open treeline.** If the building is not sealed,
that is a recurring cost rather than a solution, and we would rather quote the
exclusion.

**A recurring program over an unresolved water problem.** Drainage first.

## The crawlspace, and why we always go under

On rural acreage the crawlspace is where the answer is, and it is the step most
commonly skipped by whoever came before us.

These buildings frequently sit on piers or a shallow stem wall over bare earth,
with a vapor barrier that is either absent or was laid decades ago and has since
been pushed aside by every tradesman who has been under there. Bare soil puts
ground moisture straight into the space and into the joists above it,
continuously, in a climate that supplies plenty.

That single condition drives most of what people call us about. Moisture ants
colonize framing that has softened. Decay fungus runs where the wood stays wet,
and decay rather than insects is what actually costs structural capacity here.
Sowbugs, millipedes, springtails and Oriental cockroaches live in the damp and
occasionally come up into the house. Rodents nest in insulation that is hanging
loose because it absorbed moisture and lost its grip.

So a proper visit means going under with a light — on our back if that is what
the clearance requires — and following the affected framing to see how far it
actually runs. A report that says a joist is affected is not the same as knowing
whether that continues four feet into a bay nobody could reach on the day.

Where the space needs remediation, the sequence is not negotiable. Wet or soiled
insulation comes out rather than being treated around. The space gets dried. The
barrier goes back over clean ground. Only then does exclusion and treatment
hold, and doing it in the other order means installing new material into a
building that is still open and still wet.

## Wells, water and what it means for the work

Most property here is on a well and a septic system, and both change how we
approach a site.

A private well is a good reason to lead with exclusion and contained placements
rather than anything broadcast, and to think about where surface water goes
before scheduling exterior work. It is not a complication so much as a
constraint we would apply anyway on wooded ground, but it is worth saying
plainly to an owner who asks.

Septic systems produce their own diagnostic signals. Drain flies and fungus
gnats around a slow drain, a persistently wet patch in a yard, or a soft spot
over a drain field are a symptom to read rather than a pest to spray. Where we
find them the useful conversation is about the system and the drainage, not
about an application, and the person you need is usually not us.

## What the season does to access

Worth mentioning because it affects planning more than people expect on wooded
acreage.

Through the wet months a good share of the ground here does not dry out.
Crawlspace access that is straightforward in August can be standing in water in
January, exterior work is limited by what the weather allows, and a long
driveway that is fine in summer is a different proposition after a week of rain.

That is the practical reason we push structural work into late summer out here
rather than simply preferring it. The window when the ground is dry, the
crawlspace is workable and the exterior is accessible is genuinely short, and it
closes before most people think about pest control at all.
