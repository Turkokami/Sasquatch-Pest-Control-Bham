## A place without a middle

Custer is not a town in the way Lynden or Blaine are towns. It is an
unincorporated community — a crossroads, a school, a handful of businesses along
the highway, and a large amount of farmland and acreage spread around it with no
real edge to it.

That sounds like a detail about local government. It is actually the most
important thing about pest control here, because it changes what a property
looks like. A Custer address is usually not a house on a lot. It is a house on
several acres with a shop, a barn or a pole building, a long driveway, and open
field on at least one side.

Every one of those extra structures is a building that can hold rodents, and
almost none of them were built to keep anything out. Pest work here is a
whole-site job by default, and treating it as a house call is the reliable way to
fail at it.

## Open ground on every side

The country around Custer is field, pasture and berry acreage running west toward
the water and east toward Ferndale and the foothills. There is very little in the
way of natural windbreak between one property and the next.

That exposure matters in two directions. It means buildings here take weather that
a house inside a town does not — wind-driven rain against a west wall, siding that
works loose, seals that fail early, and flashing that gets tested every winter.
Openings appear on rural buildings faster than people expect, and most of them
appear on the weather side.

It also means the boundary between a building and open habitat is short. In a
subdivision there are several hundred yards of other people's landscaping between
a field and your foundation. Here there is often a fence line and twenty feet of
grass.

## Field and farm rodents

The rodent work here follows the farming calendar rather than the thermometer.

Through spring and summer, field and pasture ground supports a large rodent
population that nobody sees, because there is nothing to see. The cover is dense,
the food is out there, and the animals have no reason to approach a building at
all.

That changes when the ground is worked. Cutting, mowing, harvest and clearing
remove the cover across a large area within a short period, and the population
that has spent the summer in it has to go somewhere. What it goes to is the
nearest structure offering shelter and food, and on a Custer property that is
usually a shop or a barn rather than the house.

The house typically comes second, a few weeks later, once the outbuildings are
occupied and crowded. By then people have often already noticed droppings in the
shop and decided it was not worth doing anything about, which is the point at
which the job stops being cheap.

## Outbuildings are the real building stock

On acreage, the shop is where the problem lives.

Pole buildings and older barns are built for machinery and storage rather than for
exclusion. The sill detail at the base of a wall is frequently open. Sliding doors
do not seal at the bottom. Service penetrations are cut oversize and never closed.
There is usually something stored in there that a rodent will eat, and almost
always something it will nest in — insulation, sacking, cardboard, upholstery in a
vehicle that has not moved in a while.

None of that is a criticism of how the building was put up. A shop is not supposed
to be airtight. It does mean that a serious attempt at a rodent problem on a Custer
property starts in the outbuildings and works toward the house, rather than the
other way around.

The practical version is unglamorous: close the base of the walls, fit door
sweeps that actually reach the slab, screen the vents with metal rather than
plastic, get stored feed and seed into sealed containers, and get soft nesting
material off the floor.

## Cluster flies in open country

Cluster flies are a rural insect and Custer is rural in the way that produces the
most of them.

Their larvae develop inside earthworms in pasture and field soil, so the number of
flies a property gets is decided by the acreage around it rather than by anything
about the housekeeping inside. Permanent grass, damp soil and open ground produce
them in quantity, and this is exactly that landscape.

Where a Custer property differs from one in town is the wall itself. With no
neighboring buildings or mature planting to shade it, a west or south wall out here
holds heat late into an autumn afternoon and stands out from everything around it.
The flies find it easily, gather on it in numbers, and press into whatever the
siding offers.

Once they are through, they are in the wall void or the attic for the winter,
dormant. The complaint that reaches us is usually not about autumn at all. It is
about flies appearing indoors in January or February, sluggish, near a window,
with the house shut. Those insects are not coming from outside. They are coming
from inside the wall, and the decision that put them there was made months ago.

## Wasps, and why acreage makes them worse

Wasp problems scale with the amount of sheltered structure on a property and with
how much forage is available, and both of those are higher out here.

A property with a house, a shop, a barn and a carport has four times the eave,
soffit and rafter space of a house in town. Much of it is open-sided or unfinished,
which is precisely what a paper wasp colony wants. And a colony surrounded by
field and hedgerow has far more to eat, so it reaches a size that gets noticed
later in the season and is larger when it does.

Ground-nesting yellowjackets are the genuine hazard here rather than the nuisance.
A nest in a bank, a ditch edge or an abandoned rodent burrow gives no warning
until something goes over it, and on a property where mowing is a routine job that
is a realistic way to be badly stung. If there is regular mowing, it is worth
locating the activity in July rather than meeting it in August.

We do not treat honey bees and we will tell you so. A swarm is a beekeeper's
call, and there are people locally who will come and collect one.

## Well water, septic and what it changes

Most properties out here are on a private well and a septic system, and that
changes how we work rather than whether we can.

Placements stay clear of the wellhead and of the drain field. Anything applied
near either gets discussed before it happens rather than after. Drainage matters
more than it does on a town lot, because where water goes on the surface decides
where the damp ground is, and damp ground decides where a good deal of the insect
pressure comes from.

Septic also produces its own small pest questions. Drain flies breeding in a
sluggish line, or a persistent damp patch over a failing field that attracts
everything that likes moisture. Those are plumbing and drainage problems that
present as pest problems, and we would rather say so than sell a treatment for
them.

## Crawlspaces on open ground

Crawlspaces here have the same problems they have everywhere in this county, with
the exposure turned up.

The vapor barrier over the soil is the only thing between wet ground and the
underside of the floor. When it is torn, displaced or was never properly lapped,
the space above it stays humid, the joists take on moisture, and the conditions
that draw rodents, sowbugs and decay fungus arrive together.

Vents are the specific weakness on a rural building. They are low, they are at the
weather side, and the screening in them is usually the original — often plastic,
often perished, occasionally missing entirely. A crawlspace vent with failed
screening is a rodent door with a frame around it, and it is the single most
common entry point we find on properties in this part of the county.

## Birch Bay is close and this is not that page

Custer sits a few miles inland from Birch Bay, and the two get mentioned together
because of the highway exit and the school. They are not the same job.

Birch Bay is a shoreline community with a large stock of vacation and
part-time-occupancy housing, and the pest work there is shaped by buildings that
stand empty for months. Custer is working land, occupied year round, with the
outbuildings and machinery that go with it. A property here is more likely to have
a feed problem than an empty-house problem.

Where the two overlap is the marine air. Both take salt and moisture off the water,
and both see seals, flashing and fasteners age faster than they would inland.

## Carpenter ants, moisture ants and soft wood

Ant work on a Custer property is really water work wearing a different name.

Carpenter ants do not eat wood. They hollow it out to nest in, and they go for
wood that has already been softened by moisture rather than sound framing. On
acreage that means the predictable places where water has been getting in slowly
and nobody has had reason to look: a sill plate under an outside tap, framing
around a window on the weather side, the rim joist above a crawlspace that never
properly dries, the base of a post where a shop wall meets the slab.

Moisture ants are a plainer signal still. They need consistently wet wood to nest
in, so finding them is close to proof that there is water in that timber, not a
suggestion of it.

Either way, treating the insects without fixing the water buys a season or two.
The colony is removed, the damp wood is not, and something moves back into it.
Out here that is easy to miss for years, because the soft sill is behind a
workbench in a building nobody stands still in.

## Spiders and the rest of the domestic list

Spiders are the thing people ask about most and worry about most unnecessarily.
There are no medically significant spiders established in Whatcom County. Giant
house spiders and cross orbweavers account for nearly all of what turns up, and
the autumn rush indoors is males wandering to find females rather than anything
moving in.

A rural property does get more of them, for the same reason it gets more of
everything: more building surface, more insects around it to eat, and more
undisturbed corners in a shop or a barn to sit in. Knocking down webs and reducing
the exterior lighting that concentrates their food does more than repeated
spraying does.

The rest of the domestic list here is ordinary and mostly moisture-driven. Sowbugs
and pillbugs around damp foundations, earwigs in wet ground cover and leaf litter,
silverfish in bathrooms and older crawlspaces, and the occasional stored-product
insect in a pantry or a feed room. Almost all of it responds better to drying
things out and closing gaps than to anything applied on a schedule.

## Small commercial work along the corridor

Not everything out here is a farm or a house. The highway corridor carries small
commercial buildings, storage, shops and yards, and those have their own pattern.

A storage or equipment yard concentrates two things rodents want — undisturbed
cover between items, and buildings with doors that stand open through a working
day. A building with vehicle movement in and out has a different exposure from a
house half a mile away, and it needs a program rather than a visit: placements
mapped, records kept properly, and goals that are realistic for a building that
cannot be sealed the way a house can.

Where food is stored or handled, the sequence matters more than the products.
Containment first, spill discipline second, the building envelope third, and
control measures last. Nothing outcompetes an open food supply, and treating
around one is money spent to stand still.

## Stored vehicles, equipment and the things nobody moves

There is one category of rodent habitat on acreage that almost never gets
mentioned, and it accounts for a surprising share of what we find: the machinery
that has not moved in a while.

A tractor, a boat, a camper, a car under a tarp, a mower parked at the end of the
season. Each of them is a dry, enclosed, insulated cavity full of soft material,
sitting still, usually inside a building that is already accessible. Air filter
housings, engine bays, seat foam, wiring looms and duct work are all excellent
nesting sites, and wiring damage from rodents is expensive in a way that a nest in
a wall is not.

The same is true of stacked material. Pallets, lumber, sacking, feed bags,
cardboard, old carpet, coiled hose. Anything stacked against a wall and left alone
for a season becomes cover, and cover next to a building is what turns a field
population into a building population.

Nothing about this needs a treatment. It needs the stack moved off the wall and
onto a rack, a gap kept clear around the inside perimeter of the shop, and the
things that are genuinely never going to move checked rather than assumed. A
fifteen-minute walk with a flashlight along the inside wall line of an outbuilding
finds more than an hour spent anywhere else on the property.

## What a first visit usually turns up

For what it is worth, the findings on a Custer property are fairly consistent, and
none of them are exotic.

Failed or missing crawlspace vent screening, nearly every time. A gap at the base
of a shop wall or under a sliding door. A plumbing or electrical penetration cut
oversize and never closed. Feed or seed in bags rather than bins. Material stacked
directly against an exterior wall. Vegetation grown into contact with the siding,
which is a bridge as much as it is a moisture problem. And somewhere on the
property, an outbuilding that everyone had mentally written off.

That list is not a sales pitch. Most of it is work an owner can do without us, and
we would rather say which items those are than quote for all of them.

## The Custer year

**January to March.** Overwintering insects waking in wall voids and appearing
indoors on mild days. Rodents already inside and breeding. Repair and exclusion
season rather than prevention season.

**April to June.** Ground warms and ants become active. Wasp queens start nests,
which is the cheapest possible moment to deal with one — a single insect and a
structure the size of a golf ball.

**July and August.** Wasp colonies become obvious. Ground-nest risk peaks during
mowing. The window opens for treating the walls that overwintering insects will
gather on.

**September and October.** Heaviest weeks of the year. Fields cut, cover gone,
rodents moving into outbuildings, and cluster flies massing on warm walls at the
same time.

**November and December.** Whatever got in is in. Cold concentrates activity in
the heated parts of a building, which is when people start hearing it at night.

## What we will not sell you

**A recurring plan for a property that does not need one.** A lot of acreage here
genuinely does, because the pressure never stops. Some properties do not. If
sealing the shop and a single treatment will hold it, that is what we will quote.

**Interior treatment for overwintering flies.** They are dormant inside a wall,
they cannot reproduce there, and spraying the room they emerged into does nothing
about the rest. Exterior timing and sealing are the work.

**A rodent job that stops at the house.** We will not seal a house and leave four
open outbuildings on the same property, then come back in six weeks to find the
population has simply reorganized.
