## A growth area, mid-change

The Pepin Creek area on Lynden's west side is the part of town that is
currently becoming something else. New housing is going in on ground that was
farmed until recently, the city has been realigning creek flows into a new
riparian corridor and stabilizing banks downstream, and land has been acquired
specifically to protect and expand riparian habitat along Pepin and Fishtrap
Creeks.

That is three things happening at once on the same ground: agricultural land
being built on, a protected waterway being restored, and construction active
across the middle of it.

Every one of those has a pest consequence, and the combination produces a
pattern that neither a settled neighborhood nor a finished subdivision would
produce.

## Ground that was farmed until recently

Land does not stop being what it was on the day the surveyors arrive.

Former farm ground carries a resident rodent population that has lived there
for generations, along with the cover and food supply that supported it. When
that ground is cleared and graded, the population does not vanish. It moves,
within days, to whatever is closest — and on a development site, what is
closest is the new houses.

That is why so many people here get mice within weeks of moving into a
building that is a few months old, and why it feels like a verdict on the house
when it is nothing of the kind. It is a displacement event, and the buildings
happened to be there when it happened.

The second half of it is that the surrounding land is still worked. Cut and
harvest strip cover from large areas within days, and each time that happens
the animals living in it relocate that week. So this area gets the agricultural
pattern — sudden, seasonal, event-driven arrivals — layered on top of the
construction one.

Both are predictable, which is the useful part. The work to close a building is
worth doing before the next time ground is disturbed, not after.

## Tight is not sealed

New construction here is good at what it was designed for: air, weather and
insulation, to a standard older housing never approached.

Rodent exclusion is a different specification with different materials, and it
is not what anyone was checking on the way through. The failures are consistent
enough to predict before arriving.

**Service penetrations closed with foam.** Every house has a dozen or more —
water, waste, gas, electrical, data, the heat pump line set, the dryer vent —
and on new construction they are typically sealed with expanding foam. That
handles air and water exactly as intended and a mouse chews through it without
slowing down. It is the most common route we find on new houses.

**Vent screening stapled rather than fitted.** A screen tacked over an opening
instead of mechanically fixed into it lifts at a corner within a few years, and
then the vent is just an opening.

**Garage door corners.** Stand inside with the door closed and look at the
bottom corners. Daylight means a gap, at floor level, into a space connected to
the house.

**Behind trim and at transitions.** Where two materials meet, at corner boards,
and behind the trim around a cantilever or a bay, there are voids that were
never intended as access and serve as it.

None of that is bad building. It is a building detailed for weather rather than
for wildlife, and the fixes are small, mechanical and permanent.

## While the site next door is still active

An active construction site beside occupied houses reliably produces a wave of
calls, and it is worth knowing why rather than assuming something has gone
permanently wrong.

Two things are happening. Ground is being cleared, which displaces whatever
lived in it into the nearest buildings. And a site generates exactly what
attracts rodents: stored material, waste, offcuts, cover, and quiet at night.

It is temporary. What is not temporary is the state of your building, and the
right response is to close it while the pressure is high rather than to wait
for the site to finish and hope.

There is a practical note as well: on a live site, boundaries move and access
changes. If a job needs work along a boundary that is currently a construction
fence, tell us and we will plan for it rather than discovering it.

## The creek corridor, and where nothing gets applied

A riparian corridor being actively restored and protected is not somewhere to
be applying pesticide, and the restriction is the correct one.

Anything applied to ground that drains into the corridor reaches the water. So
work at that boundary moves to enclosed, secured stations, to trapping, and to
physical exclusion of the building itself. For rodents that is the more durable
method regardless, because it addresses the route rather than the individuals
crossing a surface.

What it means in practice is that boundary work here is slower and more
mechanical than a perimeter spray, and a cheaper quote that consists of a spray
along a creek buffer is not a comparable service — it is a different and worse
one.

The corridor will also become better habitat over time, not worse, because that
is the point of restoring it. A property backing onto it should expect the
pressure to be a permanent feature and should be closed accordingly rather than
treated repeatedly.

## Stormwater, ditches and standing water

New development comes with stormwater management, and it is worth separating
the designed water from the accidental water.

**Designed facilities** — detention and retention ponds, swales, bioretention —
support very little that troubles a house. What matters about them is the
margin: deliberately rough, vegetated, maintained lightly, holding cover and
moisture year-round. That is good rodent habitat by design, and houses backing
onto it get more pressure. It is not a fault; it is what the feature is.

**Accidental water** is different. Water standing for weeks in ruts,
excavations, uncovered materials and equipment on an active site can breed
mosquitoes, and that is a site-management matter rather than something any
treatment on a house addresses.

Ditches are the third category. Agricultural drainage around this area is
long-established, and a ditch line with vegetation along it is a corridor in
exactly the way a creek is.

## Landscaping: the window that is open right now

Right now the planting here is new — small, set back, and open — and on most of
these houses the foundation line can be seen and reached the whole way round.

That is a real advantage and it is temporary.

Every mature neighborhood in this county tells the same story: planting
installed when the houses were new, allowed to grow, now hard against the
siding over a deep bed of accumulated material. That combination is the single
most productive thing to fix on an older lot, and it takes years of growth to
create.

Keeping a genuine gap between planting and wall now, and keeping mulch off the
siding, costs nothing. Reclaiming one in fifteen years means removing
established shrubs somebody has grown attached to.

Irrigation matters as much. New landscaping gets watered heavily to establish,
and water applied against a foundation is the beginning of the moisture story
that this ground otherwise would not have.

## The first autumn in a new house

The first autumn is when people find out what their house does, and on the
edge of open ground it is a busy one.

From late September the sunny elevations collect the seasonal crowd. Cluster
flies — which breed outdoors in soil and have nothing to do with hygiene —
gather on warm walls, work into gaps, and overwinter in voids. Multicolored
Asian lady beetles and brown marmorated stink bugs do the same. On a new
subdivision with open ground around it there is nothing to intercept them.

Nothing in that group bites, reproduces inside, or harms the building itself,
which is why the response is sealing combined with exterior work timed to the
narrow window ahead of the wave. Too early and it has faded; too late and they are already on the wall. Because the trigger is weather rather than a date, the right
week shifts from season to season, and the only reliable way to catch it is to
have the visit on the calendar before the first cold snap arrives.

The same autumn is when the field rodents arrive, for the reasons above. A
house closed properly in August has a very different first winter from one
closed in November.

## Heat pumps and modern mechanicals

Houses here carry equipment that older housing does not, and it introduces
routes nobody thinks to check.

The heat pump line set passes through the wall, and that penetration is
frequently the single largest hole in the envelope — usually foamed and covered
with trunking, which handles weather and nothing else. The outdoor unit sits on
a pad against the wall with a gap underneath: dry, sheltered, undisturbed, and
directly against the building. Condensate lines and their drains hold standing
water in a warm sheltered spot, which is what small flies breed in.

None of it is difficult. It is simply newer than most of what gets checked.

## What the corridor will become

Worth thinking about now rather than in ten years: the restored corridor is
designed to improve as habitat, and it will.

Riparian planting matures. Cover thickens. The bank stabilizes and holds
vegetation it could not hold before. That is the entire purpose of the work and
it is good for the creek, the water quality and everything that depends on
both.

From a building's point of view it means the pressure at that boundary is not a
temporary condition associated with construction. It is a permanent feature that
will strengthen, and a house backing onto it should be closed properly once
rather than treated repeatedly forever.

That is the argument for spending on hardware now while the building is new,
the ground is accessible and the planting is small. Everything about doing this
work gets harder and more expensive later.

## Buying new here: what to ask before you sign

People move into this area straight from a builder, and there are three
questions worth asking that nobody thinks to ask.

Ask what was done about rodent exclusion specifically, as distinct from air
sealing and weatherproofing. The honest answer is usually that nothing was, and
that is not a scandal — it is not part of the specification — but it tells you
what you are taking on.

Ask what the warranty covers and for how long, and get it in a form you can
read. Vent screening, a garage door that does not meet the slab, and unsealed
penetrations through a rim joist can be defects rather than maintenance items,
depending on the terms.

And ask what the lot backs onto and what the long-term plan for it is: creek
buffer, stormwater tract, future phase, or farmland that will be built on. Each
of those is a different situation five years out, and it is much easier to find
out before than after.

## Wasps in new soffits

Wasps in new-construction soffits are a real pattern and this is where it
shows.

Modern soffit and fascia detailing creates long continuous voids with small
openings at intervals — vents, joints, the end of a run at a gable — which is
precisely what a queen looks for in spring. They are high up, invisible from
the ground, and nobody inspects them until there is traffic.

Because every house on a street shares the detail, a good nesting year produces
nests along a whole row rather than at one address, which residents reasonably
find alarming and which is normal.

The useful move is looking in June rather than reacting in August, and on
uniform housing the vulnerable detail is in the same place on every building.

## Dust, disturbance and the first two years

One more thing about living beside active work, and it is the part people find
hardest to plan for: the pressure does not arrive once and settle. It arrives
every time the ground moves.

A phase being cleared, a road being cut, a utility trench, a pond being formed
— each of those displaces whatever was living in that patch, and each produces
its own small wave into whatever is nearest. In a growth area that can happen
several times across two or three years rather than once at the beginning.

The practical consequence is that judging your house by one quiet season is
misleading in both directions. A first summer with nothing at all does not mean
the building is closed; it means nothing has been disturbed near you yet.

Close the building on its own merits rather than in response to the last event,
and it stops mattering what happens next door.

## What is worth doing, in order

On a house here the ranking is unusually clear and most of it is not a
treatment.

Close the builder gaps first — penetrations done mechanically rather than with
foam alone, vent screening fixed into the opening, garage door seal. That is a
morning and it is most of the battle.

Keep the landscaping off the walls while it is still small, and keep the
irrigation off the foundation.

Get the exterior autumn work timed properly for the first season, then judge
whether it is needed every year.

And where the boundary is a creek buffer or a stormwater margin, treat that
edge as permanent and close the building rather than expecting anything applied
to hold.

## What we would rather not sell you

A recurring perimeter program on a two-year-old house whose actual problem is a
foamed line set and a garage door gap will produce a visible result forever and
will never finish.

A spray along a creek buffer is not something we will quote, whatever anybody
else is offering.

And where the mosquitoes are coming off standing water on a construction site,
nothing applied to your building will change that, and we will say so.

## How a visit works here

We start with the surroundings, because on this ground they are the whole
story: what is being worked, what is being built, how close the creek buffer or
the stormwater margin gets, and what state the ground next door is in.

Then the building at ground level, checking the known failure points for new
construction rather than searching: penetrations including the line set, vent
screening and how it is fixed, sweeps, and the garage door corners from inside
with the door shut.

Then the soffit line for wasps and the elevations that hold the afternoon sun,
because those decide the autumn.

Then underneath and inside, with a light — quick on a house this age, and worth
doing anyway, because a crawlspace established as clean is a useful baseline
for later.

You get a plain account of what is active, what is a route rather than a
source, which items may be a builder's responsibility rather than yours, and
what should be done before the next time ground nearby is worked. The
assessment costs nothing beyond bed bug verification if that ever applies, and
nothing about the work requires a term agreement.
