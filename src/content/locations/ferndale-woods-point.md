## A gated community of single-level homes

Woods Point is a gated residential community of single-level homes for
residents aged 55 and over, with private roads and a substantial amount of
common ground managed on behalf of everybody rather than by individual owners.

Three things about that shape the work, and only one of them is about the
buildings.

The housing is uniform, low and consistently built, which makes diagnosis fast.
A good deal of the ground that matters is common rather than private, which
decides who should be acting. And the routine self-inspection that a
pest-control company quietly relies on from most homeowners — somebody putting
their head into the crawlspace, climbing up to look at a soffit, moving what is
stacked behind the shed — is not something anybody should be expecting here.

That last point is the one that changes the service most, and it is worth
starting with.

## What a scheduled look is actually worth here

Most pest problems in most houses are found by the householder, by accident,
early. Somebody notices droppings behind something, hears movement overhead,
sees a wasp using the same gap each afternoon, or goes into the crawlspace for
an unrelated reason and finds fouled insulation.

That early-warning system is doing an enormous amount of work, and it depends
on somebody being willing and able to get into awkward places.

Getting under a house is hard work. It is low, dirty, cold, and there are real
hazards — old wiring, standing water, sharp edges, and occasionally an animal
that is not pleased to see you. Nobody should be doing it to check on
something, at any age, and certainly nobody should feel they ought to.

So on this development a scheduled inspection is not the same product it is
elsewhere. It is replacing a service that in most neighborhoods is being
provided free by the occupant. That is why an annual or twice-yearly look here
finds things at a stage where they are still small, and why we would rather
sell that than a treatment for something that got to be large first.

It also means that when we come, we go under and we go up, and we report what
is there in plain terms with photographs where they help.

## Two jobs: your home and the common ground

Every call here divides into work on your home and pressure arriving from
ground that is not yours, and being clear which is which decides everything
downstream.

**Your home and your lot** is the building envelope, the garage, the
crawlspace, the planting immediately against the walls, and what is stored
outside your unit. That is where the durable work is and it is ours to do
directly.

**The common ground** is the shared landscaping, the boundaries, the road
verges, any greenbelt or buffer strips, and the shared storage areas. That
ground supports populations, and populations do not stop at a line they cannot
see.

We say this early rather than late for one reason: somebody who pays for a
thorough job on their own home, next to an untouched source on common ground
thirty feet away, will get a partial result and reasonably feel they were
misled. Where the honest answer involves the management, we will say so and put
it in writing so it can be handed over.

## Single-level housing, and the crawlspace that runs under all of it

A single-story house on a crawlspace has a larger footprint at ground level
than a two-story house of the same floor area. More perimeter, more vents,
more penetrations, and a bigger under-floor space.

That matters in two ways. There are more openings to check, and once anything
is under there it has a lot of continuous space to occupy without ever being
heard from a living room.

The specific things we find on housing of this kind are consistent. Vent
screening that has been stapled over an opening rather than fixed into it, and
has come away at a corner. Service penetrations sealed with expanding foam,
which stops air and water and does not stop a mouse. A crawl hatch that is a
board resting against an opening. Insulation sagging out of the joists, which
is both a symptom and excellent nesting material. And the garage door corners,
which on any door of a few years' age will show daylight from the inside.

None of that is unusual or a sign of neglect. It is what this construction
does, and the fixes are small, mechanical and permanent.

## The garage, which is nearly always where it starts

On single-level housing the garage connects directly to the living space and it
is the most productive place we look.

It is sheltered but unheated, it is quiet, it holds stored soft material, and it
very frequently holds birdseed, pet food, bulbs or garden products in bags
rather than in solid containers. A gap at a door corner leads straight into
that, and from there into the house.

The fixes cost very little. A proper door seal. Stored food in sealed
containers. A clear gap kept around the walls so the perimeter can actually be
seen. That last one is worth as much as the other two, because a garage stacked
to the walls cannot be inspected by anybody, including us.

## Wasps, and why we would rather look in June

Wasps deserve their own section here rather than a paragraph, because the risk
profile is different.

A sting is unpleasant for anybody. For somebody with a cardiac condition, on
anticoagulants, with a known sting allergy, or simply less steady on their
feet, an unexpected encounter with a ground nest while gardening is a
materially more serious event.

Nests get found in August because that is when the colony is large and the
traffic is obvious. They exist from spring, small and quiet, in soffit voids, at
roofline junctions, in outbuildings, under deck rails, and in the ground along
verges and boundary banks.

A walk of the eaves, the soffit line, the outbuildings and the boundary ground
in June finds most of them while they are still simple to deal with and before
anybody has walked into one. It costs nothing, we would rather do it than not,
and on this development we think it is the single most useful preventive visit
of the year.

Ground nests along common boundaries are a maintenance hazard as well as a
resident one and are worth reporting to the office rather than stepping around.

## How we work in an occupied home

There are a few things worth putting in writing about conduct, because they
matter more here than in most places.

We will tell you what we are applying before we apply it — what it is, where it
goes, where it does not go, and how long to stay off a treated area. Not because
you asked, but because that is how it should be done. Every product this
company carries is [published on this site](/what-we-use/) with its EPA registration number, its
full label and its safety data sheet, so anything we say can be checked against
the document.

We will not use the word "safe". It is unlawful to describe a pesticide in
terms that differ from its accepted labeling and no label uses that word, so
anybody who offers it to you is either unaware or hoping you are. What we can
do is show you the label, explain the precautions, and choose an approach that
suits the household.

Where there is a version of the job that uses less, or nothing at all — a
repair rather than a treatment, a bait rather than a spray, hardware rather
than chemistry — we will offer it before you have to ask.

And we will turn up when we said, or tell you before the time we said that we
cannot. Waiting in for somebody who does not come is a genuine imposition and
it is the complaint we hear most often about this trade.

## Visiting family, pets and where things go

Grandchildren visit, dogs visit, and both change placement rather than
possibility.

Anything placed goes in enclosed, secured stations, away from where anybody
small or four-legged goes. Interior work is planned around the rooms in use and
around when visitors are expected. Tell us about a visiting dog rather than
assuming we will know, because it is the kind of thing that decides where a
station goes.

## Downsizing, and what comes with the boxes

A great many people arrive here from a larger house, and a move like that
brings decades of stored belongings into a smaller building all at once. That
is worth mentioning, because it is the route for two things that surprise
people.

**Stored-product insects** travel in pantry goods and in things that have sat
in a garage or a basement for years: flour, rice, birdseed, pet food, dried
flowers, wool, old fabric. Indianmeal moths and carpet beetles both arrive this
way, already established, and emerge weeks after the boxes were unpacked. The
answer is the cupboard rather than the building, and it is quick once it has
been identified.

**Secondhand and long-stored furniture** is the main route for bed bugs, and
nobody should feel accused by that. They travel on objects and on people rather
than following how a home is kept. What matters is that they are found early,
and in a community of closely spaced homes an early report protects everybody
rather than only the household that made it.

If a move is coming up, the most useful thing anybody can do is check stored
goods before they come in rather than after. We are happy to look at anything
you are unsure about.

## Reporting early, and why nobody should feel awkward

The thing that costs households most in a community like this is not the pest.
It is the delay.

People wait because they think it reflects on them, because they do not want to
make a fuss, or because they are not certain it is anything at all. By the time
it is raised, a problem that would have been a small job has become a large
one, and in closely spaced housing it may well have reached a neighbor.

Nothing on this page is a reflection on anybody's housekeeping. Rodents follow
gaps, wasps follow cavities, and bed bugs follow luggage. All three arrive at
immaculate homes, regularly.

If you are unsure whether what you have found is anything, call and ask. We
would far rather look at something that turns out to be nothing than be called
three months later.

## Rodents, and the ordinary way in

There is nothing exotic about how anything gets into these homes.

The routes are the vent screening, the penetrations, the crawl hatch, the
garage door corners, and the gap where planting or stored material sits against
siding. On a uniform development those exist in the same places on every unit,
which makes a first visit fast and a repair specification reusable across the
whole community.

The pressure comes off the common landscaping and whatever lies beyond the
boundary, and it rises sharply whenever that ground is cut or cleared — the
animals living in it relocate within days, and the nearest building with a gap
is where they go. That is predictable, which means the sealing is best done
before the maintenance rather than after.

## Ants, and the ones that mean something

Ants along a foundation here are usually odorous house ants or pavement ants
foraging on a warm, irrigated line. A nuisance rather than damage, and they
respond to baiting along the trail. Spraying a trail often splits the colony
and makes the next month worse, so an immediate visible result is the wrong
measure.

Carpenter ants are a different animal and mean something different — they
excavate galleries in wood. Moisture ants are a report about wet timber rather
than a pest problem at all, and where we find them the answer is a moisture
repair rather than a treatment.

They look broadly similar to most people, which is exactly why identification
comes first.

## Storage, and the shared areas

A development like this usually has some shared storage — for boats, trailers,
RVs, or simply for the things that will not fit in a single-level home with no
basement.

Concentrated stored material in a low-traffic building is reliably productive
for rodents, and the damage tends to be discovered months later. Where that
storage is shared, the work has to be done for the building rather than for one
bay, because sealing one while the ones either side stay open moves the animals
a few feet.

That is a management conversation and we are happy to have it, or to give you
what you need to raise it.

## What we would rather not sell you

We will not put a home on a recurring treatment schedule when the actual
problem is a vent screen and a garage door seal. The hardware costs a morning
and it does not repeat.

We will not treat around a home for a source that is sitting on common ground
and tell you it is handled. We will say where it is and help you take it to the
right people.

And we will not describe a nuisance as a danger. Cluster flies, lady beetles
and house spiders are unpleasant and harmless, and a company that tells you
otherwise to sell a program is doing something we would rather not compete
with.

## Between visits, and what is worth a phone call

Nothing here needs monitoring by the resident, and we would much rather you
called about something that turns out to be nothing than watched it for a
month wondering. A wasp using the same gap each afternoon, a smell that was not
there last week, or droppings behind something in the garage are all worth a
call the day you notice them rather than at the next scheduled visit.

## How a visit works here

We start with the boundary — what is yours, what is common, how close the cover
gets, and what has been cut recently.

Then the building at ground level: the whole perimeter, every vent and how the
screen is fixed, the penetrations, the sweeps, the crawl hatch, and the garage
door corners from the inside with the door shut.

Then underneath, properly, with a light, through the whole crawlspace. That is
the part you should never be doing yourself and it is where most of the useful
information is.

Then the soffit line and the eaves, especially in early summer.

You get a plain account in plain language: what is active, what is old, what is
driving it, what needs doing now and what can wait, which items are building
work rather than pest work, and what belongs to the community rather than to
you. There is no fee for the visit or for the written report. Bed bug verification is billed and credited back, and nobody here is asked to commit to a schedule.
