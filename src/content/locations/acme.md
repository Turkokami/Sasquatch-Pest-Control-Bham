## One road, one river, and the valley between them

Acme is not laid out like a town. It is a stretch of Highway 9 — the Valley
Highway — with working farms strung along it, a school, a fire station, and a
scattering of houses and acreage running back toward the hills on either side.
North of it is Van Zandt. South of it are Clipper and Wickersham. Together they
make up the South Fork Valley, and the volunteer fire district that covers all
of them spans more than twelve miles of that single road.

That is a useful figure to hold on to, because it describes the place better
than a population number would. Twelve miles of valley, one highway, and no
part of it more than a few hundred yards from either the river or the treeline.

Lake Whatcom is to the west, the North Cascades and the Mount Baker–Snoqualmie
National Forest to the east, and the South Fork of the Nooksack runs the length
of the floor between them. Almost every property we work on here has farm
ground on one side and standing timber on the other, and the two send entirely
different problems at entirely different times of year.

## The river is what makes this different

Plenty of rural Whatcom County is farmland against forest. Deming is, sixteen
miles north of here on the other fork. Custer is open country. What Acme has
that neither of those has in the same measure is a river running the whole
length of the community, close to the buildings, on a floor that does not drain
quickly.

Three consequences follow from that, and between them they account for most of
what we are called out here to deal with.

**The water table is high.** In a valley floor with a river through it, ground
water sits closer to the surface than it does on higher ground, and it moves
seasonally. That is why crawlspaces here are damp in a way that has nothing to
do with the roof.

**The riparian corridor is a highway.** The vegetated strip along a river is
continuous cover, food and water running for miles. It is one of the best
rodent habitats in the county, and it passes within a short walk of nearly
every building in the valley.

**High water relocates things.** When the river comes up, it floods burrows
along the bank. Whatever was living in them does not drown; it moves to higher
ground, and higher ground here means somebody's shop, barn or crawlspace.

## Damp crawlspaces, and why the roof is not the answer

The single most common thing we find under a house in this valley is a wet
crawlspace with a perfectly sound roof above it.

People reasonably assume moisture comes from above, so they clear the gutters,
check the flashing and look for a leak. In a valley floor that is usually the
wrong direction to be looking. Moisture is wicking up out of bare soil, driven
by a water table that sits close to the surface, and a vented crawlspace over
damp ground stays damp no matter what the roof is doing.

That matters for pest control rather than only for building maintenance,
because damp wood is what turns a house from unattractive to habitable for
several of the things that live around here.

**Carpenter ants** do not eat wood, but they excavate galleries in it, and they
excavate softened wood far more readily than sound wood. A rim joist that has
spent fifteen years above damp soil is a materially easier target than one that
has not.

**Wood-boring beetles and decay fungi** need moisture content that dry timber
never reaches. The presence of either is a moisture report before it is a pest
report.

**Rodents** do not need the damp, but they are drawn to the warmth and the
cover, and a crawlspace with degraded insulation hanging out of the joists is
better nesting than anything outdoors.

The fix is unglamorous and it works: a ground-contact vapor barrier over the
soil, drainage corrected where it is obviously wrong, and the venting sorted
out one way or the other. That is building work rather than treatment, and it
is the reason a fair number of our jobs in this valley involve no product at
all.

## What the river corridor sends, and when

Most residential rodent work in Whatcom County is house mice. In the South Fork
Valley we see a great deal of rat work alongside it, and the distinction is
worth making before anybody sets a trap, because the two animals do not behave
the same way.

**Mice** occupy small spaces, breed quickly and travel very little. A mouse
problem tends to be local to one building and one route in, and it escalates
fast once it starts.

**Rats** range considerably further, are markedly cautious about anything new
in a familiar run, and produce a different signature. Heavy movement in a floor
or wall at night, gnawing that has gone through something structural, chewed
wiring in a shop, a crawlspace vent enlarged rather than merely entered — those
lean rat rather than mouse.

Getting that wrong costs a fortnight. A rat will avoid an unfamiliar object in
its run for days, so a placement strategy that works on mice reads as failure
on rats until somebody realizes what they are actually dealing with. We work it
out from the droppings, the gnawing, the runs and the smell before anything
gets deployed.

There is a seasonal shape to it as well. Farm ground supports large outdoor
populations quite happily through the warmer months, and those animals only
come at buildings when something changes — the ground is worked, cover is cut,
a food source moves into storage, or the weather turns. Cutting and harvest
strip cover from a great deal of ground in a short space of time, and the
animals living in it have to go somewhere within the week.

## When the river comes up

High water is a normal feature of life here rather than an exceptional one, and
it produces a pest problem with a shape worth knowing about in advance.

The sequence is consistent. Water rises and floods burrows along the bank.
Displaced animals move laterally and upward onto whatever ground stays dry,
which is where the buildings are. Within a few days a property that had no
rodent activity at all has a great deal of it, arriving all at once and often
into outbuildings first.

Two things are worth saying about that. The first is that it has nothing to do
with how well a place is kept. It is a displacement event, and immaculate
properties get it as readily as neglected ones.

The second is that it has an end. This is not a permanent condition to be
managed indefinitely with a recurring contract — it is a defined problem with a
beginning, and the useful response is to deal with the animals that arrived and
close the openings they used, so that the next time the water comes up they
have nowhere to go on your property.

The other half of a flood is the moisture that stays behind. Wet insulation
does not dry out on its own under a floor, and saturated material left in place
is a nesting substrate and a fungal problem at the same time. Removing it is
part of the job rather than an upsell.

## Carpenter ants, and the timber on the other side

The forest on the valley walls does what forest does everywhere in this county:
it maintains a very large number of carpenter ant colonies that have nothing to
do with anybody's house until they expand.

A mature colony establishes itself outdoors in a stump, a fallen log or the
punky base of a standing tree, and then pushes satellite colonies outward.
Those satellites hold workers and older larvae rather than the queen, and a
wall void, a rim joist or a door frame suits them as well as another log does.

In a subdivision a building generally has to generate its own problem over
years of unnoticed moisture. On a forest edge the colony is already there and
only has to move part of itself indoors — and in this valley the moisture the
ants prefer is supplied for them by the ground.

That combination is why the season here is a genuine season. Activity begins as
the ground warms and builds through summer. The first sign for most households
is not damage but large black ants indoors in March or April, one or two at a
time, usually near a window or a sink. They get killed and forgotten. What they
actually indicate is that something nearby has become active and started
foraging.

Winged ants indoors are a different and more serious signal, because they mean
a mature reproducing colony. If they are emerging inside the building rather
than arriving through a door, the colony is in the structure.

The third sign is frass — the sawdust-like material pushed out of galleries,
often mixed with insect fragments, accumulating below a crack or a joint. Frass
means an active nest above it, and where it lands tells us where to look.

## The autumn wave

From late September the valley produces the same set of visitors it produces
every year, and they arrive together.

**Multicolored Asian lady beetles** gather on sun-warmed walls on the first
genuinely cold afternoons, working the south and west elevations, and then move
into gaps around trim, siding laps and attic vents.

**Brown marmorated stink bugs** do the same thing on the same walls at
roughly the same time.

**Cluster flies** collect in upstairs rooms and window channels, usually in
numbers that seem out of proportion to any opening anybody can find.

**Spiders** come indoors for their own reasons, and this is the time of year
people notice them.

The lady beetles will nip — a light bite that stings briefly and does nothing
afterward. Beyond that, none of the three breeds indoors and none of them
damages the building. That is not a reason to shrug at them — a room full of lady beetles
is genuinely unpleasant, and the smell of a stink bug that has been trodden on
lasts — but it does change what a sensible response looks like.

Because they do not reproduce inside, the population indoors is entirely a
function of how many got in. Repeated interior spraying through the winter
kills what is already there and does nothing about the supply. Correctly timed
exterior work before the wave, plus sealing the openings they use, addresses
the supply. That is the whole of the difference between a house that has this
problem every year and one that does not.

## Two kinds of building, two different jobs

Property here splits fairly cleanly, and the split predicts the work.

**The old farmhouses and the original valley stock** were built long before
anyone thought about sealing a building envelope. Post-and-pier foundations,
board crawlspace hatches, penetrations drilled generously and never closed,
screening that has corroded from the ground side. None of that is damage. It is
a building doing what it was built to do, in an era with different
expectations. Exclusion work on these places is patient and detailed, and it
is where most of the benefit is.

**The newer construction** — and there is more of it than there was, as the
valley has drawn retirees and people working remotely — is tighter, but it is
not sealed. The failures are different and more predictable: utility
penetrations foamed rather than closed, garage door corners that show daylight
from inside, crawlspace vents with the screen stapled rather than fitted, and
landscaping installed right up against the siding.

The one thing both share is outbuildings, and on acreage that is the whole
game. Shops, barns, woodsheds and pump houses sit closer to cover than the
house does and are sealed to a much lower standard. They fill first. The house
fills from them. Sealing the house on a property whose shop is open does not
remove the population — it concentrates it and moves the problem indoors.

We quote the site here rather than the building, and if somebody only wants the
house done we will say plainly what that will and will not achieve.

## Wasps, and the ones that matter

Yellowjackets in this valley are mostly a late-summer problem and mostly an
outdoor one, and the nests that cause real trouble are the ones nobody can see.

Ground nests are found by machine rather than by eye — a mower, a brush cutter,
a tractor working an edge — and that is how people end up in an emergency room.
On acreage, knowing where the activity is before August is worth a great deal,
and it costs nothing to have somebody look while they are on the property for
something else.

Aerial nests under eaves and in outbuildings are more visible and less
dangerous, but a nest in a shop doorway is still a nest in a shop doorway. Bald-
faced hornets build the gray paper footballs people photograph; European paper
wasps build the small open combs under railings and window frames and are far
less inclined to defend them.

Honey bees are a different matter entirely and we do not treat them. If a swarm
has moved into a wall we will say so and point you at somebody who removes them
properly.

## What we would rather you did not pay us for

A fair amount of what gets sold as pest control in a valley like this one is
really moisture work wearing a different hat, and it is worth saying which is
which before anybody quotes you.

If the carpenter ants are in a rim joist that has been sitting above bare damp
soil for two decades, treating the ants deals with this year's colony and
leaves the condition that invited it. The vapor barrier is the durable fix and
it is cheaper over ten years than the treatment repeated ten times. If the
rodents are coming through a corroded crawlspace vent, a new vent screen ends
that route permanently and no product does.

That is not us talking ourselves out of work. Exclusion and crawlspace
restoration are a large share of what this company does, and they are better
work than spraying — they are finished when they are finished. What we will not
do is put a building on a recurring treatment for a problem that a morning with
a drill and some hardware cloth would have ended.
