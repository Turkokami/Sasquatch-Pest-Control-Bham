## One city, a dozen different jobs

Bellingham looks like a single market on a map and behaves like several. The
city grew outward and upward in distinct waves, and each wave left behind a
different way of failing. A house on the north flats and a house on Alabama Hill
are three miles apart and have almost nothing in common from our side of the
work. We do not treat them the same, and the first thing that happens on any
call here is working out which Bellingham we are standing in.

That is not a sales position. It is the practical reality of a city where the
oldest houses predate the First World War, the newest went up last year, and the
ground underneath ranges from marine clay on the flats to sandstone on the
hills to saturated lakeside soil out toward Silver Beach.

## The neighborhoods, grouped by what actually happens in them

The City recognizes twenty-five neighborhoods. From a pest standpoint they sort
into about six groups, and knowing which group an address falls in tells you
most of what you need to know before the truck arrives.

### The prewar flats

The Lettered Streets, Columbia, York, Sunnyland, Roosevelt and the older parts
of Birchwood are the oldest continuously occupied residential ground in the
city. Small lots, alleys behind the houses, mature street trees, and building
stock that in many cases predates 1930.

Structurally these houses share a set of features that were entirely reasonable
at the time and are a gift to rodents now. Post-and-pier foundations with a
short crawlspace and a bare dirt floor. Skirting rather than a continuous stem
wall. Balloon or near-balloon framing, where the stud cavity runs from the sill
plate to the top plate without a firestop, so a mouse that enters at grade can
be in the attic without ever crossing an open room. Original board sheathing
that has shrunk and left gaps. And decades of accumulated utility penetrations —
gas, water, electrical, cable, three generations of phone line — each one drilled
oversize and none of them ever sealed.

The alleys matter too. Alley-served neighborhoods concentrate garbage, and
garbage concentrates rats. A block where every can sits out on the same alley
on the same night supports a population that no amount of interior work will
touch. On these streets the conversation frequently has to widen from one house
to the block.

The pests follow directly: Norway rats and house mice in the crawl and the
walls, carpenter and moisture ants in any framing that has taken water, and
sowbugs, millipedes and Oriental cockroaches in the damp crawl itself. The
correct work is exclusion — sealing the envelope, screening the vents, closing
the penetrations — with treatment as the part that handles what is already
inside.

### The hillsides and the forest edge

Alabama Hill, King Mountain, parts of Barkley, Edgemoor, South Hill and Whatcom
Falls sit on slopes with trees. The stock is mostly 1960s through 1980s: cedar
and T1-11 siding, generous soffit and ridge venting, split levels, and daylight
basements cut into the grade.

Cedar is the recurring theme. It weathers beautifully and it softens, and softened
cedar is exactly what carpenter ants want. Fifty years in, the fascia, the trim
boards and the bottom edge of the siding are frequently punky enough to host a
satellite colony, and the parent colony is usually in a stump or a standing dead
tree at the property line rather than in the house at all. Treating the kitchen
counter where the ants are walking does nothing. Finding the tree does.

Slope creates its own problems. A daylight basement means part of the foundation
is buried and part is exposed, and the transition is where water and insects
both get in. Sloped crawlspaces collect water at the low end. Retaining walls
hold moisture against structure. Landscaping installed to control erosion
becomes a highway for rodents from the greenbelt to the house.

These neighborhoods also produce the heaviest carpenter ant pressure in the city, because there is more weathered cedar per house here than anywhere else and the parent colony is usually a stump or a dead limb somewhere on the lot.

### The student belt

Sehome, Happy Valley, the streets immediately below the Western campus, and
increasingly the apartment stock in Cordata and along the Barkley corridor.
This is not a housing-stock category so much as a turnover category.

Two things drive everything here. First, tenancy cycles: a large fraction of
this housing empties and refills in June and again in September, and furniture
moves between buildings in the back of pickup trucks. That is the single most
effective bed bug transport mechanism in the county. Second, density and shared
structure: in a multi-unit building the pests are a building-level population,
not a unit-level one, and treating one apartment while the neighbors go
untreated simply relocates the problem across a shared wall.

Cockroaches — German cockroaches specifically, which are an indoor,
human-associated species rather than the damp-crawl Oriental kind — are the other
signature of this belt. They travel in appliances, in cardboard and in kitchen
equipment, and they persist because they breed faster than an unplanned
treatment schedule can knock them back.

Our approach in this belt is always a building plan first: map the units,
identify the source, treat in a coordinated pass, and follow up on a schedule
that matches the life cycle rather than the invoice. Landlords who fight this
end up paying for the same problem three times.

### The lakeside

Silver Beach, Samish and the Lake Whatcom shoreline edge of the city. Cedar
again, water again, and the added constraint that this is Bellingham's drinking
water catchment. We favor exclusion, habitat correction and contained
placements on these properties and we watch the weather before doing exterior
work here at all.

Practically, lakeside houses get carpenter ants in the cedar, rodents pushing in
off the treeline, and the heaviest spider and centipede pressure inside the city
limits. Docks and boathouses add their own rodent question, and seasonal
occupancy adds another.

### The greenbelt-adjacent middle

Cornwall Park, Whatcom Falls, parts of Puget and the streets that back onto the
creek corridors. Bellingham is unusually generous with greenbelt, which is one
of the best things about living here and a permanent source of wildlife
pressure. Rodents use the greenbelt as continuous cover from one end of a neighborhood to the other.

The fix is nearly always about the interface: trimming the branch that bridges
to the roof, screening the vent, capping the chimney, correcting the grade where
the greenbelt meets the lawn. Trapping alone in a greenbelt-adjacent house is
an annuity for the pest company and a waste of money for the homeowner.

### Downtown and the commercial corridors

City Center, Irongate, the Meridian corridor and the Guide. Shared party walls,
common utility chases, food service, dumpster enclosures and loading docks. This
is genuinely different work, run on documentation and inspection readiness
rather than on a single visit.

In a downtown block the rodent population is shared between buildings whether
the tenants like it or not, and the sanitation weak point in one restaurant
feeds the population that shows up next door. Commercial work here means device
placement to a plan, a service record that will stand up to a health
inspection, and enough honesty to tell a client when the actual problem is a
dumpster lid rather than anything we can treat.

## Rats, and the specifically Bellingham version of the problem

Norway rats are the most common single reason a Bellingham household calls a
pest company, and the local pattern is worth understanding because it is not the
one described in national material.

Through spring and summer, rats here live outdoors and do well at it. Himalayan
blackberry — which is everywhere, in every greenbelt, along every alley and
across every vacant lot — is close to ideal habitat: dense, thorned, impossible
for predators to work through, and adjacent to human food. Add decking with a
gap underneath, woodpiles, compost, chicken coops, bird feeders and the creek
corridors, and the city supports a substantial outdoor population that most
residents never see.

Then the rain arrives. Somewhere between late September and early November the
ground saturates, burrows flood, and that entire outdoor population needs
somewhere dry. Your crawlspace is dry. Your garage is dry. The soffit space above
your porch is dry. There is no mystery in it — it is a housing crisis with a
predictable date.

Which is why the most valuable thing we do in this city is exclusion work in
August. A house sealed before the rain never has the interior problem. A house
sealed in December has an interior problem plus a sealed-in animal, and the
order of operations gets much more complicated.

Chickens deserve a specific mention. Backyard flocks are common in Bellingham
and they are, from a rat's perspective, a reliable year-round feed station. We
do not tell people to get rid of their chickens. We do tell them that spilled
feed and an open feed bin are what turn an ordinary yard into a rat-supporting
one, and that the coop needs to be built like it is trying to keep rats out,
because it is.

Old fruit trees do the same job without anyone deciding to feed anything. The
prewar flats are full of plums, apples and pears planted eighty years ago, and
from late August a mature tree puts down more fruit than a household picks up.
That windfall ferments on the ground through precisely the weeks the outdoor
population is building condition for winter, and a food-scrap cart whose lid no
longer seats properly does the same on a smaller scale. Neither is an argument
for taking the tree out. Clearing under it weekly through September is worth
more than anything we could place.

## Ants in a city built on wet ground

Two ants matter here and they are commonly confused.

Moisture ants are the honest ones. They are attracted to wood that is already
wet and already decaying, and finding them is genuinely useful information: it
means there is a water problem, and the ants are the symptom. Killing them
without fixing the water gets you a new colony.

Carpenter ants are the expensive ones. They excavate galleries in wood to nest,
they prefer wood that has begun to soften but will work sound timber once
established, and a mature colony in Bellingham typically has a parent nest
outdoors — a stump, a dead limb, a fence post, a landscape timber — and satellite
nests inside the structure. The satellites are what you see. Treating them
without locating the parent means a reprieve rather than a solution.

Spring is when both become visible, and it is also when people mistake winged
carpenter ants for termite swarmers. The difference is straightforward once
somebody shows you: ants have an elbowed antenna, a pinched waist and two pairs
of wings of unequal length; termites have a straight antenna, a uniform body and
four wings of the same size. We would much rather identify it correctly on a
free visit than sell the wrong treatment.

## The fall spider season

Every September the phone starts ringing about large fast spiders indoors, and
every year the answer is the same and reassuring. These are almost always giant
house spiders, which are established throughout western Washington, are harmless
to people, and are not moving indoors because it is getting cold. Adult males
are wandering because they are looking for mates, and the ones people find in
bathtubs are males that got in, could not climb back out and are simply stuck.

Bellingham has no brown recluse. It has never had brown recluse. Black widows
exist in Washington but are associated with the drier country east and south of
the Cascades and are genuinely rare here. Anyone who tells you otherwise while
holding a quote is selling fear.

What we can do is reduce the population sensibly: knock down webs on the
exterior, deal with the harborage under decks and in crawl vents, correct the
exterior lighting that concentrates the insects the spiders are eating, and seal
the gaps at door sweeps and thresholds that most of them use to get in.

## Wasps, hornets and the Bellingham summer

Yellowjackets are the main event, and they turn up in three places with
predictable regularity: in the ground along the edge of lawns and greenbelts, in
wall voids entered through a gap in the siding or a failed vent screen, and in
soffits and eaves on the sunny side of a house.

The seasonal shift matters. Early in the summer a colony is feeding protein to
brood and is essentially uninterested in people. From late July the brood
production winds down, the workers switch to sugar, and that is when they start
appearing at picnics, cans of soda and fallen fruit. The colony is also at its
largest and most defensive at exactly that point, which is why late-season
nests are the ones that put people in urgent care.

Bald-faced hornets build the gray papery football nests people photograph and
send to us, and they get reported as northern giant hornets several times a
year. The genuine northern giant hornet was detected in this county between 2019
and 2021 and was declared eradicated from the United States in December 2024. We
are glad to look at a photograph and tell you what you have.

Paper wasps under eaves and deck rails are the mildest of the three and often do
not need treating at all if they are somewhere people are not. We will say so.

## Cockroaches, and which one you have

Worth separating, because two very different insects get the same name here.

German cockroaches are indoor, human-associated, and travel in appliances,
cardboard and kitchen equipment. They breed fast and are concentrated in the
multi-unit and rental stock, and they need a coordinated building-level plan
rather than unit-by-unit treatment.

Oriental cockroaches are a damp-crawlspace insect. They live outdoors and under
buildings, come up through drains and crawl vents, and are reporting a moisture
condition rather than a hygiene one. They are common in the prewar flats for
exactly that reason.

The treatments are genuinely different, so identification comes first.
