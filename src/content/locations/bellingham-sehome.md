## A forest in the middle of a city

Sehome is unusual and the reason is Sehome Hill. The arboretum on top of it is
not a landscaped park with mown edges; it is a genuine second-growth forest,
several hundred acres of it, sitting in the middle of a dense residential
neighborhood with the university on one flank.

That produces a combination we do not see anywhere else in Bellingham: urban
housing density with rural pest pressure. A three-story apartment building on a
city street can back directly onto continuous forest, and everything that
implies comes with it.

The second defining feature is the ground. Sehome is steep. The hill falls away
toward the bay and the housing is built into it, which means split levels,
daylight basements, buildings that meet earth on several elevations at once, and
retaining walls doing structural work all over the neighborhood.

Between them, the forest and the slope account for most of what we deal with
here.

## What the arboretum delivers

A forest edge is a supply line. It runs continuous cover from deep habitat right
up to a building, and on an ordinary city block an animal has to cross open
ground to reach a house. Here, along the arboretum boundary, it does not.

**Deer mice** come off the wooded margins in a way they simply do not in the
flat parts of the city, along with Norway rats and house mice using the same
cover. They find crawl vents, gaps where a deck meets a building, service
penetrations, and the underfloor space of anything on posts.

Where droppings need clearing in an enclosed space, we will tell you to
ventilate first and avoid sweeping or vacuuming dry material. That is the
sensible precaution whatever the risk turns out to be in a given building, and
it costs nothing to do properly.

**Spiders and centipedes** are heavier here than anywhere in the city except the
lake shore, and for a straightforward reason: damp wooded ground produces
insects in quantity, and everything that eats insects follows. Residents new to
the neighborhood are frequently alarmed by the September volume.

**Carpenter ants** find the parent-colony conditions the arboretum provides in
abundance — stumps, standing dead wood, fallen limbs, and the decaying timber of
old retaining walls. On a property backing onto the forest, the parent colony is
often not on the property at all, which is a limitation worth knowing before
somebody sells you a treatment that implies otherwise.

None of this is a problem to be solved by treating the forest, and nobody should
suggest it. The pressure is permanent. What changes the outcome is the building
envelope, and that is finite work: vents screened with material that lasts, the
gap under a deck closed, branches that bridge to a roof trimmed back, grade
corrected where planting runs up against siding, and firewood moved off the
wall.

Trapping alone against a forest edge is a recurring cost rather than a result,
and we would rather say that at the first visit.

## What the slope does

Steep ground changes the building envelope in ways that matter more than people
expect.

A **daylight basement** means part of the foundation is buried and part is
exposed, and the transition between them is where water and insects both find
their way in. That line is frequently below grade on one side of a building and
above it on the other, and it is rarely detailed as carefully as either
condition would be on its own.

**Sloped crawlspaces** collect water at the low end and hold it long after the
rest has dried. The framing above that low end is what softens, which is where
the moisture ants and the decay fungus will be. On a stepped or partly excavated
crawl the access is often at the high end, which means the evidence is at the
far end from the hatch and a report written without going in is worth very
little.

**Retaining walls and terracing** hold moisture against structure. Where they
are old and wooden they become parent colony sites in their own right, which
makes them worth a specific look on any carpenter ant job here.

**Decks over grade** create a sheltered, dry, undisturbed space underneath that
is excellent harborage, and they attach at a ledger. Ledger flashing is a
chronic defect in this housing stock, and a badly flashed ledger produces
exactly the sustained wet framing that carpenter ants, moisture ants and decay
all require.

**Drainage** is the thread through all of it. Water moves downhill through soil,
arrives at an uphill foundation wall, and finds the lowest point available. A
downspout discharging onto a bank that drains straight back at the building is a
common and entirely fixable finding, and it is one we make often.

## The housing, in two layers

Sehome's stock divides roughly into two, and they behave differently.

**On the hill** there are older and frequently substantial houses, some of them
handsome turn-of-the-century properties on large lots. These carry the standard
older-Bellingham pattern — shallow crawlspaces, bare earth, vent screens that
failed decades ago, accumulated penetrations — complicated by the slope and by
mature planting held close to the buildings.

Several have been divided into units over the years, and every division created
new junctions between old framing and new. Those junctions are rarely sealed as
well as either section on its own, and they are where we find entry points more
often than anywhere else in a converted house.

**Below and around the campus** the density rises and the stock shifts toward
purpose-built apartments of several eras, plus houses let to groups. Here the
issues are more conventional in kind — service penetrations closed with expanding
foam, shared refuse areas, and the connected-structure problem described
below — but the scale is larger.

## Apartment buildings, and getting the scope right

A good share of our Sehome work is multi-unit, and it is the situation where
scope decides the outcome more than technique does.

In a building with shared structure the pests are a **building-level
population**. Wall voids, plumbing chases, shared attics and common service runs
connect units, and insects move through them regardless of who pays which lease.

Treat one unit thoroughly and the population next door is untouched. It moves
back. The tenant concludes the treatment failed; the manager concludes the
tenant reintroduced it. Neither is right — the scope was wrong.

What works is a coordinated plan: establish which units are affected, identify
the likely source, treat in one pass rather than as separate call-outs, and
follow up on an interval matched to the life cycle rather than to the invoice.

The good news specific to Sehome is that this is more practical here than in a
neighborhood of individually owned divided houses. A purpose-built block usually
has one owner or one manager, which means one decision-maker and one schedule.
When that person will authorize a building-level plan, the result is different
in kind rather than incrementally better.

**Bed bugs** and **German cockroaches** are the two that most reward this and
punish the alternative hardest. Both travel readily between units. Both are far
cheaper to resolve early and thoroughly than late and piecemeal. And with
student turnover moving furniture through these buildings twice a year, both
have a reliable route in that has nothing to do with how the building is kept.

The only visit we charge for is bed bug verification, and that charge is deducted from the treatment when you proceed. Everything else costs nothing to look at.

## Refuse in a dense neighborhood

If there is one physical thing that changes rodent outcomes here, it is the
refuse situation, and in a neighborhood of apartment buildings and shared houses
it compounds fast.

Shared bins that will not close. Collection points sized for fewer occupants
than a building actually has. Rubbish staged beside a container between
collections. Recycling that gets rained into and left. Each of those is a food
supply, and by October it is feeding a population that then goes looking for
somewhere dry.

The corrections are unglamorous and they are not ours to make: enough capacity
for the real occupancy, lids that close, a hard surface rather than ground
contact, and collection matched to the building. Managers who fix it see the
difference quickly. Those who do not are buying quarterly service against a food
source they are providing, and we would rather write that in the report than
service around it.

## The autumn, and the useful window

The seasonal pattern here is the Bellingham one with the forest edge added.

Through summer, rodents live outdoors and do well — the arboretum alone supplies
everything they need. Then the rain sets in through late September and October,
the ground saturates, burrows flood, and the whole population wants somewhere
dry within a few weeks.

Student housing at that exact moment has just refilled with people, food and
belongings, which makes it a good target at the worst possible time.

The work that prevents all of this is exclusion, and the right window is August:
dry ground, dry crawlspaces, easy exterior access, and the movement still weeks
away. In this neighborhood August is also when a great deal of the rental stock
is empty, which makes it easier to do rather than harder. That is a genuine
scheduling advantage and almost nobody uses it.

A building sealed in August does not have the winter problem. One sealed in
December has an interior problem plus whatever is now sealed inside it, and the
order of operations becomes considerably more complicated.

## Ants, and reading them properly

Two ants matter here and the difference decides the approach.

**Moisture ants** colonize wood that is already wet and already decaying. They
cannot establish in sound dry lumber, so finding them is useful information
rather than merely a nuisance — the water is the cause and the ants are the
symptom. On a sloped Sehome lot the water is usually findable: a downspout at
the foundation, a bank draining back at the building, a deck ledger with no
flashing, or the low end of a stepped crawlspace.

**Carpenter ants** prefer wood that has begun to soften and keep a parent colony
outdoors. On the arboretum edge that parent is frequently in the forest itself
rather than on the property, and where that is the case we will say so plainly
rather than implying we have solved something we have not. Where it is on the
lot — an old retaining timber, a stump, a woodpile — finding it is what makes the
treatment hold.

Come spring, the winged ones get mistaken for termite swarmers almost every time. The
quickest tell is the waist: sharply pinched on an ant, uniform on a termite.

## Spiders, and what to tell a nervous new tenant

Every September the calls start, and in a neighborhood that has just refilled
with people who have not lived through a Pacific Northwest autumn before, the
volume is high and the alarm is genuine.

They are giant house spiders. Harmless, long established across western
Washington, and the large fast ones people find are wandering males looking for
mates rather than an invasion coming inside for warmth. The ones stranded in a
bath are males that got in and could not climb out.

No brown recluse has ever been recorded here, and the species has never established anywhere in Washington. Nobody should be selling a treatment on that
basis.

What genuinely reduces the population is exterior work: clearing webbing,
dealing with harborage under decks and porches, correcting outside lighting that
concentrates the insects they feed on, and sealing door sweeps and thresholds.
On a building against the arboretum the lighting point matters more than most
people expect.

## What we will not sell you

**A single-unit treatment sold as a building solution.** If the population is
shared, we will say so before taking the work rather than after the third visit.

**Anything with fur or feathers.** Birds and wildlife are outside what we do,
and on a forest-edge property that question comes up regularly. We will tell you
what you have and point you to somebody who handles it.

**A quarterly program in place of a refuse fix.** If the bins are the food
supply, more visits are an indefinite cost.

**A promise about the arboretum.** Nothing applied to your property reduces the
population living in a forest, and any company implying otherwise is selling
something.

## How a visit here works

We start outside, and on a Sehome property that walk takes longer than it does
on flat ground. Every elevation where the building meets earth, the drainage and
where it discharges, the retaining walls and terracing, the deck and its ledger,
the vents and penetrations, the branches reaching the roof, and the forest
boundary if there is one.

Then underneath — properly, with a light, working to the low end of a stepped
crawl rather than reporting from the hatch. Then the attic, then the interior.

For an apartment building we start with the manager instead: how many units,
which are affected, what connects them, where refuse goes, and what the turnover
calendar is. That conversation is twenty minutes and it decides whether the work
resolves anything.

You get a plain account of what we found, what is driving it, what needs doing
now, what can wait, and which items belong to a builder, a landscaper or a
drainage contractor rather than to us. On a hillside property a real share of the
answer is water management, and saying so costs us the recurring service.

Looking is free for every pest we handle with the single exception of bed bugs,
and nobody is asked to sign a term agreement.

## Wasps, and the hillside problem

Wasps are worth their own note here because the terrain changes where they nest
and how dangerous that is.

**Yellowjackets** nest in the ground, and a steep neighborhood is full of the
cavities they want: banked ground, root-bound slopes, the void behind a
retaining wall, gaps in terracing, and the undisturbed strip between a fence and
a bank. A nest in a slope beside a path or a set of steps is invisible until
somebody disturbs it, and by late summer the colony is at its largest and most
defensive.

That is a genuine hazard in a neighborhood where a lot of people are walking
narrow paths and stairs between buildings, and where a good share of residents
are new to the area and would not recognize the warning signs.

**Bald-faced hornets** build the gray paper nests people photograph and send us.
On a wooded hillside they are frequently unnoticed until the leaves come down in
autumn, at which point the colony is finishing naturally anyway.

**Paper wasps** under eaves, on deck rails and in the corners of outbuildings
are the mildest, and where they are somewhere nobody goes we will often
recommend leaving them alone rather than treating for the sake of it.

The cheapest intervention by a wide margin is a walk of the eaves, decks and
ground edges in late spring, when a founding nest is the size of a golf ball and
a two-minute job. By August the same nest is a scheduling problem and a safety
one.

## Insulation, and what the autumn leaves behind

On a neighborhood with this much rodent pressure it is worth setting out what
actually happens inside a building over a winter.

Rodents do not simply pass through insulation, they tunnel and nest in it, and
they are incontinent as they travel. Over a season a run becomes a contaminated
channel rather than a single soiled patch, and compressed, soiled material has
lost most of the R-value somebody is paying to heat against.

In an attic that shows up as staining on a ceiling, a smell that is difficult to
place, and a heating bill that has quietly climbed. In a crawlspace it shows up
as insulation hanging loose, because it absorbed moisture and lost its grip, and
that loose material is then both nesting habitat and a moisture problem in its
own right.

Where remediation is needed the sequence is not negotiable: exclude first,
remove and replace second. Installing new material into a building that is still
open means paying twice, and we will say so rather than quoting the part that is
easier to sell.
