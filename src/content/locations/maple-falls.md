## Twenty-six miles up, and a different economy at the top

Maple Falls is the junction where Silver Lake Road meets the Mount Baker
Highway, and it is the last place on the way up the valley that functions as a
crossroads rather than as a turn-off. Above it the road climbs toward Glacier
and the mountain; below it the valley widens back out into the farm ground
around Deming and Nooksack.

That position matters more than the elevation does. Pest control in most of
this county is written around terrain — what grows next to the building, what
the ground does when it rains, how old the framing is. Up here the terrain is
only half of it, because a large proportion of the buildings between Maple
Falls and Glacier are not lived in by the people who own them. They are
rented by the night. Snowline, Peaceful Valley and Mt. Baker Rim are
substantially vacation-rental communities, the county's own cabins and lodge
at Silver Lake Park take bookings a mile north up Silver Lake Road, and a
great many individual properties run on a listing site.

A building that hosts thirty or forty different parties between November and
April has a pest exposure that nothing in Lynden or Ferndale has, and it is
not a worse exposure, just a different one. The things that arrive in a
suitcase do not care how well the crawlspace is sealed.

So this page is organized around that, rather than around the same forest-edge
sequence that applies ten miles down the road in [Deming](/locations/deming/).
Much of that applies here too, and the parts that do are covered below. But if
you manage a property up this valley, the section you want is the next one.

## Winter is the busy season, and that inverts the calendar

Almost every other page on this site is written for a building that is used
less in winter. Up here a great many are used more.

That single fact reverses most of the normal advice. The quiet, empty months
when exclusion work is easy to schedule are the summer ones; the months when a
problem gets discovered and has to be dealt with between bookings are the
snowy ones. A rodent coming indoors in December is coming into a building with
the heat on, a full kitchen and people in it, rather than into a shut-up house
nobody will open until May. The pressure is higher and the tolerance is lower,
because somebody is paying to be there.

It also changes when the useful work can be done. Sealing a building is
outdoor work on the outside of a wall, and from the first lasting snow until
it goes, the bottom few feet of that wall — where most of the entry points
are — cannot be seen, let alone worked on. The window for doing exclusion
properly on an upper-valley property closes earlier than it does in town and
opens later, which is why we would rather look at a cabin in September than in
January, even though January is when it is noticed.

## Bed bugs are the problem this area actually has

This is the one that distinguishes Maple Falls from everywhere else we work,
and it is worth saying plainly: bed bugs are not a sign of a dirty building,
and in a nightly rental they are not a sign of a badly run one either. They
are a sign that a lot of people have slept there.

Bed bugs move with luggage, bedding, clothing and furniture, and WSU
Extension's [PestSense fact
sheet](https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/bed-bugs/) describes the
mechanism without any of the folklore: they hitch rides and lay eggs on the
things people carry, and they move between rooms on infested items. Nothing in
that list has anything to do with how a property is kept. A cabin that takes
forty bookings a season is simply exposed forty times.

What makes them expensive up here is the turnover itself. In a house, an
infestation is found by the people living with it, usually within a few weeks,
because somebody is being bitten in the same bed every night. In a rental
nobody stays long enough to join the dots. A guest wakes up with marks, blames
the hot tub or the bedding, says nothing or leaves a review that mentions
nothing useful, and goes home — possibly with the problem in their bag. The
next party arrives to a cleaned room. By the time it is identified, it has had
a season to spread through a building and sometimes between buildings under
the same management.

The practical consequences are these. Finding it early is worth far more here
than anywhere else, because the clock runs in bookings rather than in weeks.
Over-the-counter sprays are worse than useless on a changeover, because they
scatter an infestation into adjacent rooms without ending it and resistance to
the common consumer active ingredients is widespread. And a treatment has to
be planned around the booking calendar, honestly, in advance — which means
telling a manager that a two-hour gap is not a treatment window rather than
taking the job and hoping.

Our [bed bug work](/services/bed-bug-control/) starts with a paid verification
visit for exactly this reason. It is the only pest we charge to come and look
at, and it is credited against the treatment. Up here it pays for itself more
often than it does in town, because [carpet
beetles](/pest-library/carpet-beetle/) and the small marks they leave on
bedding get reported as bed bugs regularly, and treating a building for the
wrong insect costs a great deal more than a verification visit does.

## What a changeover crew can realistically be asked to check

Cleaning crews on a turnover have between forty minutes and a couple of hours
for a whole cabin, and asking them to do a proper bed bug inspection is asking
for something that cannot be delivered. Asking them to look at four specific
things on the bed, every time, is reasonable and catches a lot.

Mattress seams and the piping around the edge, particularly at the head end.
The underside of the bed frame where the slats meet the rails. The back of the
headboard, especially where it is bolted or hangs on a cleat. And the seam of
any upholstered chair in the bedroom. What they are looking for is not
insects — those hide during the day — but small dark spotting that does not
wipe off like a stain, shed skins, and occasional live insects in the
tightest crevices.

Two more things help more than their effort suggests. Pale mattress
encasements make the evidence visible instead of hiding it in a seam, and
pitfall interceptors under the bed legs turn a question into a record that
anyone can read on the next visit. Neither treats anything. Both mean a
problem is found in the first fortnight rather than in the third month.

## Rodents, and what the snow does to the timing

Rodent work up here follows the same basic rules as anywhere in the county,
with one change: snow removes the walk from the equation.

In open country a mouse crossing to a building is exposed, and that exposure
limits how casually it happens. Under a snowpack there is a sheltered layer at
ground level that runs continuously from the brush line to the foundation, and
an animal can travel it without surfacing. A heated building at the end of
that run is the warmest, driest, best-provisioned thing for a long way, and in
a valley where most of the heated buildings have people cooking in them all
weekend, it is also the best-smelling.

So the arrival is concentrated into the weeks after the snow settles rather
than spread across the autumn, and it goes straight to the heated structure
rather than working through the outbuildings first the way it does on farm
ground further down the valley. [Deer mice](/pest-library/deer-mouse/) are the
common animal in the wooded parts, [house mice](/pest-library/house-mouse/)
around the more developed clusters, and [Norway
rats](/pest-library/norway-rat/) wherever there is sustained rubbish or feed.

The entry points are the ordinary ones and they are all low: a crawlspace vent
with failed screening, a gap where a line passes through the rim joist, the
base of a garage door, the sill under a deck. Every one of them is under the
snow line from the first real storm onward. That is the whole argument for
doing this work in the autumn, and it is the single most useful thing on this
page for anyone who owns a building up here.

If a cabin has been closed and you are opening it to droppings, do not sweep
or dry-vacuum them. The Washington State Department of Health's [hantavirus
guidance](https://doh.wa.gov/you-and-your-family/illness-and-disease-z/hantavirus)
is the reason, and the method it sets out — ventilate first, wet the material
down, lift it with gloves on — takes a few minutes longer and avoids putting
dust into the air of a room somebody is about to sleep in.

## Carpenter ants in buildings designed for snow

The upper valley has a particular building stock: steep roofs, deep eaves,
heavy exposed beams, big decks, and a great deal of cedar. All of it is a
sensible response to snow load, and most of it gives carpenter ants more of
what they want than a conventional house does.

[Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) do not eat wood; they excavate
galleries to nest in it, and they strongly prefer wood that moisture has
already softened. WSU Extension's [carpenter ant fact
sheet](https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/carpenter-ants/) is the
regional reference for the species and the point it keeps returning to is the
water. Treating ants in a wall whose wood is still wet buys you a season.

What makes the problem particular here is that the wet wood is so often
structural and exposed. A beam end sitting in a deck ledger. The outer face of
a glulam where the eave does not quite cover it. The bottom of a post that
spends four months in a drift. A roof valley on a steep pitch that sheds onto
the same corner of the deck every year. None of those are neglect — they are
the parts of a mountain building that take the most weather by design — but
they stay damp far longer than siding does, and they are where we find
galleries.

Seasonal occupancy makes it worse in one specific way. A leak in an occupied
house gets noticed in days. A leak in a cabin between bookings runs until
somebody opens the door, and a slow one behind a shower wall or around a
skylight can run most of a season. By the time it is found, the repair is a
carpentry job and the ants are a consequence rather than a cause.

The sign to act on is not the ants. It is **frass** — the shredded, fibrous
material they push out of a gallery, which collects directly beneath it on a
sill, a tread or the top of a baseboard. It is not sawdust, it often contains
insect fragments, and it tells you where to look in a way that finding an ant
on a counter never does.

## What snow does to the work after it is done

Exclusion on a mountain property has to survive things that exclusion in town
does not, and this is where cheap work gets found out.

A roof releasing a load takes the gutter with it, and often the vent screens,
the drip edge and whatever was fixed along the soffit. Hardware cloth stapled
over a crawlspace vent lasts exactly until the first slide or the first
shovel. Plow berms and shoveled piles stand wet weight against the base of a
wall for months, which rots the trim that the sealing was fastened to.
Ice at a blocked gutter backs water under the roofing, which is where several
of the damp-wood problems above actually start.

So the work we do up here is fastened into sound material, kept clear of the
shed line where it can be, and chosen in metal rather than in anything a
season of freeze and thaw will embrittle. It is also worth looking at again
after a hard winter, which is not something we would say about the same job in
Birch Bay. A property that was sealed properly in October and had six feet of
snow off the roof in January is not necessarily sealed in April.

## Wasps through the busy end of summer

Summer up here is not quiet, it is just differently busy — the valley fills up
with people walking, camping and using the trail, and the wasps reach their
largest and most defensive colonies at exactly that point in the season.

The nests that matter are the ones near where people actually stand: in the
ground beside a path or a trailhead, in the wall void by a cabin's back door,
under a deck, in the eave over a hot tub. Ground-nesting yellowjackets are the
genuine hazard rather than the nuisance, and on a property with brush being
cleared or a verge being mowed there is a lot of ground where one can be found
the hard way.

Open-combed paper wasps under an eave are usually the lesser problem and can
often be left where they are not in anybody's way. We will tell you which one
you have before treating anything.

We do not treat [honey bees](/pest-library/honey-bee/), here or anywhere, and
we will say so rather than quietly doing it. A swarm is a beekeeper's job.

## Crawlspaces, outbuildings and water that comes from a well

Crawlspaces up this valley stay damp for the same reasons they do anywhere
under a canopy, plus one more: meltwater. Ground that is frozen or saturated
under snow sheds into the perimeter rather than through it, and a crawlspace
that copes with ordinary rain can take standing water in a thaw.

The vapor barrier over the soil is the only thing between that ground and the
underside of the floor. Torn, displaced or badly lapped, it is not doing its
job, and the result is softened framing for the ants above and bedding
material for the rodents. Getting that right fixes several complaints at once
and is frequently the most useful money spent on a building out here.

Most properties up here are on a well and a septic system rather than on
municipal services, which matters for one practical reason: where we place
rodent devices and what we apply near a wellhead or a drainfield is decided by
the label and the site, not by convenience. If you are on a well, say so when
you book. It is a thirty-second conversation and it changes placement.

## The Maple Falls year

**December to March.** The busiest months. Buildings full, snow down, rodents
arriving at the heated structure, and bed bug problems surfacing in rentals at
the worst possible point in the booking calendar.

**April and May.** The thaw. Meltwater in crawlspaces, damage from the winter
becoming visible, and the first carpenter ant activity as the ground warms.
This is when a season's worth of unnoticed leaking gets found.

**June to August.** Carpenter ants at their peak and wasp colonies building
toward their largest size. The valley is full of people outdoors. This is the
right window for the repair and sealing work nobody can get at in winter.

**September to November.** The most important eight weeks of the year for
anyone who owns a building here. The walls are visible, the weather is still
workable, and everything done now is still in place when the snow arrives.

## What we will not sell you

**A bed bug treatment squeezed into a changeover that cannot hold it.** We
would rather lose the booking slot than hand the next guest a problem that has
been scattered rather than ended.

**Carpenter ant treatment with no attempt to find the water.** We will do the
treatment and tell you plainly that it is temporary while the wood stays wet.
On a mountain building that is usually a repair conversation, and sometimes it
is a roofer's conversation rather than ours.

**Exclusion fitted in a way that will not survive a roof shedding onto it.**
If the only place to seal is directly under a shed line, we will say that it
needs re-checking rather than call it permanent.

**A year-round program for a building that is used six weekends a year.** Some
properties up here genuinely need continuous service and we will say so. A
cabin used occasionally, sealed properly, usually does not.

expect and is the part worth not rushing.

If it is not urgent, send a photograph first. Identification is free, and on a
property three-quarters of an hour from our yard it is often enough to tell
you whether a visit is needed at all.
