## The six weeks that decide the winter

There is a specific, narrow, and slightly unfair fact underneath almost every
winter pest call in Whatcom County: the problem arrived during a six-week
stretch in the autumn, through openings that were there all year, and the only
cheap moment to have done something about it was several weeks before anybody
had a reason to think about it.

Late summer is when this work is worth doing. October is when the phone rings.
The distance between those two is the whole subject of this page.

This is not a subtle argument and it is not ours. University of Minnesota
Extension, writing about the insects that pile onto walls every autumn, tells
homeowners to make repairs to the openings they get in through **before the end
of August**. Penn State Extension, writing about cluster flies, says mechanical
exclusion is the best method for keeping them out of buildings, and that where an
exterior application is used it belongs in the autumn just before the flies
congregate. Neither source recommends anything for a wall cavity in January,
because there is nothing useful to recommend.

## What is actually arriving, and why it all arrives together

Two entirely separate groups move toward buildings on the same schedule, for the
same reason, through the same gaps. That coincidence is what makes late-summer
sealing such good value, because one afternoon's work addresses both.

**The overwintering insect complex.** Cluster flies, brown marmorated stink
bugs, boxelder bugs, western conifer seed bugs and ladybird beetles are
unrelated animals with different diets and different life cycles, and they all
do the identical thing in October. As nights cool, the adults that need to
survive to spring look for a sheltered crevice, gather on the elevations holding
the afternoon sun, and work inward through whatever openings already exist.
Minnesota Extension's own framing is that chilly mornings and shorter days send
them out to settle in for the winter — and that because they are not feeding
while they sit on the outside of a building, products aimed at feeding insects
have nothing to act on.

None of them can make a hole. They have no jaws for it. Every route in was
already there.

The species-level detail — how to tell them apart, what each one does, and why
none of them damages anything indoors — is on the
[brown marmorated stink bug](/pest-library/brown-marmorated-stink-bug/),
[cluster fly](/pest-library/cluster-fly/),
[boxelder bug](/pest-library/boxelder-bug/) and
[western conifer seed bug](/pest-library/western-conifer-seed-bug/) profiles,
and there is no point restating it here. What matters for this page is the
timing, which is identical across all of them.

**Rodents, on a few weeks' delay.** As nights cool and outdoor cover dies back,
a heated building stops being one option among many and becomes the best one
available. Mice come first and rats follow. Unlike the insects, they can make an
opening — a quarter-inch gap is enough to get incisors around an edge and start
removing material — which is why the standard for rodent work is a physical
barrier rather than a sealant.

Put those together and you get a single window. From roughly the third week of
September to the first week of November, a building is being probed continuously
by two different kinds of animal using the same list of openings. Everything
that is going to get in gets in during that stretch.

## Why the wall void changes everything

The reason the calendar is so unforgiving is that the barrier between "outside"
and "inside a cavity" is the only barrier that matters, and it is one-way in
practice.

Once an overwintering insect is in a wall void, a soffit run or an attic, almost
nothing reaches it. It is not feeding, so a residual has nothing to be picked up
from. It is not moving across surfaces in any consistent pattern, so contact is
a matter of luck. It is wedged in a space with no access. Minnesota Extension
does not recommend indoor insecticides for these insects at all, on the grounds
that they are not living or reproducing indoors in the first place.

And the version that appears to succeed is worse than the version that does not.
Penn State makes the point explicitly for cluster flies: killing them where they
sit leaves carcasses, carcasses feed carpet beetles and larder beetles, and the
larvae disperse into the rooms below to work on wool and stored textiles. You
would be trading a harmless nuisance for a destructive one, in a space you
cannot get into.

Rodents produce a different but equally awkward version. Seal a building with an
active population inside and the animals cannot get out, which means they die
somewhere inaccessible, which means a smell, flies and eventually opening
something up.

So the rule that governs the whole job: **the sealing has to happen before, or
it has to happen after a population is confirmed gone.** There is no version
where you close a building in late October and get a good outcome from it.

## Walking the building

Allow an hour. Go in order rather than wandering, take notes, and photograph
anything you are not going to fix today. This is roughly the order we work in.

### Foundation and rim joist

Start at one corner and go all the way round, low, looking at the band where the
foundation meets the framing above.

On the pre-war housing stock in the older Bellingham neighborhoods, this line is
where the money is. Post-and-pier construction plus eighty years of settlement
produces genuine gaps between foundation and framing that are invisible from
outside and obvious from underneath. On newer stem-wall construction the line is
tighter and the failures are localized — a form-tie hole, a crack, a place where
a contractor cut through.

Also look at the relationship between the ground and the building. Soil or bark
mulch bridging up to the siding is both an entry route and a moisture problem,
and correcting it is a shovel rather than a trade.

### Crawl vents

The single most common entry on older houses here, and the item most often
"fixed" in a way that does not hold.

Check each one for three things: whether the screen is intact, whether it is
metal, and whether the mesh is fine enough to matter. Original screens rust
through in this climate. Replacements are frequently window screen, which is an
insect material asked to do a rodent job, or coarse mesh, which stops nothing.
Also check that the vent is not buried behind landscaping or half-filled with
soil, because a blocked vent is a moisture problem before it is anything else.

The detail on this — mesh, gauge, fastening and how it interacts with required
ventilation area — is on
[crawlspace vent screening](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/crawlspace-vent-screening/).

### The crawlspace access

Warped, propped, missing, or opening into a space stacked with belongings. It
needs to close, latch, and still be openable by somebody who has to get in.

### Utility penetrations

Water, gas, electrical, cable, refrigerant lines, irrigation, and every service
added since the building went up. UC IPM lists openings around water pipes,
electric wires, sewer pipes, drain spouts and vents among the primary rodent
routes into buildings, and in our experience almost nobody seals them.

The gap around the pipe is the issue, not the pipe. A hole cut generously with a
hole saw and never closed is a standing invitation, and the contractor who ran
the line in 2019 had no reason to think about it.

Check hose bibs, exterior outlets, and anywhere a low-voltage line has been
stapled through siding.

### Dryer and bath vents

Plastic louvered covers crack, warp and stop closing, and the duct behind them
is a sheltered run straight into the building. Minnesota Extension names dryer
vents specifically in its list of what to seal against autumn invaders.

Check that the flap actually falls shut. Check the perimeter where the hood
meets the siding. And check for any disused duct that terminates in open air
behind a cover nobody has looked at in a decade.

### Soffit and fascia

This is where the overwintering insects get in, and it is why so many buildings
still have the problem after somebody has carefully sealed everything reachable
from the ground.

Look at open soffit returns, the junction where a soffit meets a wall, gaps at
the fascia line, and the open ends of vinyl siding and its J-channel. Penn State
lists cracks around windows, doors, siding, utility pipes, behind chimneys and
underneath the fascia as the openings to seal for cluster flies, and that list
maps almost exactly onto what we find.

Binoculars from the ground are a legitimate first pass. A ladder is the second.

### Roofline, vents and chimney

Gable vents and roof vents with damaged or coarse screening. Any point where a
lower roof meets an upper wall, which on a house with an addition is often a
genuine hole rather than a gap. Chimney flashing, and the chimney crown itself.
Roof penetrations for plumbing vents and flues.

Anything overhanging — a branch touching the roof, a climber on the siding — is
a route as well as an opening, and cutting it back is part of this job.

### Doors, sweeps and thresholds

Every exterior door, and particularly the ones nobody thinks of as exterior: the
garage side door, a basement door, a shop or utility door. Stand inside with the
door closed and look for daylight underneath.

Minnesota Extension's advice is to install door sweeps or thresholds on all
exterior entry doors, and this is the highest return per dollar of anything on
this list. It is also the item that wears out — a sweep on a door used forty
times a day has a service life measured in a few years.

### The garage

Worth its own stop, because it is usually treated as outside the building and is
usually connected to it.

The bottom corners of a sectional door essentially never seal, and the gap there
is exactly the size that matters. The seal along the bottom of the panel
hardens and stops conforming to an uneven slab. And the wall between the garage
and the house is frequently penetrated for services without anybody closing the
holes, so a garage that admits mice admits them to the kitchen a day later.

## Materials, which is where most of this fails

Correct material fastened badly is a flap, not a barrier. Wrong material
fastened well is worse, because it looks finished.

**Quarter-inch hardware cloth** is the workhorse for rodents. UC IPM's rule is
that every opening larger than a quarter inch should be closed to exclude both
rats and mice, and it is emphatic that lightweight chicken wire is not a
candidate — rats pass through the openings. Its own material specifications run
to galvanized wire cloth and expanded metal in the 16 to 19 gauge range, which
is the useful part of the guidance: mesh size decides what gets through, gauge
decides whether it survives being chewed.

Quarter-inch mesh is the right call rather than half-inch because half-inch
stops rats and passes mice, and a building sealed against only one of the two is
not sealed.

**And here is the trade-off worth understanding.** Quarter-inch hardware cloth
stops rodents and passes air, which is exactly what a crawl vent needs. It is
also an open door to a wasp queen looking for a wall void in spring, and to
every insect on the overwintering list. Where a single opening has to do both
jobs, the answer is to back the rodent mesh with a finer insect screen and
accept a small reduction in airflow. Screening for rodents alone on a vent that
has historically produced wasps is solving half a problem and calling it done.

**Sheet metal** anywhere something will be chewed at directly: door bottoms,
corners, edges, the leading edge of anything a rodent can get purchase on.

**Copper mesh** as packing behind a sealant, because it does not rust out the
way steel wool does in this climate.

**Mortar and masonry patch** for concrete and block.

**Caulk and insect screen** are sufficient — genuinely sufficient — for the
overwintering insects on their own. They cannot chew, so a sealed joint holds.
Minnesota Extension recommends silicone, elastomeric latex or silicone-acrylic
caulk, spray foam or copper mesh for larger spaces, and sealing masonry openings
of an eighth of an inch or larger. On a soffit run or a window-to-siding joint
where rodents are not a factor, this is the whole material list.

What does not work on its own: expanding foam as a barrier, which a rat removes
in an evening; steel wool alone, which rusts out and leaves a hole and a stain;
window screen over a rodent-sized opening; and any of the above stapled loosely
across a hole rather than cut oversize and fastened to sound substrate. Most
failed exclusion comes apart at the top edge within a year, and it comes apart
because of fastening rather than material.

## Screening is not blocking

One error is common enough to warrant its own warning, and it is usually made by
somebody being conscientious.

A crawl vent is not a defect. It is part of a moisture system, and Washington's
code sets a minimum ventilation area for the under-floor space precisely because
the alternative is a space that cannot shed what the ground puts into it.
Blocking vents with foam board, plastic sheet or expanding foam because rodents
were coming through them trades a rodent problem for a decay problem, and decay
is the more expensive of the two by a wide margin.

The same logic applies at the roof. Soffit and roof vents are there to move air
through the attic. Closing them because insects were getting in produces
condensation, damp insulation and eventually the conditions for exactly the
organisms this whole exercise is meant to keep out.

So the operation is screening, not blocking. Every opening that exists for
ventilation stays open and gets a barrier across it that passes air and stops
animals. Every opening that exists for no reason — a redundant duct, an
abandoned penetration, a hole somebody cut and never closed — gets closed
outright.

If you are unsure which category an opening falls into, the test is simple: ask
what it was for. If the answer is airflow, screen it. If nobody can say what it
was for, it is a hole.

The longer version of why this matters in this climate is
[why crawlspaces here stay wet](/guides/why-crawlspaces-here-stay-wet/), which is
the moisture side of the same building.

## What to do yourself, and what to hand over

There is a real division here and it is mostly about height and confined space.

**Reasonable to do yourself.** Door sweeps and thresholds. Garage door seals.
Sealing around hose bibs, outlets and low-voltage lines. Caulking window and
door perimeters at ground level. Clearing soil and mulch away from siding.
Cutting back vegetation touching the walls. Replacing an accessible crawl vent
screen where the opening is sound and reachable. Closing the interior side of
things — around a recessed light housing, a bathroom fan surround, an attic
hatch, the gap behind baseboards on an exterior wall — which Penn State
specifically recommends to stop insects emerging out of a cavity into rooms.

That last category deserves emphasis because it is undervalued. It does not stop
anything entering the building, but it stops it entering the room, and for most
households that is the part actually experienced.

**Worth handing over.** Anything on a ladder, which is most of the soffit,
fascia and roofline work and is where the overwintering insects are getting in.
Anything under the house, where the work is on your back in limited clearance
and the gaps are the ones nobody has ever seen. Anything where the substrate has
gone soft, because screening over decayed wood is cosmetic. And anything on a
building that already has something living in it, because then the job has a
sequence and getting the sequence wrong is the expensive mistake.

## Sequence, which matters more than thoroughness

Three steps, in this order, always.

**Check first.** Before closing anything, establish whether the building already
has a population. Droppings, gnawing, staining along a run, insect accumulation
at windows in the previous winter, wasp traffic at a specific point on the
siding. This is a genuinely different question from where the openings are, and
it is the one people skip.

**Deal with what is inside.** Trap, confirm quiet, then move on. On an empty
structure — a vacant rental at turnover, a seasonal property before it closes
for the winter — this step disappears and sealing can start immediately.

**Then close, from the ground up.** Foundation line, vents, penetrations, doors,
then the roofline. The order is practical rather than doctrinal: the low work is
faster, and finishing the roofline last means the ladder comes out once.

## The honest position on the timing

This page argues against our own October.

October is the busiest month in this trade, and a good share of it is buildings
that would not have needed us if somebody had spent an afternoon on them in
August. Publishing that is not generosity; it is the position we would rather
compete on. A building sealed in late summer is a customer who calls us about
the next house, and a customer paying a subscription to manage a problem that a
day of work would have addressed is a customer with a reason to leave.

The uncomfortable part is that August is the month in which nothing is wrong.
There is no scratching in the wall, no insect at the window, no reason to think
about it. That is precisely why it works, and it is also why almost nobody
books it.

If you take one thing away: **put a reminder in the calendar for the third week
of July.** Not because anything happens in July, but because it is the last
point at which you can book the work and still have it done in time.

## If it is already autumn

The advice does not become useless, it just changes shape.

Do the checking step properly, because it now matters. Close what can be closed
without trapping anything inside — door sweeps, thresholds, garage corners,
utility penetrations at ground level, anything on a wall that has no cavity
behind it. Leave the wall voids and the roofline until you know what is in them.
Deal with what is already inside on its own terms: vacuum the insects rather
than spraying them, and trap rodents before sealing.

Then put the full walk in the diary for July. A single winter of noting which
windows produce insects and which walls they gather on outside is genuinely
useful information for next summer's scope, and it is free.
