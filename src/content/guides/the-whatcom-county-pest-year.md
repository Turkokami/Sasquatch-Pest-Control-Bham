## Pest problems here run on a calendar

Almost nothing that happens in a Whatcom County building is a surprise. The
insects and animals that turn up follow the weather, the weather here is
consistent enough to plan around, and the same calls arrive in the same weeks
every year.

That has a practical consequence worth stating at the top. **Almost every
problem people call about in October could have been prevented in August**, for
less money, in better weather, and before anything got inside a wall.

Nobody does that, because in August nothing is wrong. This page is an attempt to
make the calendar visible enough that somebody might.

## January and February

**Quiet for arrivals, busy for discoveries.**

Nothing much is moving into buildings. What people notice now is what got in
during the autumn: rodents established in a crawlspace or an attic, and
overwintering insects — cluster flies, stink bugs, boxelder bugs, ladybird
beetles — roused by a mild spell and appearing at upstairs windows. Those are
not new arrivals. They are the autumn population being woken by warm weather,
and they will keep reappearing on every warm day until spring.

Wasp and hornet colonies are gone. Only mated queens survive, dormant somewhere
sheltered, and the paper nests in the trees are empty.

Decay in wet timber is slow but not stopped. The Forest Products Laboratory puts
decay fungi at their optimum near 77°F, slowing markedly below 50°F. A crawlspace
in February is at the cold end of that band — which means the process is
continuing quietly rather than pausing.

**What to do:** interior work. Sealing the penetrations under sinks, behind
appliances and at cabinet bases, where a crawlspace connects to a kitchen. It is
warm, dry, indoor work and it is genuinely useful. Also: plan the summer
exterior work now, while last autumn is fresh enough to remember.

## March and April

**The ground warms and things start moving.**

Ant activity resumes as colonies become active. The first wave of kitchen ant
calls follows the first stretch of warm days, and the second, larger wave
follows heavy rain — shallow outdoor nests flood and the colony moves to higher,
drier ground, which is frequently the inside of a building.

Overwintering insects that survived the winter become active and try to leave.
This produces a second wave of indoor sightings that looks like a new problem
and is the end of the old one.

Wasp queens emerge and begin founding nests. Each one is a single insect
building something the size of a golf ball, and this is the cheapest and safest
moment in the entire year to deal with a nest that would otherwise be a
four-thousand-insect problem in September.

Spider egg sacs hatch and enormous numbers of spiderlings disperse. Almost
nobody notices.

**What to do:** walk the property looking for founding wasp nests, in eaves,
sheds, under rails and in the untended margins. Ten minutes in April is worth a
great deal in September.

## May and June

**Growth, and the best month nobody uses.**

Insect populations build. Wasp colonies expand quietly. Carpenter ant activity
becomes visible — this is when frass appears below trim and workers turn up on
counters after dark, because the colony is provisioning.

Dampwood termite and moisture ant findings surface as people open up buildings
for summer work and look at timber they have not looked at since last year.

The ground begins to dry, which makes crawlspace and exterior work practical for
the first time since autumn.

**What to do:** this is when the crawlspace work should happen. Ground cover,
drainage, downspouts, grade, clearing buried vents. Dry ground and long days,
and none of it is possible in December. Also the moment to book anything a
contractor is involved in, before their season fills.

## July and August

**The peak of everything outdoors, and the window that matters.**

Wasp and yellowjacket colonies are growing steeply. Foraging shifts from protein
to sugar through August, which is when they arrive at fallen fruit, bins and
outdoor eating.

Carpenter ants are at maximum visible activity.

Spider populations are building toward autumn, feeding on the summer's insects.
The number that make it to maturity is set now, which is why a warm summer
reliably produces a heavy September.

Ants may come indoors looking for water rather than food during a dry stretch,
which is why a spotless kitchen can get a trail in August with nothing left out.

**What to do, and this is the single most important paragraph on this page:**

**The second half of August is the window.** Exterior sealing before the rodent
push. Crawl vents rescreened in quarter-inch metal. Door sweeps replaced,
particularly on garage and basement doors. Roofline and soffit openings closed
before overwintering insects arrive. A walk of the grounds for yellowjacket
ground nests before autumn yard work finds them the hard way. Any exterior
treatment for overwintering insects, which works applied before they move in and
is largely wasted afterwards.

Everything on that list is cheaper, easier and more effective in August than at
any other time, and every item on it prevents a call we will otherwise take
between September and February.

## September

**The month everything arrives at once.**

The spider wave begins. Mature males leave their webs to find females, they
travel on foot, and they walk into buildings. It reads as an infestation and it
is a mating season lasting three to six weeks.

Yellowjacket and hornet colonies reach maximum size and maximum defensiveness,
in exactly the weeks people are outside doing late-season yard work. Nearly every
serious sting incident we hear about happens in a four-week window now.

The rodent push starts as nights cool. Cover dies back, natural food declines,
and a heated building with a quarter-inch gap becomes a considerably better
proposition than it was in July.

Overwintering insects begin gathering on sunlit south and west walls.

In Bellingham specifically, student move-in concentrates travel, second-hand
furniture and turnover into a few weeks, which is the local bed bug season.

**What to do:** if August was missed, do it now anyway — later is worse than
August and much better than November. Check the building before sealing, because
closing a building with something already inside is the one mistake that makes
things worse.

## October and November

**The peak of the indoor year.**

Rodent pressure at its height and staying there. This is when the scratching in
the walls starts, when droppings appear in kitchens, and when crawlspaces get
occupied.

Overwintering insects move into wall voids, attics and soffits, and the walls go
quiet again.

Spiders taper through October and are largely finished by November.

Hornet nests become visible as leaves fall — and almost always the colony is
already finishing, which is why the honest answer to a nest discovered now is
usually to leave it.

Wasp colonies collapse with the first hard frosts.

**What to do:** respond rather than prevent, because the prevention window has
closed. Trap before sealing. And note what happened and where, because that note
is what makes next August productive.

## December

**Rodents settled in, everything else dormant.**

What is inside is inside. Populations in crawlspaces and attics continue through
the winter because the building supplies what the season does not.

Overwintering insects appear indoors on warm days.

**What to do:** deal with what is active, and resist the temptation to seal a
building with a live population in it.

## The same year, by pest

The month-by-month view above is how the year feels from inside a building.
Here is the same information organized by organism, which is more useful if you
already know what you are dealing with.

**Rodents.** Pressure climbs from late September, peaks October and November,
and stays elevated all winter because the building supplies what the season does
not. Effectively no seasonal decline once they are inside. Prevention window:
August. Response window: whenever you notice, and sooner is much cheaper.

**Carpenter ants.** Visible activity from spring through late summer, peaking as
the weather warms and again around the swarm period. A single large ant indoors
in February is more significant than one in July, because it suggests something
overwintering inside rather than wandering in. Prevention: keep timber dry and
keep woodpiles, stumps and fallen wood away from the building, any time of year.

**Moisture ants and dampwood termites.** No meaningful season, because the
driver is a wet building rather than the weather. Found whenever somebody
finally looks. Prevention: the crawlspace work in May or June.

**Yellowjackets and hornets.** A single queen in spring, steep growth through
July and August, maximum size and defensiveness late August into September,
colony collapse with the first hard frosts. Nests are never reused. Prevention:
a walk of the property in April, when a nest is one insect.

**Spiders.** Populations build all summer; males mature and walk from late
August into October; over by November. Prevention: door sweeps and exterior
lighting, done in August.

**Overwintering insects** — cluster flies, stink bugs, boxelder bugs, ladybird
beetles, conifer seed bugs. Gathering on sunlit walls late September into
October, dormant in voids through winter, roused by warm days, leaving in
spring. Prevention: roofline and wall sealing plus an exterior treatment, late
August. Nothing works once they are inside.

**Household ants.** Spring activity, a larger wave after heavy rain at any point
in the season, and a further push in autumn. Driven by weather more than by
anything in your kitchen.

**Bed bugs.** No weather season at all — they travel with people. The local
pattern is a September peak in Bellingham driven by student move-in and
second-hand furniture, and a June echo when it all goes back on the curb.

**Decay fungi.** Active whenever timber is wet and temperatures are above about
50°F, which in this county is most of the year. The one genuinely continuous
process on this page, and the most expensive.

## If you only do one walk a year

Make it late July or early August, allow an hour, and look at these in order.

**The foundation line, all the way round.** Every crawl vent: is it clear of
soil and landscaping, is the screen intact, is it metal? The crawl access: does
it actually close and latch? Every pipe and cable where it enters the building.

**The ground itself.** Does it fall away from the building? Is soil bridging up
to the siding? Where does each downspout discharge, and is it far enough out?

**Doors.** Every exterior door, especially the garage side door and any basement
door. Can you see daylight underneath?

**The roofline**, from the ground with binoculars if need be. Soffit and fascia
junctions, gable and roof vents, anywhere a lower roof meets an upper wall.

**Vegetation.** Anything touching the walls or overhanging the roof. Climbers on
siding.

**The untended margin** — the strip along a fence line or treeline that never
gets attention. This is where ground nests are, and where stored material has
been sitting on soil since spring.

**Under the house**, if you are willing. Ground cover intact and lapped, no
standing water, sill plate firm under a probe.

Write down what you find. That list, actioned in the last two weeks of August,
is the whole of preventive pest control in this county.

## The pattern underneath

Three things drive nearly all of that.

**Temperature.** It sets when insects become active, when they overwinter, when
colonies collapse and when rodents look for shelter. Our long mild autumn
stretches the transition over weeks rather than days, which is why the season
here has a long shoulder rather than a sharp edge.

**Moisture.** The wet season from October to April keeps timber wet, and wood
that gets wet here does not get a hot dry stretch to dry out in. That is the
engine behind every structural pest problem in the county, and it operates all
winter while everything else is quiet.

**Resource availability outdoors.** Cover, food and water. When they decline in
autumn, buildings become comparatively attractive. Nothing about the building
changed; the alternative got worse.

## What the preceding season predicts

Year-to-year variation is real and it is mostly set before you notice it.

A warm summer with good insect numbers produces more spiders reaching maturity,
and therefore a heavier September.

A wet spring floods more shallow nests and produces more ant incursions.

A mild winter allows more overwintering survival across several species.

Local disturbance — clearing, construction, a hedge coming out, a neighboring
property developed — displaces populations and pushes them into buildings within
a couple of hundred feet, on whatever schedule the work happens.

None of that is predictive enough to plan around precisely. It is enough to know
that a heavy autumn usually had a warm summer behind it, and that this is not a
sign anything is wrong with your house.

## Why the calendar differs across the county

Whatcom County is not one place, and the same month lands differently depending
on where a building sits. The differences are consistent enough to plan around.

**Wooded lots and the greenbelt edge.** Everything arrives earlier and heavier.
More carpenter ants, because the parent-colony material — stumps, deadwood,
fallen timber — is permanently available. More spiders, because damp wooded
ground produces insects in volume. Deer mice rather than only house mice. The
autumn wave starts a week or two sooner and runs harder.

**Waterfront and near-water property.** Wind-driven rain on exposed elevations,
salt air shortening the life of vent screens and flashing, and a permanent
rodent corridor along any shoreline or creek margin. Rat pressure here is closer
to constant than seasonal, because the corridor supplies water, food and cover
through the winter.

**Farm and acreage.** Feed is the dominant factor and it operates year-round.
Outbuildings multiply the number of ground-nest sites and the number of rodent
harborages. The wasp risk in late summer is materially higher because people are
operating machinery in exactly the ground where nests are.

**Older in-town neighborhoods.** Small lots mean a neighbor's compost heap,
feeder or neglected outbuilding is within a rat's normal range. The building
stock is the leakiest — original crawl vents, additions from three different
decades, and trim that has moved.

**Newer subdivisions.** Better sealed, so fewer entries, but often built on
ground that was cleared recently. Disturbance displaces established populations
into the nearest structures, which is why a new development can have a heavier
first two autumns than it ever has afterwards.

**Low-lying ground near the rivers and sloughs.** The water table responds to
the season, so crawlspaces that are merely damp in August hold standing water in
February. Everything moisture-driven is worse and earlier here.

None of that changes the shape of the year. It changes the amplitude, and it
changes which items on the August list matter most for your particular building.

## The version of this you can act on

If you take one thing from the calendar, take this: **there are two months that
decide the year, and neither of them is the month you will feel like calling.**

**May or June** for the crawlspace: ground cover, drainage, downspouts, grade,
vents cleared. Dry ground, practical weather.

**Late August** for the envelope: vent screening, door sweeps, roofline and
soffit openings, the grounds walk for ground nests, and any exterior treatment
for overwintering insects.

Do those two and the autumn is a quieter, cheaper season. Skip them and you will
be phoning somebody in October along with everybody else, at the busiest point
in the year, to deal with something already inside a wall.

We are aware this argues against our own October revenue. It is still the right
advice, and a building sealed in August is a customer we would rather have than
a subscription we have to keep justifying.
