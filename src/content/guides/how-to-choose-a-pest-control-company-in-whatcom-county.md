## What a useful version of this page looks like

Most articles on choosing a pest control company are written by pest control
companies and list criteria the writer happens to meet. That is an advertisement
with a numbered list in it.

The test of whether a page like this is worth anything is simple: does it name a
criterion the writer fails? This one does, and the failure is in the section on
licensing, which is the section that matters most. Read that part first if you
are only going to read one.

Everything else here is what we would tell a friend who asked, including the
parts that send them elsewhere.

## Start with the license, because it is free and public

Washington licenses this trade, and the licenses are a matter of public record.
The Washington State Department of Agriculture runs a license search on its own
site, open to anybody, at no cost. There is no reason to take a company's word
for its licensing when the state will tell you directly.

Two things are worth knowing before you look.

**The license types are different things, not seniority grades.** The Washington
Pesticide Application Act defines a commercial pesticide applicator as "any
person who engages in the business of applying pesticides to the land of
another," and a commercial pesticide operator as "any employee of a commercial
pesticide applicator who uses or supervises the use of any pesticide and who is
required to be licensed." One attaches to the business of applying; the other
attaches to an individual doing the work. A company's crew may be licensed as
operators. That is normal and it is not a lesser thing.

**A license carries categories, and the categories are the useful part.** A
Washington pest control license record shows the license type, its status, its
expiration date, and the categories the holder carries. For structural work the
two that matter are PCO General, which covers general structural and household
pest control, and PCO Structural, which WSDA describes as the control of pests
that attack structural material — fungus, termites, carpenter ants and
wood-boring beetles.

That second one is worth asking about specifically. If your problem is in the
timber of the building rather than on the kitchen counter, a company whose
licensing does not include the structural category is not the right company for
it.

**Ask for the number, then look it up yourself.** Not because most companies are
lying, but because it takes two minutes and a company that gets awkward about
the request has answered a different and more important question.

## The credential most people do not know exists

Here is the part that decides whether this page is honest.

There is a separate Washington license called a structural pest inspector
license, created under a different statute from the one that licenses pesticide
application. RCW 15.58.205 provides that no individual may perform services as a
structural pest inspector, or advertise that they perform those services,
without holding that license from the director. The same section adds a second
requirement that surprises people: that license "is not valid for conducting a
complete wood destroying organism inspection unless the inspector owns or is
employed by a business with a structural pest inspection company license."

So there are two credentials there, an individual one and a company one, and
both are needed before anybody can produce the report a property sale runs on.
State law defines a complete WDO inspection as one carried out "for the purpose
of determining evidence of infestation, damage, or conducive conditions as part
of the transfer, exchange, or refinancing of any structure in Washington state."

**We do not hold either of those credentials.** We hold treatment licenses. If a
lender, a buyer, an escrow officer or a real estate agent has asked you for a
wood-destroying organism report on a sale, the document has to come from a
licensed inspector working for a company that holds the inspection company
license, and that is not us. We will tell you that on the phone rather than
after taking a booking, and the same is true if you call us in a panic three
days before closing.

What we are certified to do is the treatment of the organisms an inspector's
report identifies, which is separate work and is usually the part that comes
next.

This distinction is not a technicality and it is the most common way a pest
control company misleads a homeowner in this state, usually by accident. The
word "inspection" gets used loosely by everyone, including us in the sense of a
free assessment before a quote, and it is not the same thing as the regulated
activity above. If a transaction depends on it, ask directly whether the company
holds the structural pest inspector credential and the matching company license,
and ask the same question of us.

## The questions that actually separate good from bad

Long question lists are easy to produce and hard to use. These five do most of
the work.

**"What species is it?"** Ask before any figure is discussed. The answer should
be a species or a straightforward "I want to look at this properly before I say."
"Ants" is not an answer — odorous house ants, moisture ants and carpenter ants
are three different jobs with three different prices and one of them is really a
building repair. A company that quotes before identifying is quoting a product,
not solving a problem.

**"What is driving it?"** There is always a reason: food, water, harborage, or a
way in. If nobody can name the driver, the treatment is aimed at the symptom and
you will be having this conversation again. In this county the driver is
frequently water — a blocked crawl vent, a downspout at the foundation, a torn
ground cover — and a company that never looks under the house will not find it.

**"What happens if it comes back?"** The useful version of this question is not
whether they will return but on what terms, and for how long, and written down
where. Ask what the stated conditions are and whether they are published
anywhere you can read before you sign. Our own re-service terms are set out in
full, with their limits, on a page you can read [before you buy
anything](/our-guarantee/). Any company can put a promise in an advertisement.
The conditions are where you find out what the promise is worth.

**"Is there a contract, and how do I leave?"** The length of the term matters
less than the exit. Ask about the cancellation notice, any early termination
charge, and whether a discounted or free initial visit becomes repayable if you
cancel. That last one catches people. Ask before signing; afterward is not a
negotiation.

**"Who is actually coming, and what will they do on the visit?"** A route
technician doing a twenty-minute lap of the building is a different service from
someone spending an hour with a light in the crawlspace, and both get sold under
the same words.

## Red flags, in rough order of how much they should worry you

**A quote without a visit.** For anything beyond a wasp nest you can point at,
this is not a price. The two variables that move a job most in this county —
what the crawlspace is like, and how open the building is — cannot be judged
from a phone call. A figure given before either is known will move once someone
arrives.

**Pressure at the door.** Door-to-door selling is legal and some of it is honest,
but the combination of an unscheduled visit, a same-day discount, and an
inspection of your property offered on the spot is the standard structure of a
bad deal. Nothing in this trade is genuinely urgent to the hour. Take the card,
look up the license, call back tomorrow.

**Fear-based selling.** Watch for a diagnosis that arrives with an emotional
charge attached: your home is being eaten, this is a health emergency, this
spreads to the neighbors. Some pest problems are genuinely serious and a
competent technician will say so calmly and be able to explain why. If the
argument is doing more work than the evidence, be careful.

**Species that do not live here presented as your problem.** Brown recluse and
black widow both come up in local sales conversations and neither is established
here. So does "termites" used as a single scary noun, when the species we
actually deal with in Whatcom County are dampwood and subterranean termites,
with quite different biology and quite different implications. A company that
does not know which one it is looking at is not qualified to tell you what it
means.

**A promise that sounds absolute.** Nobody can commit to a building never having
another insect in it, and any assurance phrased that way either has conditions
that are not being shown to you or is not going to be honored. The useful
version is narrow, written and specific.

**Reluctance about the paperwork.** A licensed applicator has to keep records of
what was applied and has to give the owner of the treated land a copy. Hesitation
about that is a serious signal.

**No mention of anything except a treatment.** On a Whatcom County house, an
assessment that produces no observation about drainage, ventilation, grade or an
open gap somewhere is an assessment that did not look very hard.

## What a license does not tell you

Worth saying, because the licensing section above could give the wrong
impression. A license is a floor, not a recommendation.

What it establishes is that somebody passed the examinations, carries the
categories, keeps up the continuing education and pays the fees. Those are real
things and a company without them should be ruled out immediately. What it says
nothing about is whether the technician will go into your crawlspace, whether
anyone will identify the ant, whether the recommendation will be honest, or
whether the company answers the phone in February.

So the license check is a screen you run first and quickly. Everything after it
is judgment, and the questions in the next section are how you exercise it.

## On price, briefly

Price is the thing people want to lead with and the thing that tells you least
early on, because two quotes for the same problem are usually two different
jobs. What is included, whether repair work is in or out, how many visits, and
what the terms are on coming back all move the figure more than any margin does.

That subject has [its own page here](/guides/what-drives-the-cost-of-pest-control/),
which sets out what actually moves a number in this county and how to read a
quote against another one. The relevant point for choosing a company is narrower:
a figure produced without a visit is not comparable to one produced after
somebody has been under the house, and treating them as though they were is how
people end up disappointed by the cheaper of the two.

## Franchise or local, which is the wrong question

People ask this a lot and it is not the axis that decides anything.

A national operation brings scale, availability, standardized procedures and
somebody to escalate to. A local one brings knowledge of this specific housing
stock, this climate and this county's seasons, and usually the same technician
each visit. Both models produce good companies and bad ones.

The things that actually predict quality — does anybody identify the species,
does anybody look at the building, is the recommendation honest, are the terms
written down — cut straight across the distinction. Apply the criteria to the
company in front of you rather than to the category it belongs to.

The one place the distinction genuinely bites here is regional knowledge. A
technician trained on a national curriculum in a dry state can be perfectly
competent and still not know that the explanation for most of what happens in
this county is under the floor and made of water. Ask about Whatcom County
specifically and listen for whether the answer is local.

## Insurance and the practical questions

Ask whether the company carries general liability insurance and whether it can
produce a certificate. Most can, most will, and property managers and commercial
accounts should ask as a matter of routine rather than as a challenge.

The other practical items, none of them glamorous: is the vehicle marked, does
the technician identify themselves, are appointments given as a time or as a
day, does anybody tell you afterward what was done, and does the phone get
answered by a person who can find your account. Those are not quality signals
about pest biology. They are signals about whether the business is run, and a
business that is not run will fail you at the point where you need it.

## What you are entitled to afterward

Washington is specific about this and most customers do not know it.

A commercial pesticide applicator must keep records of each application
including the location, the year, month, day and the beginning and ending times,
the product name as it appears on the registered label, the EPA registration
number, the crop or site treated, the amount applied, the concentration, and the
name of the individual who made the application. Those records must be kept for
no less than seven years, and the applicator must provide a copy to the owner of
the land the pesticide was applied to.

That is a record of what was done. It is worth asking for, worth keeping, and
worth reading — the EPA registration number lets you look up the label yourself
and read the same safety directions the technician was working to.

## Reviews, and what they can and cannot tell you

Read the text, not the score. Almost every company in this trade shows a high
average, so the number carries very little signal. What carries signal is the
content: did the problem actually resolve, did the company come back when asked,
how do they respond when somebody is unhappy, and are the detailed reviews
describing the same service you are being sold.

A negative review answered like an adult is worth more than a page of five-word
praise. And in a county this size, the most reliable source is still somebody on
your street who had the same problem last winter.

## Where we are the wrong choice

This is the section that makes the rest of the page worth reading, so it is
specific rather than modest.

**A wood-destroying organism report for a property sale.** Covered above. We do
not hold the inspector credential or the company license and cannot produce that
document. Call a licensed inspector.

**Wildlife.** Birds, bats, squirrels, raccoons, opossums. Not our work, not our
licensing, and a company that offers everything is usually good at less of it
than it claims.

**Mosquitoes and ticks.** We do not sell treatment for either. In this county
mosquito problems are usually a standing-water problem on or near the property,
which is a drainage conversation rather than a spraying one.

**Free assessment, with one exception.** The pre-service visit is free for
everything except bed bugs, which carry a paid verification visit — that one is
credited against the treatment if you proceed, but it is a real charge if you
only want the visit. We would rather say that on this page than have it appear
on an invoice.

**Formal monitoring on a single-family home.** Logged trap counts and trend
reporting are a commercial-account service. On an ordinary house, from us or
anyone else, "monitoring" means a technician checking the same places each visit.

**Scale.** We are a local company. In late September and October, when the
rodent calls start, the schedule tightens. If you need somebody within
twenty-four hours during the autumn peak, a larger operation may genuinely serve
you better, and being honest about that is cheaper than missing an appointment.

**Anything upstream of the pest.** Where the answer is a drainage contractor, a
plumber, a builder or an insulation company, that is what we will tell you, and
we will not sell you a recurring plan as a substitute for it.

## If something goes wrong

Most disagreements in this trade are about expectations rather than
competence, and most of them resolve in one phone call if the call happens
early. Say what you expected, say what happened, and ask what they propose. Do
it in writing as well as by phone, dated, and keep the copy.

If that does not work, the agency that issues these licenses is the agency that
enforces the statutes behind them. The Washington State Department of
Agriculture is the regulator for pesticide application in this state, and the
license record you looked up at the start is held by the same department that
takes complaints about the work.

Two things make a complaint effective and both are things you have to do at the
time rather than afterward: keep the application records you were given, and
photograph the situation with dates. A dated record beats a recollection every
time, in this as in every other dispute.

## Buying this for a business

Briefly, because a commercial buyer is asking a different set of questions.

The license check is the same. What is added is documentation: a device map, a
record of each visit, trend information over time, and material available when
an auditor or a health inspector asks for it. If you are running a food
business, a care setting, a school or a warehouse, ask specifically what the
service produces on paper, because that is half of what you are buying and it is
the half that fails audits when it is missing.

Also ask who covers you when your usual technician is on vacation, how emergency
calls are handled outside normal hours, and whether the company has worked in
your type of facility before. A residential route service scaled up is not the
same thing as a commercial program.

## A short checklist

Take this to whichever company you call, including this one.

Look up the license yourself on the state's site. Ask which categories it
carries. Ask whether they hold the structural pest inspector credential if a
sale depends on it. Insist on a visit before a price. Ask for the species, not
the group. Ask what is driving it. Ask what happens if it returns, and where
those conditions are written. Ask how you cancel and what it costs. Ask for the
application record afterward. And notice whether anybody ever tells you that
something is not needed.

The last one is the strongest signal available. Recommending work is what every
company does. Declining work is a demonstration of the judgment you are actually
paying for, and it is much harder to fake than a review score.
