## Which wasps and hornets actually live in Washington

Fewer than the internet suggests, and the dangerous list is shorter still.

WSU Hortsense sets out the family for this state, and it divides cleanly into
two halves. On one side, the social wasps: German, western and aerial
yellowjackets, the bald-faced hornet, native paper wasps, the introduced
European paper wasp and the native golden paper wasp. These build colonies,
they defend them, and they account for essentially every sting anybody in this
county is going to receive.

On the other side, the solitary wasps — potter and mason wasps, mud daubers,
digger wasps, grass-carrying wasps — which WSU notes make up most of the family
by species count. Each female works alone, provisions a few cells and leaves.
There is no colony to defend, no alarm response, and almost no reason for one of
them to sting a person.

That split is the single most useful thing on this page. Nearly every anxious
phone call about a large frightening wasp turns out to be a solitary species
going about its business, and nearly every genuine problem turns out to be a
yellowjacket colony in the ground or in a wall.

## Yellowjackets, which are what people usually mean

Stout, banded yellow and black, in numbers, and the reason wasp control exists.

WSU names three for Washington. The **western yellowjacket** is the native one
and the usual culprit in a lawn or bank — the species page is at
[western yellowjacket](/pest-library/western-yellowjacket/). The **German
yellowjacket** is introduced and is the one most associated with wall voids and
structures. The **aerial yellowjacket** is a *Dolichovespula* rather than a
*Vespula*, builds a papery gray nest out in the open in a shrub or under an
eave, and is on the library at
[aerial yellowjacket](/pest-library/aerial-yellowjacket/).

Yellowjacket nests hold thousands of wasps by late season, and WSU describes
them as nesting underground or behind walls. Both of those placements are the
difficult ones, for opposite reasons: a ground nest is where somebody mows, and
a wall nest is where nobody can see what is happening.

The two problem scenarios each have their own page, because the right answer
differs completely:
[yellowjackets nesting in the lawn](/services/wasp-control/yellowjackets-in-the-lawn/)
and
[a wasp nest inside a wall void](/services/wasp-control/wasp-nest-in-a-wall-void/).

## Bald-faced hornet, which is not a hornet

Black and white, big, with the football-shaped paper nest everybody photographs.

WSU is direct about the taxonomy: the bald-faced hornet is technically a
yellowjacket rather than a true hornet. That matters less than the behavior,
which is the part people get right — WSU describes it as aggressively defending
its nest, and that reputation is earned.

The nest is the giveaway: a large enclosed gray paper sphere hanging from a
branch, a soffit or occasionally a fence, growing through the summer to the size
of a basketball. Unlike a yellowjacket nest in the ground, you can see exactly
what you are dealing with, which is both reassuring and misleading. A visible
nest is not a safe nest.

There is a species page at
[bald-faced hornet](/pest-library/bald-faced-hornet/), and the practical version
— when it needs removing and when it genuinely does not — is at
[a bald-faced hornet nest in a tree](/services/wasp-control/bald-faced-hornet-nest-in-a-tree/).

## Paper wasps, including the European one that took over

Long-legged, slender, hanging a small open comb from a stalk under a sheltered
edge.

A paper wasp nest looks nothing like a yellowjacket nest. It is a single
downward-facing comb with the cells visible, no paper envelope around it, hung
from a short stalk under an eave, a deck rail, a window frame or inside a shed.
WSU describes colonies of roughly six to twenty.

The **European paper wasp** is the one that changed the picture here. It is
introduced, it is identified by its bright orange antennae, and WSU describes it
as aggressive — unusually so for a paper wasp — and recommends removing its
nests in winter. It has become common in and around buildings across this
region, and because its nest is often tucked somewhere tight rather than hanging
in the open, people meet it by accident. The species page is at
[European paper wasp](/pest-library/european-paper-wasp/).

Native paper wasps and the **golden paper wasp**, which WSU describes as golden
yellow and active April to October, are far more easygoing. WSU notes paper
wasps generally do not sting unless the nest is somewhere people pass closely.
A paper wasp comb under a quiet eave is a genuine case for doing nothing.

## Black wasps in Washington, and what they are

One of the most-searched wasp questions in this state, and the answer is almost
always something harmless.

**Mud daubers.** Slender, long-waisted, often blue-black with a metallic sheen,
and the builders of the tubular mud nests on the underside of eaves and in
sheds. Solitary, essentially never aggressive, and they hunt spiders. See
[mud dauber](/pest-library/mud-dauber/).

**Great golden digger wasp.** Large, black, with orange-red on the legs and
abdomen, nesting in bare soil. Alarming at close range and uninterested in
people. See
[great golden digger wasp](/pest-library/great-golden-digger-wasp/).

**Grass-carrying wasp.** Black, slim, and the reason dried grass appears stuffed
into the track of a storm window. Solitary and harmless. See
[grass-carrying wasp](/pest-library/grass-carrying-wasp/).

**Bald-faced hornet.** Black and white rather than black, and much stockier
than any of the above. This is the one on the list that will sting you, and the
nest tells you immediately which you have.

**Potter and mason wasps.** WSU describes them as mostly black or brown with
yellow or white markings, building mud or sand brood chambers in existing
cavities. Solitary again.

The pattern worth noticing: if it is black, slender and alone, it is almost
certainly solitary and almost certainly not a problem. If it is black and white,
stocky, and there are many of them going to one place, that is a colony.

## Are there murder hornets in Washington?

No. And the local part of this story is worth telling properly, because it
happened here.

The northern giant hornet — the insect the press named the murder hornet — was
found in **Whatcom County**. WSDA located and destroyed four nests between 2020
and 2021, all in this county, and then detected nothing at all in 2022, 2023 or
2024. At the end of 2024, WSDA and the USDA declared the species eradicated from
Washington and from the United States, on the strength of those three clear
years.

So the honest position for anybody in Bellingham, Lynden, Ferndale or Blaine is:
it was here, it is not here now, and the people who removed it are the ones who
say so. Anything large and frightening in your garden this summer is a bald-faced
hornet, an aerial yellowjacket, or one of the big solitary wasps above.

WSDA's own framing is worth keeping, though. They got here once, and they could
do it again, which is why the reporting system still exists. If you genuinely
believe you have seen one, WSDA wants a photograph or a specimen — they cannot
confirm a report without one — plus the location, the date and the direction it
flew, through their Hornet Watch report form, hornets@agr.wa.gov, or
1-800-443-6684.

## The yellow-legged hornet, and the 2026 interception

The current item, and most pest pages in this state have not caught up with it.

In April 2026 a live yellow-legged hornet was photographed at the Port of
Vancouver, Washington. WSDA records it as an interception rather than an
establishment, and is running trapping and outreach in that area to confirm
nothing else is present.

That is the whole of what is known, and it is worth stating exactly that
narrowly. One hornet, at a port, hundreds of miles from here, with the response
already underway. The species is a serious honey bee pest in Europe, where WSDA
notes losses of up to 30% of hives, which is why an interception gets this level
of attention.

What it is not is a reason for anybody in Whatcom County to worry about the
wasps in their garden. If you keep bees, WSDA's email updates on the invasive
hornet project are the thing to sign up for.

## Telling a wasp from a bee, and why it matters

Because the two go to completely different places in this business.

Wasps have narrow waists, smooth or sparsely haired bodies, and legs that hang
in flight. Bees are rounder, visibly furry, and carry pollen. A honey bee swarm
is a tight cluster of thousands on a branch; a wasp nest is a structure.

The distinction decides the work. We do not treat honey bees — a swarm or a
colony goes to a beekeeper — and bumble bees and mason bees are left alone as a
matter of policy rather than as a favor. That position is set out at
[bee removal](/services/bee-removal/) and the identification detail is in
[bees vs wasps vs hornets](/blog/bees-vs-wasps-vs-hornets-identification/).

If you are not sure, a photograph settles it in under a minute and costs
nothing.

## Why late summer is when everything goes wrong

The season has a shape, and knowing it explains most of what people notice.

A yellowjacket colony starts in spring as a single overwintered queen. Through
early summer it is small, busy hunting caterpillars and flies, and almost
invisible. By August it has reached its maximum size — thousands of workers in a
mature nest — and then the behavior changes. WSU describes the queen dying in
late summer and the workers switching from seeking protein to seeking sugar.

That switch is why wasps appear at barbecues, on fallen fruit, around bins and
inside drink cans in late August and September, having been no trouble at all in
June. It is not that they have become aggressive. It is that there are far more
of them and they are now looking for exactly the things people take outdoors.

The whole colony dies at the first hard frost except next year's queens, which
disperse and overwinter alone. Nothing survives in the nest, and the nest is
never reused. The seasonal picture across every pest here is in
[the Whatcom County pest year](/guides/the-whatcom-county-pest-year/).

## When a nest matters, and when it does not

Not every nest is a job, and we would rather say which is which.

**Leave it, usually:** a paper wasp comb under a quiet eave away from doors; a
bald-faced hornet nest high in a tree at the back of a property; any nest found
in October that will be dead within weeks; mud dauber tubes; anything solitary.

**Deal with it:** a ground nest beside a path, a gate or anywhere a mower goes;
anything in a wall void, which gets worse rather than better; a nest at a door,
a window that opens, or a child's play area; any nest where somebody in the
household has a sting allergy.

**Do not do it yourself:** anything you cannot reach standing on the ground,
anything in a wall, and anything where retreat is awkward. The emergency room
visits from this come overwhelmingly from ladders, not from stings.

And never plug the entrance of a wall nest. Wasps that cannot get out the way
they came in will work their way inward, and the next place they appear is a
bedroom ceiling.
