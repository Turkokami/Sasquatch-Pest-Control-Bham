## The phrase that stopped meaning anything

Ask any pest control company in this county whether they practice integrated
pest management and the answer will be yes. Ask what it means on their jobs and
you will usually get a description of a quarterly perimeter spray with a web
sweep attached, plus a sentence about using the least amount of product
necessary.

That is not integrated pest management. That is a repeating application with a
better name on it.

The term is worth rescuing, because the thing it describes is real, it is
defined in public by people with no product to sell, and it genuinely produces
better outcomes in this climate than the alternative. So this page does three
things: sets out what the published definitions actually require, describes what
that looks like on a real job in Bellingham or Ferndale rather than in the
abstract, and gives you a way to check whether the company standing in your
kitchen is doing it.

## What the definition actually says

Start with the EPA, because it is the source everyone in the trade gestures at
and almost nobody quotes.

EPA calls IPM "an environmentally friendly, common sense approach to controlling
pests," and describes it as focusing on pest prevention and using pesticides
only as needed. It then sets out four steps, in order:

**Identify pests and monitor progress.** Correct identification first, and
records of what was monitored, where, and on what schedule.

**Set action thresholds.** EPA defines the action threshold as "the pest
population level at which the pest's presence is a nuisance, health hazard; or
economic threat."

**Prevent pests.** Remove the conditions that attract them — food, water,
shelter — through weatherization, sanitation and telling people what they are
doing that keeps the problem fed.

**Control pests.** Only once the threshold has been crossed, and then using "the
most effective, lowest risk options," which EPA lists as trapping, heat or cold
treatment, physical removal, or a pesticide application.

Washington State University's IPM material lays out the same sequence with one
addition worth noticing: evaluation. Check often to see whether you still have
the problem, and work out which of the things you did was the thing that
mattered. WSU's landscape material describes urban IPM as an approach that "does
not rely totally on pesticides," and orders the tactics — cultural, sanitation,
biological, mechanical, and chemical last.

The University of California's program puts it as "an ecosystem-based strategy
that focuses on long-term prevention of pests or their damage through a
combination of techniques," and is blunt about the decision at the center of it:
monitor the numbers, then decide "whether the pest can be tolerated or whether
it is a problem that warrants control."

Three independent land-grant and federal sources, three ways of saying the same
thing. Identify. Watch. Decide. Change the conditions. Treat narrowly and only
where the decision says to.

## Identification is not a formality

This is where most jobs are won or lost, and it is the step that gets skipped
most often because it costs time and produces no invoice line.

The category is not the answer. "Ants" is not an identification. In a Whatcom
County kitchen in April, a trail of small dark ants is most likely odorous house
ants, and the correct response is a bait matched to what the colony is currently
feeding on plus a look at what is drawing them. If the same trail turns out to
be moisture ants, the correct response is a moisture investigation, because
moisture ants are a symptom of wet, decaying wood and treating them without
fixing the water is buying a year. If it is carpenter ants, the question becomes
whether you are looking at a parent colony outside in a stump or a satellite
inside a wall, and the answer changes the whole job.

Same three-letter word, three completely different pieces of work, and no way to
choose between them without a hand lens and someone who knows what they are
looking at.

The rodent version is the same. Rat and mouse droppings are distinguishable by
size and shape, the gnawing is different, the entry sizes are different, and the
bait station placement that works for one is wasted on the other. Norway rats
and roof rats use different parts of a building. A technician who says "we'll
put out some stations" without having established which animal is present has
skipped the step the entire method rests on.

And identification is often the moment the honest answer turns out to be that
nothing needs doing. A spider in the garage in October is an adult male walking
in search of a mate and will be dead in weeks whatever you do. Cluster flies in
an upstairs window in March are the ones that came in last September and are
trying to get out. Neither of those is a treatment problem, and saying so is
part of the method rather than a failure to sell.

## Monitoring, and an honest word about it

Monitoring means knowing what is happening rather than assuming. In practice it
is sticky traps in the places insects travel, snap traps or tracking patches set
and then read, a record of where activity was seen and when, and comparing this
visit to the last one.

In commercial work this is real. A food handling account has a device map, each
device gets checked and logged, trend lines get looked at, and a rise in counts
at the back door triggers a look at the back door. That is the version EPA's
records language describes, and it is the version an auditor asks to see.

In residential work, and here is the part the trade does not say out loud, it is
usually much thinner. Nobody is logging trap counts monthly on a three-bedroom
house in Sudden Valley, ourselves included. What residential monitoring honestly
consists of is a technician checking the same locations on each visit, the
customer reporting what they have seen since, and a comparison between the two.

That is not nothing — it is how you find out that the activity moved from the
crawlspace to the wall cavity, or that what you did in September worked. But a
company selling you a "monitoring program" on a house is, nine times out of ten,
describing a scheduled visit. Ask what specifically gets monitored and how it
gets recorded, and the answer will tell you which version you are buying.

## Thresholds, which is the uncomfortable part

The action threshold is the piece of IPM that pest control companies quietly
drop, because it is the piece that says some level of pest presence does not
warrant a treatment.

Selling is easier when the threshold is zero. Zero is also achievable in very
few places and expensive in all of them.

Where a threshold of zero is genuinely right: bed bugs anywhere, cockroaches in
a commercial kitchen, rodents in any food handling area, and stored product
pests in a facility subject to audit. In those settings, presence is the trigger
and the argument is over.

Where zero is the wrong target: spiders in a garage or a crawlspace, a few
foraging ants on a patio in June, cluster flies in an unheated attic,
overwintering stink bugs on a south wall in October, sowbugs under a doormat,
and most of what walks through a house in the fall. All of those are seasonal,
externally sourced and largely unaffected by anything applied to a foundation.
A company that treats each of them as an emergency has not set a threshold. It
has set a schedule.

The honest position on a threshold is that it is partly yours. Your tolerance
for a spider in a bathtub is not our decision, and WSU's material puts this
directly to the homeowner: work out what you can accept. What we can do is tell
you which situations get worse if left, which ones resolve without help, and
which ones are pointing at a building problem you would want to know about
anyway.

## The hierarchy, and why it is in that order

Every source above orders the tools the same way, and the order is not
sentimental. It runs from most durable to least.

**Sanitation and habitat modification first.** Pet food left down, an open
compost pile, a bird feeder twenty feet from the house, fallen fruit under an
apple tree, laurel hedging pressed against siding, firewood stacked on the
foundation, a garbage enclosure that does not close. Every one of those is a
food or harborage supply, and none is affected by a product. UC IPM is explicit
on cockroaches: insecticides "are most effective in controlling cockroaches when
combined with sanitation and exclusion practices," and removing other food
sources "will greatly enhance the effects of baits."

**Exclusion second.** Close the way in. A quarter-inch gap at a pipe
penetration, a corroded crawl vent screen, a door sweep worn at the corners, an
open crawl hatch, a gable vent with the mesh gone. Sealed properly in metal and
mortar rather than foam, that work does not need repeating, which is the whole
argument for it.

**Habitat and moisture third**, which in this county is nearly as important as
the first two. A wet crawlspace produces moisture ants, dampwood termites,
springtails, sowbugs and the spiders that follow them. Drying it removes several
species at once, permanently, without a product.

**Mechanical control fourth.** Traps, physical removal, vacuuming, a nest taken
down at the right time of day.

**Chemical control last**, chosen for the target and placed where the target
goes.

The reason this order matters commercially is that the first three are mostly
not our work. They are a homeowner's afternoon, a gutter contractor, a builder,
or a drainage job. A company whose recommendations never leave its own price
list is not applying the hierarchy — it is applying the part of it that it
bills for.

## Where chemicals fit, precisely

The last step is not a lesser step. When the threshold is crossed, treating
properly matters, and doing it narrowly is what makes the difference.

**Baits over sprays, where a bait exists.** UC IPM's ant guidance says baits are
"a key tool for managing ants and the only type of insecticide recommended in
most situations," and that "when properly used, baits are more effective and
safer than sprays." For cockroaches the same program states that bait products
"are the primary pesticides used," and that aerosols and total-release foggers
"are often ineffective since they don't reach the crevices where cockroaches
harbor."

**Crack and crevice over broadcast.** Product placed into the void, the gap
behind the trim, the plumbing penetration, the wall junction — the places the
insect actually travels — rather than a film across a surface people and animals
touch. It uses a fraction of the material and puts it where it does the work.

**Target-specific over broad-spectrum**, where a target-specific option exists
and is effective.

**No application at all where the finding does not support one.** This is the
step with no product to defend it and it is the one that most distinguishes a
program that is running on evidence from one running on a calendar.

## What this looks like on a job here

Abstractions are cheap. Here is the shape of three actual local jobs.

**Ants in a Ferndale kitchen, late April.** The visit starts at the trail, not
at the truck. Follow it back: along the counter, down the cabinet side, into the
gap where the sink drain passes through the wall. Outside, the same wall carries
a downspout discharging at the foundation and a hedge touching the siding. Under
the sink the cabinet base is stained and the particle board is soft.

Identification says moisture ants, and now the job has changed. The treatment
conversation is short and the building conversation is long: the downspout gets
extended today, the hedge gets cut back, and the soft cabinet base is a symptom
of a leak somebody needs to find. A perimeter spray would have knocked down the
foragers, the customer would have been pleased for six weeks, and the wood would
have kept rotting.

**Rats in a Bellingham crawlspace, mid-October.** Droppings and rub marks along
a joist, insulation pulled down in two bays, and a crawl vent with the screen
corroded through on the north side. The property has a plum tree dropping fruit,
a compost bin without a lid, and a laurel hedge running the length of the fence.

The program has four parts and only one of them is a product. Habitat: fruit
picked up, compost lidded, hedge cut back off the ground. Exclusion: the failed
vent rescreened in quarter-inch metal, the hatch made to close, two pipe
penetrations sealed. Mechanical: traps set on the runs rather than scattered.
Chemical: exterior stations if the pressure warrants them, sited where they can
be serviced and where a dog cannot reach them, and reviewed rather than left
forever. The follow-up visit reads the traps and looks at whether new droppings
have appeared since — which is the evaluation step, and it is the one people
skip.

**A wasp nest in Lynden, August.** Identification first, and it decides
everything. A honey bee colony in a wall is not our job and gets referred to a
beekeeper. A paper wasp nest under a shop eave, thirty feet from any door, with
nobody going near it, may need nothing at all — it will be empty by November.
Yellowjackets in a ground nest beside the back step in August, with children
using that step, is over the threshold and gets treated that day, at the nest,
after dusk, rather than by spraying the eaves of the house.

Three jobs, three different answers, and in two of them the most valuable thing
delivered was not an application.

## How to tell whether you are getting it

Six checks, all of which you can apply from your own kitchen.

**Did they name the species?** Not "ants" or "spiders" — the species, or an
honest "I want to look at this under magnification before I tell you." Vagueness
here predicts vagueness everywhere.

**Did they go where the answer is?** On the housing stock in this county that
means the crawlspace, with a light, along its length. A visit that stayed on the
lawn has not seen the building.

**Did they tell you what is driving it?** There is always a reason. Food, water,
harborage, a way in. A company that cannot name the driver is treating a
symptom.

**Did they recommend something they do not sell?** Extend that downspout. Cut
the hedge back. Get the gutter looked at. Store the pet food differently. If
every recommendation happens to be a service on their price list, the hierarchy
is not being applied.

**Did anything change between visit one and visit two?** A program running on
evidence looks different each time because the findings differ. A program
running on a calendar looks identical, because it is.

**Did you get a record of what was applied?** Washington requires a commercial
applicator to keep records of each application — location, date and start and
end times, the product name as it appears on the registered label, the EPA
registration number, the amount and concentration applied, and who made the
application — and to provide a copy of those records to the owner of the land
treated. That is a legal entitlement, not a courtesy, and it is the single most
checkable thing on this list.

## The technical reason repetition fails

There is an argument for this method that has nothing to do with values, and it
is the one that persuades people who find the environmental case unconvincing.

Repeatedly applying the same class of material to the same population selects
for the individuals that survive it. That is not a controversial claim; it is
the reason the medical world worries about antibiotics, and insects reproduce
considerably faster than bacteria do in a hospital. In structural pest work it
shows up most sharply in bed bugs and in German cockroaches, both of which have
well documented resistance to materials that worked reliably a generation ago.

An approach built on rotating a couple of products across a foundation four
times a year is, at the population level, a training program. An approach that
changes the food supply, closes the entry and uses a bait matched to the target
does not create the same pressure, because most of the work is being done by
something no insect can develop resistance to. You cannot evolve your way
through a sealed vent.

## Why the climate here sharpens all of this

The method matters more in Whatcom County than it would in a dry place, and the
reason is that so much of what we deal with is a moisture problem wearing an
insect costume.

Thirty-five inches of rain, a wet season running from October to April, and no
hard freeze to interrupt it. Under those conditions a crawlspace with a torn
ground cover or a blocked vent stays damp for six months at a stretch, the
framing above it stays in the range where decay proceeds, and the species that
require wet wood arrive on their own. Moisture ants, dampwood termites,
springtails, sowbugs and the spiders feeding on them are all downstream of the
same defect.

Treat that chemically and you are applying product to a building that is
generating new conditions faster than you can respond to them. Dry the space and
several problems leave at once and do not come back. That is the hierarchy doing
what it is supposed to do, and it is the reason a company here that never talks
about water is not really working on the problem.

## Two claims to be skeptical of

**"We use an IPM-based approach."** So does everyone, by their own account. The
phrase carries no information. The follow-up question that does carry
information is: what did the last job you ran here involve that was not an
application?

**"Our IPM program is a quarterly service."** A frequency is not a method. There
are good reasons to visit a building on a schedule — seasonal pressure here is
genuinely predictable, and a spring visit and a late-summer visit catch
different things. But the schedule is a logistics decision. What happens on the
visit is the method, and those are two different conversations that get merged
on purpose.

## Where we fall short of the ideal

A page that describes a standard and then implies the writer meets it perfectly
is worth very little, so here is the gap.

We do not run logged, trend-tracked monitoring on single-family houses. Our
residential thresholds are conversations rather than written numbers. We sell
recurring programs, and a recurring program is exactly the structure that drifts
toward calendar application if nobody is paying attention — the pressure is
real, and the check on it is partly yours. And we have a commercial interest in
your problem needing a treatment, which is a bias you should assume is present
in every company that visits you, including this one.

What we can say is that the assessment is free and comes with no term agreement
attached, that we would rather tell you to fix a downspout than sell you a plan
on a wet crawlspace, and that we will put the reason we think a treatment is or
is not warranted in plain language you can push back on.

The method is only worth something if the person applying it is willing to reach
the conclusion that costs them the sale. That is the whole test, and it is not a
difficult one to watch for.
