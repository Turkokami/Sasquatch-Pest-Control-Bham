## What actually turns up inside a house here

Less variety than people expect, and nearly all of it falls into four groups.

Western Washington has a specific climate and a specific building stock — wet
winters, mild summers, crawlspaces, and a great deal of wood — and the insects
that end up indoors here reflect that. WSU Pestsense maintains fact sheets for
the household and structural pests of this state, and running down that list
against what we actually see on jobs produces four recognizable groups plus a
handful of outliers.

**Autumn invaders.** Species that overwinter as adults and come indoors to do
it. They arrive in October, they do no damage, and they are a sealing problem.

**Moisture species.** Insects that need damp conditions and are therefore
telling you something about the building before they are telling you anything
about themselves.

**Stored-product pests.** Kitchen and pantry insects, which arrive in packaging
rather than through a gap.

**Fabric pests.** Carpet beetles and clothes moths, which are slow, quiet, and
noticed late.

Then there are ants, which are their own category and the largest single
service we run, and spiders, which have their own guide.

## The autumn arrivals

The biggest group by phone call volume, and the one that worries people most
unnecessarily.

Every October the same set of insects appears on sunny south and west walls and
starts working its way into window frames, soffits and attics:

- [Cluster fly](/pest-library/cluster-fly/) — slow, furry, in upstairs windows
  all winter. Breeds in earthworms outdoors, never indoors.
- [Boxelder bug](/pest-library/boxelder-bug/) — black with red lines, in
  numbers, on the warm wall.
- [Western conifer seed bug](/pest-library/western-conifer-seed-bug/) — large,
  brown, clumsy, and the one most often mistaken for something dangerous.
- [Multicolored Asian lady beetle](/pest-library/multicolored-asian-lady-beetle/)
  — not the native lady beetle, and it stains.
- [Brown marmorated stink bug](/pest-library/brown-marmorated-stink-bug/) —
  shield-shaped, and the smell is real.

None of them breeds indoors. None of them damages anything. They came in
through gaps that were there before they arrived, which is why the entire
answer to this group is
[sealing a building before fall](/guides/sealing-a-building-before-fall/) and
why the work is worth doing in August rather than in October. The problem page
for the pattern is
[bugs on the south wall every October](/services/stink-bug-control/bugs-on-the-south-wall-every-october/).

## The ones that mean a moisture problem

The most useful group on this page, because the insect is a symptom.

[Silverfish](/pest-library/silverfish/) in a bathroom, springtails around a
shower tray or a damp doorframe, sowbugs and
[pillbugs](/pest-library/pillbug/) against a foundation,
[millipedes](/pest-library/millipede/) coming under a door after rain, psocids
or booklice on damp plaster and stored paper — every one of those is an animal
that cannot survive in dry conditions.

Two of this group do matter structurally.
[Moisture ants](/pest-library/moisture-ant/) nest in wood that is already wet
and are among the most reliable indicators of a leak there is, which is the
subject of
[moisture ants mean a water problem](/services/ant-control/moisture-ants-mean-a-water-problem/).
And the [Pacific dampwood termite](/pest-library/pacific-dampwood-termite/)
needs wood with high moisture content, which in this county means a wet
crawlspace, a failed gutter or ground contact.

Why this keeps happening here is the subject of
[why crawlspaces here stay wet](/guides/why-crawlspaces-here-stay-wet/). The
short version is that a vented crawlspace in a maritime climate spends much of
the year taking on moisture it cannot shed, and a great deal of what people
call a bug problem is a ventilation and drainage problem wearing legs.

## The kitchen and the pantry

These arrive in a bag, not through a window.

[Indianmeal moth](/pest-library/indianmeal-moth/) is the common one — the small
moth flying in lazy loops near the ceiling, with webbing and clumped grain in a
bag of flour, birdseed, dried fruit or pet food.
[Sawtoothed grain beetle](/pest-library/sawtoothed-grain-beetle/) and the
cupboard and drugstore beetles WSU lists are the same story in a different
shape.

The important thing about this group is that treatment is almost never the
answer. The infestation is in a specific package; finding that package ends it.
Spraying a kitchen does not, and WSU's standing advice against pesticide on food
preparation surfaces applies with full force. The service page is
[beetle and pantry pest control](/services/beetle-control/) and the moth side is
[moth control](/services/moth-control/).

Fruit flies, fungus gnats and drain flies are a related family of complaints
with a related answer — find the wet medium, remove it — and they are covered
under [fly control](/services/fly-control/) and in
[how long do flies live](/services/fly-control/how-long-do-flies-live/).

## Fabric, carpet and wool

Slow, quiet, and noticed after the damage.

[Carpet beetle](/pest-library/carpet-beetle/) larvae feed on wool, silk, felt,
feathers, pet hair and the dust under furniture that nobody moves.
[Webbing clothes moth](/pest-library/webbing-clothes-moth/) does the same in
stored wool. Both are found late, because the first sign is damage rather than
an insect, and both concentrate in the places nobody disturbs — under a heavy
sofa, behind a wardrobe, in a box in a closet.

Carpet beetle is also the species most often confused with bed bugs, which is
worth knowing before anybody panics about a bedroom.

## The ones that bite, and the ones that only look as though they should

A short real list and a much longer imaginary one.

**Actually bite people here.** [Bed bugs](/pest-library/bed-bug/),
[cat fleas](/pest-library/cat-flea/),
[rodent mites](/pest-library/rodent-mite/) after a rodent problem has been
resolved and the mites have lost their host, and
[the western black-legged tick](/pest-library/western-black-legged-tick/)
outdoors. That is close to the whole list for a house in this county.

**Look alarming, do not bite.** Western conifer seed bugs,
[earwigs](/pest-library/earwig/), house centipedes, mud daubers,
[grass-carrying wasps](/pest-library/grass-carrying-wasp/), pseudoscorpions,
and nearly every spider in the building. Earwigs in particular carry a
reputation out of all proportion to anything they do.

If something is biting you and you cannot find it, that is a specific problem
with a specific set of answers, and
[bites, but no bugs found](/services/bed-bug-control/bites-but-no-bugs-found/)
works through it properly.

## Are there kissing bugs in Washington?

No — and the question is asked often enough in this state to need a straight
answer.

CDC publishes the United States distribution of triatomine bugs species by
species, with the states each one occurs in. Washington does not appear for any
of them. The listed ranges run through the Southwest, the South and the
Southeast: the nearest states on the list are California, Nevada, Utah and
Colorado, and most species are confined to Arizona, New Mexico and Texas.

What gets photographed and sent to us as a possible kissing bug is, almost
every time, a [western conifer seed bug](/pest-library/western-conifer-seed-bug/).
It is large, brown, flat-backed, flies badly, lands on people, and turns up
indoors in exactly the season when somebody has just read an article about
Chagas disease. It feeds on conifer seeds and cannot bite a person.

This is the same shape of answer as
[brown recluse spiders in Washington](/services/spider-control/brown-recluse-spiders-in-washington/):
a frightening species that genuinely exists, genuinely matters where it lives,
and does not live here.

## The ones that are not insects at all

Worth separating, because the treatment logic is different.

[Clover mites](/pest-library/clover-mite/) — the tiny red specks on a sunny
wall in spring and autumn. Arachnids, lawn feeders, harmless, and the subject of
their own profile.

Sowbugs and [pillbugs](/pest-library/pillbug/) — crustaceans, closer to a crab
than to a beetle, and entirely dependent on damp.

[Millipedes](/pest-library/millipede/) and house centipedes — many-legged,
unrelated to each other, and both moisture indicators.

Pseudoscorpions — tiny, with visible pincers, genuinely startling at first
sight, and predators of other small household arthropods. They are a sign of a
healthy building rather than a sick one.

And [spiders](/guides/spiders-in-washington-state/), which have their own guide
because the questions people ask about them are different from the questions
they ask about anything else.

## Which of these actually need treating

A minority, and that is the point of writing the list out.

**Treat, usually.** [Carpenter ants](/pest-library/carpenter-ant/) in a
structure. [Bed bugs](/services/bed-bug-control/). German cockroaches. Fleas
with an active host. Dampwood termites and powderpost beetles in sound
structural wood. A yellowjacket nest somewhere people go.

**Fix the building instead.** Every autumn invader. Every moisture species.
Most spiders. Most millipede, sowbug and silverfish complaints.

**Fix the cupboard instead.** Every stored-product pest.

**Do nothing.** Pseudoscorpions, most solitary wasps, cellar spiders,
lady beetles, a single wandering anything.

The honest version of the whole subject is in
[what integrated pest management actually means](/guides/what-integrated-pest-management-actually-means/)
and in
[low-impact treatment, and what it can and cannot do](/guides/low-impact-treatment-and-what-it-can-and-cannot-do/).

## Identifying what you have

Photograph it before you squash it.

From above, against something pale, with a coin or a ruler in frame for scale,
and in focus. That single photograph settles most identifications in seconds,
and it costs you nothing to send one to us. The whole library, species by
species, is at [the pest library](/pest-library/).

What is worth telling us alongside it: where you found it, what month, whether
there was one or several, and whether that spot is damp. Those four things
usually matter more than the photograph does, because they decide which of the
four groups above you are in — and that, rather than the species name, decides
what anybody should do about it.
