## What is different about a commercial year

The organisms are the same ones a house gets. The calendar is the same calendar.
What differs is the building and what happens when something is seen.

A commercial building has more perimeter, more openings, and more doors held
open by people doing their jobs. It receives deliveries, and deliveries arrive on
pallets and in corrugate that have been sitting somewhere else. It runs warm at
night. It stores food, or packaging, or paper, or all three. And it usually has
several organizations touching it — an operator, a landlord, a facilities
contractor, a cleaning company — which means the gap that lets a rodent in is
frequently nobody's item on nobody's list.

Then there is the consequence. In a house, a mouse is a nuisance. In a
restaurant it is a health department finding. In a food plant it is a
nonconformance in an audit that a customer contract depends on. In a care
setting it is a licensing matter. In an apartment building it is a habitability
question with a statute behind it. The organism did not change; the cost of it
being seen did, by orders of magnitude.

That asymmetry is why commercial pest management is organized around prevention
and paperwork rather than around response, and why the season that matters most
is the one where nothing is wrong yet.

## The documentation question, taken first

Everything else on this page is easier to plan once this part is settled, so it
goes at the front.

**The requirement exists in federal rule for food facilities.** 21 CFR 117.35,
the sanitation section of the current good manufacturing practice regulation,
states plainly that pests must not be allowed in any area of a food plant, that
effective measures must be taken to exclude pests from manufacturing,
processing, packing and holding areas and to protect against contamination of
food on the premises, and that pesticides may be used only under precautions and
restrictions protecting food, food-contact surfaces and packaging materials.
Note the verb in the middle of that: exclude. The rule is written around keeping
pests out of the building, not around treating them once they are in.

Penn State Extension's summary of the pest management expectations under the
Food Safety Modernization Act lands in the same place — exclusion of pests from
fully enclosed buildings, measures to prevent establishment where a structure is
only partly enclosed, and routine monitoring.

**A third-party audit goes further than the rule, and asks to see the system.**
The pest management standards published for food processing and handling
facilities, which are the reference point most auditors work from, set out what
a documented program looks like. A service record is expected to carry the
product used with its registration number, the target pest, the rate or
concentration, the time, the location, the quantity of finished product, the
date, and signatures from both the technician and the facility. Monitoring
devices are placed to a spacing and inspected on a stated frequency, interior
devices more often than exterior. And — the part facilities most often miss —
the data those devices produce is expected to be trended and passed to whoever
coordinates the program so that somebody can act on it.

The practical translation is that an auditor is not really auditing the pest
control company. They are auditing whether your facility has a system, whether
the system produced information, and whether anybody did anything with it. A
folder of visit slips with no trend and no corrective action satisfies the first
of those three and fails the other two.

**What we can supply and what we cannot.** For general pests we provide a
written record of each visit and the treatment performed, in a form that goes
into your file and stands up to being read by somebody who is not a technician,
along with device placement and the results over time.

There is one category where the answer is different and it is worth being exact
about, because facilities ask for it. A document identifying damage or
infestation by wood-destroying organisms is a regulated report in Washington,
with its own required contents, written by a licensed structural pest inspector.
We do not produce that document and will not offer one under a different name.
What we hold authorizes treatment of what such a report identifies, and the
records we supply describe the treatment we performed.

**Retention.** Keep your own copies rather than relying on the contractor's.
Twelve months is the common working minimum in food service; longer is cheap and
occasionally decisive. For schools and licensed day cares, Washington's own
notification and record requirements sit on top of everything above, and are set
out separately in
[pest control in Washington schools](/guides/pest-control-in-washington-schools/).

## January and February

**Quiet outside, busy inside, and the best two months for administration.**

Nothing new is arriving. What surfaces now is the autumn's population making
itself known: rodents established in a service void or a ceiling space, and
overwintering insects roused by a warm spell or by the heating running, emerging
at upper windows and in offices. Neither is a new arrival, and treating them as
one leads to spending in the least productive month of the year.

German cockroaches in a commercial kitchen do not care what month it is. They
live in warm, food-rich equipment year-round, and a winter uptick usually means
a sanitation or harborage change rather than a season.

Stored-product pests get discovered now, because winter is when somebody
finally pulls the shelving and counts the stock. The infestation established in
September.

**Ahead of the season:** this is the planning window. Confirm the records are
complete and where somebody could find them under pressure. Review last
autumn's monitoring data as a whole rather than visit by visit — the trend is
the deliverable, and January is when there is time to read it. Put the summer
exclusion work in the budget while the reason for it is still fresh, because by
the time it is obviously needed again it will be too late to fund.

## March and April

**The ground warms and the outdoor pressure resumes.**

Ants become active, and the second and larger wave follows heavy rain rather
than warmth — shallow outdoor nests flood and the colony relocates to somewhere
drier, which is often a wall. Break rooms, staff kitchens and any area with a
drink station take these first.

Wasp queens emerge and begin founding nests, each one a single insect building
something small. On a commercial site the places they choose are predictable:
under signage, inside electrical and utility cabinets, in the recesses around
dock canopies, under handrails, and in the untended strip between a fence line
and a parking area.

Overwintering insects that survived the winter try to leave, producing a final
flurry of indoor sightings that reads as a new problem.

**Ahead of the season:** walk the grounds for founding nests, and get them dealt
with while each is one insect rather than a colony. Ten minutes with a
flashlight around signage and cabinet doors in April removes most of the
late-summer sting risk on a commercial property. This is also the month to
schedule the exterior work for summer, before every contractor's season fills.

## May and June

**The dry ground months, and the only practical window for groundwork.**

Insect populations build. Fly pressure rises around waste areas and drains as
temperatures climb, and drain-breeding flies in particular are a sanitation
problem that treatment alone will not hold. Deliveries increase across most
sectors, and delivery volume is the single largest route by which stored-product
pests enter a warehouse or a grocery back-of-house.

The ground dries out, which matters more than it sounds. Grading, drainage,
downspout discharge, dumpster pad work and anything involving a trench is
practical now and is not practical in December.

**Ahead of the season:** the groundwork. Move the dumpster pad if it is too
close to the building, fix the drainage that has been pooling against the
foundation since October, and have the drains cleaned rather than treated.
Confirm that the waste contractor's schedule matches your actual volume in
summer rather than your volume in February. Book the exclusion work now for
July or August.

## July and August

**The most important eight weeks in the commercial year, and the ones when
nothing appears to be wrong.**

Wasp and yellowjacket colonies grow steeply, and their foraging shifts toward
sugar through August, which is when they arrive at outdoor seating, bins and
loading areas. Fly pressure peaks. Ants may come indoors for water during a dry
stretch, which is why a spotless kitchen gets a trail in August with nothing
left out. Rodent pressure has not started, and that is exactly the point.

**Ahead of the season — this is the paragraph to act on:** the entire autumn is
decided here.

Dock door seals, dock leveler pits and vehicle bumpers, which are the highest
volume rodent entry on most facilities. Door sweeps on every exterior door,
particularly delivery and kitchen doors where they wear fastest. Vent and
louvre screening in metal. Roofline, soffit and roof-penetration openings closed
before overwintering insects arrive. Utility penetrations sealed where services
pass through walls. The grounds walked for ground nests before autumn
landscaping finds them the hard way. And any exterior treatment aimed at
overwintering insects, which works applied before they move in and does very
little afterward.

Every item there is cheaper, easier and more effective in August than at any
other point in the year, and every one of them prevents a call somebody will
otherwise make in November.

## September

**Everything arrives at once, and the audit calendar tends to arrive with it.**

The rodent push begins as nights cool. Outdoor cover dies back, natural food
declines, and a heated building with a quarter-inch gap becomes a considerably
better proposition than it was in July. Spiders reach maturity and start
walking, which on a commercial site is a cosmetic and complaint problem rather
than a real one. Yellowjacket colonies reach maximum size and maximum
defensiveness in precisely the weeks when outdoor work is happening.

In Bellingham specifically, the student return concentrates turnover, travel and
second-hand furniture into a few weeks, which lands on multifamily operators and
on anybody running furnished accommodation.

**Ahead of the season:** if the summer work did not happen, do it now anyway —
September is worse than August and far better than November. Check the building
before sealing it. Closing a structure that already has a population inside is
the one mistake that reliably makes things worse, and it is the mistake most
commonly made by a facility reacting late.

## October and November

**The peak of the indoor year, and the point at which prevention has closed.**

Rodent pressure is at its height and stays there. Overwintering insects move
into wall voids, soffits and ceiling spaces, and the walls go quiet again until
the heating wakes them. Wasp colonies collapse with the first hard frosts, and
nests found now are almost always finished.

Heating comes on, which changes the interior environment and starts the cycle of
insects emerging into occupied space on warm days.

**Ahead of the season:** respond rather than prevent, because the prevention
window has shut. Increase monitoring frequency at the perimeter and at receiving
rather than everywhere. Trap before sealing. And write down what happened and
where, in enough detail to be useful in July — that note is the entire input to
next summer's scope, and it is the thing most facilities fail to keep.

## December

**Settled in, and quiet in a misleading way.**

What is inside is inside, and it does not decline seasonally because the
building supplies what the weather does not. Holiday trading raises waste volume
and delivery frequency in retail and food service at exactly the moment when
staffing is thinnest and back-of-house discipline slips. Overwintering insects
appear on warm days.

**Ahead of the season:** hold the sanitation basics through the busiest trading
weeks, and resist sealing anything with a live population behind it. If a
facility closes over a holiday period, that closure is a genuinely useful window
for work that is disruptive during trading, and for schools it also lines up
with the statutory exemption for applications made during a closure of at least
two consecutive days.

## Deliveries, the route that ignores the calendar

Everything above is weather-driven. One major route into a commercial building
is not, and it deserves its own heading because facilities consistently
underrate it.

Stored-product pests — grain beetles, pantry moths, weevils — do not walk in
through a gap in October. They arrive inside product, inside packaging, and
inside the corrugate and pallets the product travels on, on whatever day the
truck comes. A receiving door is a bigger risk to a dry goods store than a
foundation crack, and no amount of exterior sealing addresses it.

The controls are correspondingly different. Look at incoming goods before they
go into storage rather than after: damaged packaging, webbing in the corners of
a case, live insects in the corrugate. Rotate stock so nothing sits long enough
to support a full generation in the dark behind a pallet. Keep pallets off walls
so the gap can be seen and swept. And treat a single finding in one case as a
sourcing question rather than a building question, because that is usually what
it is — the infestation was somebody else's before it was yours.

Cockroaches travel the same way, in cardboard and in returnable containers, and
in a commercial kitchen an introduction that arrives on a Tuesday will not wait
for a season. It is the reason a food business needs monitoring that runs all
year rather than a program shaped only around the autumn.

The useful mental model is two independent pressures on one building: a seasonal
one arriving through the envelope, and an aseasonal one arriving through the
door with a signature on it. They need different work, and a program built for
only one of them has a permanent blind spot.

## The same year, by facility type

The calendar above is the shape. Which parts of it hurt depends on what the
building does.

**Food service.** The highest consequence and the strictest expectations. German
cockroaches in equipment year-round, flies at drains and waste through summer,
rodents from late September. The recurring failures are almost always the same
three: a worn sweep on the delivery door, a dumpster too close to the building,
and drains that need servicing rather than treating. Documentation needs to be
current and retrievable, not filed somewhere.

**Grocery and retail food.** Everything above, plus stored-product pests in dry
goods and a receiving door that opens all day. The valuable habit is pulling
shelving on a schedule rather than when something is noticed, because webbing
and frass behind a pallet is the earliest visible signal you will get.

**Warehousing and distribution.** Rodents at the dock and in palletized goods.
Dock doors are the dominant entry route and dock leveler pits the dominant
harborage. The exterior perimeter matters more here than the interior, and the
single highest-value habit is keeping dock doors closed except during active
loading.

**Multifamily.** The building is the unit of work, never the apartment. Shared
walls, utility chases and a common waste area mean sealing one unit's
penetrations while the chase above stays open relocates the problem. Turnover is
the opportunity: an empty unit is the cheapest time to seal, and the sequencing
problem that plagues occupied buildings disappears.

**Institutional and public buildings.** Schools, municipal offices, libraries,
community facilities. Large perimeters, doors that open all day, portables and
outbuildings, and a public that notices. Schools carry their own statutory
notification, posting and record requirements under Washington law, and the
bait exemption within that statute is the reason a well-built school program
runs on exclusion and contained bait rather than on scheduled spraying. The
same standard is worth adopting in any building where children spend time.

**Industrial and processing.** The largest envelopes and the longest lists of
penetrations. Rodent damage to wiring is a genuine downtime and fire risk rather
than a talking point, and monitoring discipline is what converts a discovery in
a production area into a flag at the perimeter weeks earlier.

## The structural items nobody owns

A pattern worth naming, because it is where most commercial pest budgets leak.

The problems concentrate at a small number of predictable failures: a dock seal
that has gone, a door sweep worn through, waste stored too close to the
building, a drain that needs servicing, a roof or soffit opening that has been
open for years, and a penetration in a mechanical room nobody has closed.

Every one of those is a maintenance item, not a pest control item. They usually
sit outside the pest contractor's scope, which means they get re-reported month
after month while the treatment budget manages a symptom.

Our position on commercial and institutional accounts is that structural items
go in writing on every visit, in a form somebody in facilities can cost and
action, and that we keep raising the same dock door until it is fixed or until
somebody tells us in writing to stop raising it. That is not a lever. It is the
only mechanism by which a program stops being a standing charge.

## The planning calendar, compressed

If a facility manager takes one thing from this page, take the sequence.

**January.** Read last year's trend as a whole. Budget the summer work.

**April.** Grounds walk for founding wasp nests. Book contractors.

**May and June.** Groundwork: drainage, dumpster placement, drains, grading.

**Late July and August.** The envelope: dock seals, sweeps, vent screening,
roofline, penetrations, grounds walk, exterior treatment for overwintering
insects.

**September to November.** Monitor and respond. Trap before sealing. Write down
what happened and where.

**December.** Hold the basics through peak trading. Use any closure.

Two of those months decide the year, and neither is a month in which anything
appears to be wrong. That is the whole difficulty, and it is why the work needs
a budget line rather than a purchase order raised in a hurry.

## How we work commercial accounts

Exclusion first, monitoring second, treatment where it is warranted and not
where it is not. Practically: a walk of the envelope, the waste area and
receiving before anything is proposed; a program built on monitoring and
contained placements rather than scheduled broadcast application; structural
items written up every visit for facilities to action; records kept, handed over
and legible to somebody who is not a technician; and scheduling that respects
your trading hours and, in a school, the notification window rather than
treating it as an inconvenience.

The assessment is free, there is no term agreement, and if what your building
needs is three door sweeps, a dock seal and a conversation about where the
dumpster sits, we would rather tell you that in August than sell you a schedule
in November.
