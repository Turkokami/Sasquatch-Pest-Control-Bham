## The confusion that costs two weeks

Here is the sequence we see, several times a year, and it wastes the same
two weeks every time.

A sale is under way. Somebody's report comes back with findings — dampwood
termites in the sill plate, carpenter ant galleries in a rim joist, decay in a
crawlspace. The buyer's lender wants it addressed. Everybody starts phoning
pest control companies.

And then a week disappears while people work out that the document and the
treatment are two different things done by two different kinds of licensee, and
that the company they called can do one and not the other.

This page exists to compress that week into a phone call. It is not legal
advice — we are a pest control company and not attorneys — but the distinction
is not complicated once somebody sets it out.

## What counts as a wood-destroying organism

Broader than people assume. In Washington the group covers organisms that
attack structural material: **fungus, termites, carpenter ants and wood-boring
beetles.**

Two things follow from that list.

**Decay fungus is on it.** In this climate that is the finding we encounter most,
and it is frequently the most consequential. Wood that has been wet long enough
for rot to establish has lost strength permanently, and it does not recover on
drying.

**Carpenter ants are on it.** Homeowners tend to file carpenter ants under
nuisance insects rather than under structural pests. In a transaction they are
neither — they are a wood-destroying organism, and a finding involving them
carries the same weight as a termite finding.

## Two different documents

This is the part worth understanding properly.

**A complete WDO inspection report** is the regulated document. Under
**WAC 16-228-2045**, a report identifying damage or infestation by
wood-destroying organisms must be a complete report, and a complete report has
specific requirements. Among them:

- a **WSDA Inspection Control Number**, obtained under RCW 15.58.450 and
  displayed prominently in the upper third of the front page
- the date the inspection was conducted, on the first page
- the name of the property owner, their representative, or the purchaser
- the complete property address
- the **name of the inspector and their WSDA license number**, on the first page
- documented findings of damage, infestation, and conditions conducive to either
- **diagrams** showing where findings are and where areas were inaccessible
- a list of **all excluded areas**

WSPMA material adds that it is illegal for a business to conduct complete WDO
inspections without a valid company license from the director.

**A specific inspection** is the other kind. WSPMA describes it as being for
on-site proposals or estimates — and, importantly, it may **not** be used in a
real estate transaction, a restriction that must be stated on the document in
bold print.

So the short version: the document a transaction needs is the complete one, and
it comes from a separately licensed inspector with a company license and an
inspection control number.

## Where we sit, precisely

We want to be exact about this, because being vague about it would win us work
we should not have.

**We are not structural pest inspectors.** We do not perform the inspection a
sale requires and we do not issue that report. If your transaction needs one,
you need a different licensee, and we will tell you that rather than take the
job and sort it out later.

**We are licensed to treat wood-destroying organisms.** That is a WSDA license
category — PCO Structural — and it covers the control of pests that attack
structural material, including fungus, termites, carpenter ants and wood-boring
beetles. Treating what somebody else's report identified is squarely our work.

**What you get from us afterwards is a treatment record**: which items were
treated, what product was used, where, and on what date, plus a note of which
items are building repairs rather than pest treatments. It is deliberately not
a findings document, because a document setting out findings of wood-destroying
organism damage is the regulated report described above, and issuing one is not
something we do.

That boundary is not a limitation we are apologizing for. It is the reason you
can rely on what we do say.

## What actually has to happen after a finding

A report with findings on it usually generates three separate pieces of work,
and confusing them is where money gets wasted.

**One: the water.** Almost every wood-destroying organism finding in this county
sits behind a moisture problem. Dampwood termites and moisture ants require wet
wood. Decay fungus requires wet wood. Carpenter ants prefer timber that has
already begun to soften. The water is the cause and the organism is the
consequence, so a treatment applied to a building that stays wet is a treatment
that will be needed again.

The usual sources: grade falling toward the foundation, downspouts discharging
at the wall, a missing or torn crawlspace vapor barrier, blocked or buried crawl
vents, a slow plumbing failure under a bathroom, a deck ledger with no flashing,
a failed window sill detail, a bathroom fan discharging into an attic.

Fixing that is a drainage contractor, a plumber, a roofer or a builder. Not us,
and we will say so.

**Two: the timber.** Wood that has lost structural capacity does not recover.
Where a sill plate, rim joist, post or beam has gone, it needs cutting out and
replacing, and that is a carpenter's or a builder's job. Where it is wet but
still sound under a probe, correcting the water may be enough.

**Three: the organism.** The treatment. Ours.

An efficient transaction runs those in parallel rather than in sequence. The
common failure is treating first, repairing later, and discovering the repair
exposed material that should have been treated while it was open.

## Sequencing it so it does not take a month

The order that saves time and money.

**Get the treatment quote while the report is being written.** The two are
independent. Waiting for the report to be finalized before phoning anyone adds a
week for no reason. We can look at a property and price the likely treatment
scope on the strength of what has been found so far.

**Water first, or at least concurrently.** Not because it delays anything, but
because a builder replacing a sill plate wants to know whether the drainage is
being corrected before they close the wall.

**Structural replacement before final treatment where it overlaps.** New timber
going into a location with a history is worth borate-treating before it is
covered, which costs very little at that moment and a great deal later.

**Treatment of what remains.**

**Documentation as it happens**, so that nobody is reconstructing what was done
three weeks later while a closing date moves.

## What we find behind these reports, in this county

Since the point of a report is to tell you what is there, it is worth setting
out what "there" usually turns out to be in Whatcom County, because it differs
from the national picture in ways that matter to a transaction.

**Decay comes first, and it is usually the largest number.** The climate here
does most of the work: a long, mild, persistently wet season from October to
April, and no hot dry stretch to drive moisture back out of timber that has
taken it on. Wood that gets wet in this county tends to stay wet. Findings that
read as an insect problem are, more often than not, a water problem with an
insect living in it.

**The sill plate and rim joist are the usual location.** That is the timber
sitting directly on the foundation, and it collects water from splash-back, from
grade that falls toward the building, from a foundation wicking ground moisture,
and from general crawlspace humidity. It is also structural, which is why a
finding there carries more weight than the same finding in a fence post.

**Dampwood termites rather than subterranean.** The species that drives the
industry in other parts of the country is a minor concern here. The local one
requires wood that is already wet and decaying, which constrains it — and which
means a dampwood finding is always also telling you about moisture. Worth
checking for mud tubes on a foundation wall regardless, because a subterranean
finding is a different and more serious conversation.

**Carpenter ants at scale on wooded lots.** Properties with mature trees,
stumps, standing deadwood and accumulated garden timber produce carpenter ant
findings at a rate the rest of the county does not. On those, the difficulty is
rarely the treatment; it is locating the parent colony, which may be a hundred
feet from the building on land the seller owns.

**Wood-boring beetles, frequently historic.** A beetle finding is not
automatically live. Several species infest only freshly felled or recently
milled timber and die out once the wood has seasoned, and treating those is
spending money on a problem that already ended. Establishing whether an
infestation is active matters more here than anywhere else on a report, because
the honest answer is quite often that no treatment is warranted.

**Crawlspaces that have never been looked at.** A great deal of the older
housing stock here has a vented crawlspace with an original vapor barrier in
pieces or none at all, corroded vent screens, and an access hatch that stopped
sealing decades ago. None of that is a pest finding on its own. All of it
decides whether the building has pest findings.

## Reading a report without panicking

Reports are written to be complete rather than to be reassuring, and a first
read tends to land badly. A few things that help.

**Separate the three categories.** Damage, active infestation, and conditions
conducive to infestation are different things with different urgency. A report
listing conducive conditions and no activity is describing a risk, not a
problem — and conducive conditions are usually the cheapest thing on the page
to fix.

**Look at where, not only what.** Damage in a fence post, a deck rail or a
detached shed is not damage in a sill plate. The location is what determines
whether this is a maintenance item or a structural one.

**Check the excluded areas list.** It is a required element and it tells you
what could not be seen. An inaccessible section of crawlspace is a gap in the
picture rather than a clean result for that area, and it is disclosed precisely
so it can be weighed.

**Ask what the water source is.** If the report identifies decay or a
moisture-dependent organism and nobody can say where the water is coming from,
the diagnosis is incomplete regardless of how thorough the document looks. That
question is worth asking before anybody quotes anything.

**Do not assume the estimate matches the scope.** A repair estimate and a
treatment scope cover different work. Getting them separately makes both easier
to evaluate and usually cheaper.

## The timeline, realistically

Transactions run on dates, so here is what the treatment side actually takes.

**Getting a quote: a couple of days.** We come out, look at what has been found,
and price it. There is no queue for this and it does not depend on anybody's
inspection schedule.

**Straightforward treatment: usually within the week of instruction.** A
localized carpenter ant or dampwood termite treatment is not a long job.

**Where structural replacement is involved: longer, and not our timeline.** A
builder cutting out and replacing a section of sill plate sets that schedule.
The useful thing is to get the builder looking at it in parallel rather than
after we have quoted.

**Where drainage or roofing is the underlying cause: longer again, and
weather-dependent.** This is the item most likely to slip a closing date, and
it is the one people leave until last.

The pattern worth avoiding is sequential: report, then treatment quote, then
builder quote, then drainage quote, each waiting on the last. Run them
concurrently from the day the findings land and the whole thing compresses to
about a two weeks.

## For sellers

Two things, both unglamorous.

**Deal with it before you list.** A finding discovered during a transaction is
negotiated under time pressure with somebody else's estimate in front of you,
and the concession is reliably larger than the repair would have cost. The same
work done in your own time, with your own choice of contractor, is cheaper and
less stressful.

**Look at the crawlspace.** It is where the findings come from. Sill plate and
rim joist probed rather than glanced at, vapor barrier intact, vents clear and
screened, no standing water, no timber in ground contact. If somebody is going
to find something, it is better that it is you.

## For buyers

**Read the excluded areas.** A complete report lists what could not be
inspected, and that list is genuinely informative. An inaccessible crawlspace
corner or a sealed area is not a clean bill of health for that area — it is a
gap in the picture, and it is disclosed precisely so you can weigh it.

**Look at the conducive conditions, not only the findings.** A report noting
timber in ground contact, poor drainage and inadequate ventilation with no
current infestation is telling you what will happen next. That is more
actionable than a finding, because it is cheaper to fix.

**Get the treatment quoted separately.** The scope on a repair estimate and the
scope of a pest treatment are different, and bundling them makes both harder to
evaluate.

**Ask what the water source is.** If nobody can tell you, the diagnosis is
incomplete regardless of how thorough the report looks.

## An open question we are not resolving here

In the interest of not overstating the position set out above: the WSDA
category description for PCO Structural indicates that the category may perform
*specific* wood destroying organism inspections — the proposal-and-estimate kind that
cannot be used in a transaction. WSPMA's material describes the same
distinction.

If that reading is right, our licensed scope is somewhat wider than this page
claims. We have not acted on it, because a category description and a trade
association article are not a regulatory determination, and the cost of being
wrong about a licensing boundary is not a paragraph of copy.

It is a question for WSDA or an attorney and it is on our list. Until it is
answered in writing, we describe our scope conservatively. We mention it here
rather than quietly, because a company that only discloses the uncertainties
which happen to favor it is not being straight with you.

## A note on refinancing

Most of this page is written around a sale, because that is where the volume
is, but refinancing produces the same requirement and a slightly different
dynamic.

In a refinance there is no counterparty negotiating against you, which removes
the time pressure and the concession arithmetic. What is left is a lender with
a condition to satisfy and an owner who now knows something about their own
building that they did not know last month.

That is a better position to be in than it feels. Findings discovered during a
refinance can be dealt with properly, in sequence, without a closing date
moving — water first, then structure, then treatment — which is the order that
actually holds and the one a sale rarely allows.

It is also the moment to deal with the conducive conditions rather than only the
findings, since nobody is waiting on you. Crawlspace vapor barrier, drainage,
grade, ground contact: unglamorous, one-off, and the reason the next report
comes back clean.
