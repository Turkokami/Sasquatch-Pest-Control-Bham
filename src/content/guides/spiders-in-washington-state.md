## What you are actually likely to have

Nearly every spider call we take in Whatcom County turns out to be one of six
animals, and five of them need nothing done about them at all.

**Giant house spider** — the big fast brown one, in the bathtub, in the garage,
crossing the floor at nine in the evening in September.

**Hobo spider** — a close relative, similar enough that nobody identifies it by
eye reliably, and no longer considered medically significant.

**Cross orbweaver** — the one that builds the perfect wheel across the back
door overnight and sits in the middle of it with a pale cross on its abdomen.

**Cellar spider** — the pale, spindly one in the corner of a garage ceiling
with a messy tangle of a web. The thing people call a daddy long legs.

**Yellow sac spider** — small, pale, usually spotted on a ceiling or a wall at
night, and one of the two in the state worth a second look.

**Clover mites** — not spiders at all, but they arrive by the hundred and
everybody searches for them as tiny red spiders, so they get a section here.

The rest of this guide takes them one at a time, says which is which, and is
honest about the two that genuinely matter and the one that does not live here.

## Do black widow spiders live in Washington state?

Yes, but almost certainly not where you are standing. The Washington State
Department of Health describes black widows as
[common to eastern Washington](https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/pests/spiders),
with a few small populations west of the Cascades. The animal is a dry-country
species and the eastern half of the state suits it.

Whatcom County does not. In our own work here we do not find them, and we do
not get calls that turn out to be one. The honest position is that a western
Washington black widow is possible rather than expected — the state's own
wording allows for small western populations, and spiders travel in freight,
firewood, plant deliveries and moving loads like everything else.

What a real one looks like is not subtle. The adult female is glossy jet black,
round-bodied, roughly a half-inch in the body, and carries the red hourglass on
the underside of the abdomen. She hangs upside down in a tangled, strong,
slightly crackly web, usually in something undisturbed and sheltered at ground
level — under a woodpile, in a meter box, inside a rarely used outbuilding, in
the back of a crawlspace vent well.

The health department's own description of the risk is calmer than the
reputation: the venom is dangerous, but the spider will usually try to escape
rather than bite unless she is guarding eggs.

If you are east of the mountains and you find one of these in the garage, treat
it seriously. If you are in Ferndale and you have found a shiny black spider,
the overwhelming likelihood is that it is a young orbweaver or a false widow,
and we would far rather look at a photograph than have you reach for anything.

## Giant house spider, the big one in the bathtub

The [giant house spider](/pest-library/giant-house-spider/) is the single most
common source of alarm in this county and the single most harmless.

It is brown, hairy, with long banded legs, and a leg span that can reach the
size of a drink coaster in a mature male. It builds a flat sheet web with a
funnel retreat at one edge — in a garage corner, under a workbench, behind
stored boxes, in a crawlspace. It is fast in a straight line and it stops dead,
which is the behavior that makes people jump.

It is in the bathtub because it fell in. The glaze is unclimbable and the drain
is a dead end, so a spider that went looking for water or for a female in the
night ends up stranded in the bottom of the tub, where it is found at seven in
the morning by somebody who did not want to find it.

It does not bite people in any meaningful sense, it has no medically
significant venom, and it eats a great many other things you would like less.

## Hobo spider, and the reputation it did not deserve

The [hobo spider](/pest-library/hobo-spider/) spent two decades on American
pest control websites as a flesh-rotting menace, and it is not one.

The Washington State Department of Health now states that hobo spiders are not
considered dangerous as previously thought, and that there is no evidence hobo
spider venom has caused skin necrosis or necrotic wounds in people. That is a
state health authority retracting a claim, which is rarer than it should be.

The identification problem is separate and it is real. WSU Puyallup published a
whole bulletin on it —
[how to identify, or misidentify, the hobo spider](https://wpcdn.web.wsu.edu/wp-puyallup/uploads/sites/408/2015/02/PLS-116-How-to-Identify-or-Misidentify-the-Hobo-Spider.pdf)
— and the short version is that you cannot do it from a photograph, or from the
chevron markings people are told to look for. It takes magnification and the
right anatomy. Anyone naming a hobo spider confidently from a phone picture is
guessing.

Which matters mostly because the two facts combine well: it is very hard to
tell a hobo spider from a giant house spider, and since neither is dangerous,
it has stopped being a question worth answering urgently. The whole business is
on [is this a hobo spider](/services/spider-control/is-this-a-hobo-spider/).

## Yellow sac spider, and yellow spiders in Washington state

The [yellow sac spider](/pest-library/yellow-sac-spider/) is the other one the
state names as medically significant, and it is the one most people have never
heard of.

It is small — a quarter-inch body — pale straw or greenish-cream, with a
slightly darker stripe down the abdomen and dark tips to the jaws. It does not
build a catching web. It hunts at night across walls and ceilings and spends
the day in a small silk sac in a corner, a curtain fold or a ceiling junction.
Those little white sacs in the angle of a ceiling are the usual sign.

The bite is genuinely painful and produces a red, sore, slow-healing mark. It
does not produce the dramatic tissue loss the internet attributes to it, and a
great many lesions blamed on this spider are something else entirely. The state
health department records it in eastern Washington and in the Seattle area; it
is present here, and it is the spider most likely to bite somebody in bed,
because people roll onto one that has come down a wall.

**Other yellow spiders in Washington.** The one people photograph in the garden
is usually the goldenrod crab spider — bright lemon or white, sitting
motionless with its front legs held open on a flower head, waiting for a bee.
It is an ambush hunter, it changes color slowly between yellow and white to
match the flower it is sitting on, and it is harmless to people. Young
[cross orbweavers](/pest-library/cross-orbweaver/) can also look strikingly
yellow before they darken.

## Cross orbweaver, the wheel across the doorway

If a perfect wheel of a web appeared across your back door overnight in
September, this is what made it.

The [cross orbweaver](/pest-library/cross-orbweaver/) is the classic garden
spider here: a fat round abdomen in brown, orange or cream, with a pattern of
white dots that reads as a cross. It builds a new orb most nights, sits head
down in the middle of it, and takes it apart and eats it again before morning.

It is harmless. It is also one of the best things in a garden, and the sensible
response to one across a doorway is to move the web rather than the spider —
break one anchor line with a stick and it will rebuild a foot to the left that
night. Knocking it down every morning for a fortnight achieves nothing except
making the spider rebuild, which costs it a great deal and costs you nothing.

## Cellar spider, or daddy long legs, and the venom myth

The pale spindly thing in the corner of the garage ceiling with legs like
threads is a [cellar spider](/pest-library/cellar-spider/), and it is the
subject of the most durable spider myth in circulation.

The claim is that it has the most powerful venom of any spider but fangs too
short to break human skin. The Burke Museum at the University of Washington
keeps a standing page on it, because it is the myth they get asked about most:
[daddy-longlegs would be deadly](https://www.burkemuseum.org/blog/myth-daddy-longlegs-would-be-deadly),
and the short answer is that nobody has ever shown it to be true.

Part of the confusion is that three different animals share the name. The
long-legged thing on the outside wall of a shed with one single body segment is
a harvestman — an arachnid, but not a spider, and it has no venom at all. The
long-legged thing bouncing off a lamp is a crane fly, which is an insect. The
pale one in the ceiling corner with a messy web is the actual cellar spider.

All three are harmless, which makes the myth unusual: it is wrong about the
venom, and it is frequently also wrong about which animal is being discussed.

## Tiny red spiders, which are not spiders

Hundreds of bright red specks on a sunny windowsill, a south-facing wall or a
concrete patio in spring or autumn, that leave a red smear if you wipe them.

Those are clover mites, and
[WSU PestSense](https://pestsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/clover-mites/)
describes them as occasional household pests that feed on clover and grasses,
do not bite people or animals, and cause no structural damage. The red smear is
body pigment, not blood.

They come from lawn growing right up against the building, and they come in
numbers because that is how they live outdoors. They cannot feed or breed
indoors and die within a day or two. The two useful responses are both
outdoors: vacuum rather than wipe the ones inside, because crushing them stains
grout, paint and curtains, and keep a band of bare ground or gravel between the
turf and the wall so there is nothing to walk in from.

They are also the reason tiny red spiders is one of the highest-volume spider
searches in the country while being almost entirely not about spiders. There is
more on them, including what to do about a wall that gets them every year, on
[the clover mite profile](/pest-library/clover-mite/).

## Black spiders in Washington state, in order of likelihood

This is the search people make when they have found something dark and want to
know whether to worry. In this county, running from most to least likely:

**A young giant house spider or hobo spider.** Immature individuals are much
darker than the adults and are far and away the most common answer.

**A false black widow.** Glossy, round-bodied, dark brown to near-black, in a
messy tangle web in a garage or crawlspace. This is the spider most often
reported to us as a black widow. It has no hourglass — at most a pale crescent
or collar at the front of the abdomen — and it is harmless.

**A ground or wolf spider that came in under a door.** Dark, stocky, hairy,
fast across the floor, no web at all. It is lost, not resident.

**A young cross orbweaver**, which can be very dark before its pattern shows.

**A jumping spider.** Small, black, often with white markings, moves in
deliberate hops and turns to look at you. Harmless and rather good company.

**An actual black widow** sits at the bottom of that list in Whatcom County,
not the top, and the glossy jet-black body with the red hourglass underneath is
not a judgment call when you see one.

## When is spider season in Washington?

Late August to the middle of October, and nothing has gone wrong when it
happens.

What changes in autumn is not the number of spiders in the building; it is
their behavior. The large house-dwelling species mature at the end of summer,
and the males then abandon their webs and walk, sometimes for weeks, looking
for females. A male that spent his whole life in a crawlspace corner is
suddenly crossing the living room floor in the open at nine in the evening. He
was there in July. He was just not walking about.

A warm summer with good insect numbers reliably produces a heavier autumn, for
the straightforward reason that more spiderlings survived to maturity. The
season ends when the males have finished, which is usually within six weeks.

The practical consequence is that nothing treated in October changes the next
six weeks much. What changes them is door sweeps, window screens and exterior
lighting, done in August. That is covered in full on
[spiders appearing every fall](/services/spider-control/spiders-appearing-every-fall/).

## What is not here, whatever the internet says

**Brown recluse.** The state health department states that brown recluse
spiders do not live in Washington. There is no established population anywhere
in the state. Specimens arrive occasionally in freight or in somebody's
belongings from the Midwest and do not persist. Nearly every recluse bite
diagnosed in this state turns out to be a bacterial skin infection, and the
medical literature on that is extensive and unkind to the spider.

**Black widows in the quantity people expect.** Covered above: an eastern
Washington animal with a small western foothold, not a Whatcom County one.

**Tarantulas, funnel-webs and the rest of the material in national articles.**
The Pacific Northwest Insect Management Handbook's
[section on spiders](https://pnwhandbooks.org/insect/structural-health/public-health/public-health-spider)
is the regional reference, and the list it covers is short.

We mention this because a national pest control article that lists five
dangerous spiders and then sells you a treatment is describing a country rather
than a county, and the difference is most of the fear.

## When a spider is actually worth treating

Rarely, and we will say so before we quote.

Spiders walk on the tips of their legs and do not groom themselves the way an
insect does, so they pick up far less residue from a treated surface than a
cockroach or an ant. A perimeter application has a real but modest effect on
them, and most of what it does achieve is indirect — reducing the insects they
came in to eat.

What actually works is mechanical, and most of it is not a pest control
purchase. Sweeping webs down and continuing to do it. Door sweeps that meet the
threshold. Screens intact. Gaps at utility penetrations closed. And the one
people resist: moving exterior lights away from doors, or switching them to
warm color temperatures, because a bright white light above a back door is an
insect trap and the spiders are simply following the food.

We do treat for spiders in a few honest situations — a heavy orbweaver load on
a commercial building's loading face, a crawlspace being worked on anyway, a
property where somebody has a genuine phobia that is affecting their life, or a
confirmed yellow sac population in a bedroom. The rest of the time the right
answer is a vacuum, a list of gaps, and the knowledge that it stops in October.
