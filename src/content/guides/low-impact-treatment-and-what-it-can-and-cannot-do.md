## A phrase with nothing behind it

There is no legal definition of eco-friendly pest control. No agency issues that
designation, no standard governs who may use the phrase, and no penalty attaches
to using it about a service that is identical to the one next door.

That is the most useful sentence on this page, and it is the one the trade has
no incentive to write.

None of which means low-impact work is a fiction. Some of it is real, measurable
and worth paying for. The problem is that the real parts and the marketing parts
travel under the same word, and the real parts are usually the less profitable
ones. So this page separates them: what genuinely reduces what you and your
household are exposed to, what is overstated, and where a low-impact approach
stops being able to do the job.

## What the labeling rules actually permit

Start with what a manufacturer is allowed to print, because it is stricter than
what a service company is allowed to say.

The National Pesticide Information Center — run by Oregon State University under
a cooperative agreement with the EPA — states plainly that "the term 'green' is
not defined by any regulatory agency," and that "the U.S. EPA does not allow
safety claims, such as 'non-toxic,' 'green,' or 'natural,' in the labeling of
any registered product."

Read that twice. The words that appear all over pest control advertising are
words that may not appear on the container the product came in.

"Organic" is different, and it is worth being precise. It has a real definition,
administered by the USDA National Organic Program, and products allowed in
organic production are a defined set. But that definition is built for
agricultural production. It is not a household safety rating, and a material
permitted in organic farming is not thereby harmless in a kitchen.

## What genuinely lowers exposure

Four things, in order of how much difference they make.

**Exclusion, which uses no product at all.** This is the floor of the whole
argument and it is not close. A crawl vent rescreened in quarter-inch metal, a
crawl hatch made to close, a pipe penetration sealed, a door sweep replaced —
these keep the animal out permanently, cannot be tracked onto a floor by a dog,
cannot wash into a ditch, and never need reapplying. On the older housing stock
in this county, exclusion resolves a large share of rodent work outright.
Nothing marketed as green comes close to it, because the comparison is against
zero.

**Changing the conditions.** Drying a crawlspace, extending a downspout, cutting
a hedge back off the siding, lidding a compost bin, picking up fallen fruit,
moving the pet food. Also zero product, and in this climate it removes whole
species at once. UC IPM's cockroach guidance is explicit that removing food
sources "will greatly enhance the effects of baits," and that insecticides work
best "when combined with sanitation and exclusion practices."

**Baits placed where children and pets cannot reach them.** This is the single
biggest change available once a product is genuinely warranted. UC IPM's ant
guidance states that baits are "a key tool for managing ants and the only type
of insecticide recommended in most situations," and that "when properly used,
baits are more effective and safer than sprays." The same document notes that
"perimeter treatments pose more risk of environmental upset than baits in bait
stations." A gel bead in a hinge void or a station under a cabinet toe kick is a
tiny quantity of material in a place nobody touches. A band sprayed around a
house is a much larger quantity on surfaces everybody walks past.

**Targeted application instead of broadcast.** Crack and crevice work puts
material into the gap behind the trim, the void the wasps are using, the
plumbing chase. The volume difference between that and a perimeter band is not
subtle — it is the difference between treating linear inches of gap and treating
the whole footprint of a building. And the material that is not applied is
material that cannot be tracked in, breathed, or washed off a hard surface into
a storm drain. UC IPM warns directly that "pesticides applied in your home and
landscape can move and contaminate creeks, rivers, and oceans," which in a
county whose ditches run to the Nooksack and to Bellingham Bay is not an
abstract concern.

There is a fifth item and it belongs at the top of the list rather than the
bottom: **not treating**. An application that was not warranted has an
environmental profile of exactly its own volume, and no benefit to offset it. A
company willing to tell you that the spiders in your garage in October are
seasonal and will resolve on their own has just delivered the lowest-impact
service available.

## What is overstated

Now the other half, and this is where the trade earns its reputation.

**"Natural" tells you nothing about hazard.** NPIC states it about as directly as
a public agency can: "Some of the most toxic substances on earth are natural,
such as snake venom, ricin, and arsenic." Botanical materials are chemicals.
NPIC's own guidance on natural and biological products notes that plant oils
"can be harmful to the eyes, and people may be allergic to them," that metallic
materials like zinc and sulfur "can contaminate surface water," and concludes
that "no matter how natural, all pesticides should be used with care." Risk
depends on toxicity and exposure, not on origin.

**The minimum-risk exemption is a paperwork status, not a safety finding.** This
one deserves unpacking because it is the technical fact underneath a lot of
green marketing. Under section 25(b) of FIFRA, a product whose active
ingredients come from a published list, whose inert ingredients come from an
approved list, which lists all its ingredients on the label, which makes no
claim to control organisms that threaten human health, which identifies its
producer, and which does not make false or misleading statements, is exempt from
the requirement to be registered federally.

Exempt from registration. That is the whole of it. EPA's own guidance on this
category notes that because the agency does not review exempted products, claims
implying federal endorsement are misleading. Nobody at EPA has assessed whether
the product works or how safe it is in use — the exemption is granted on the
composition of the formula, not on a review of the product.

So "EPA-exempt" on a service brochure means the opposite of what a reader
naturally takes it to mean. A registered conventional product has been through a
federal review. An exempt one has not.

**Frequency is left out of the arithmetic.** A product that has to be reapplied
every month is not obviously lower impact than one application that resolves the
problem. Twelve visits put twelve doses into a building and create twelve
opportunities for exposure, and the total volume over a year can comfortably
exceed the conventional alternative. Short residual life is a genuine advantage
for the environment outside and a genuine disadvantage for how often somebody
has to come and spray your kitchen. Both belong in the comparison and only one
of them usually appears in the sales conversation.

**A green plan is often the same plan.** The most common version of this in the
trade is a service where the schedule, the route, the equipment and the coverage
are identical to the standard program, and the only change is which product is
in the tank. That is not a lower-impact method. The method is what decides
impact — whether anybody identified the species, whether anything was closed up,
whether the application was placed or sprayed. Swapping the bottle while keeping
the broadcast is a change of label rather than a change of exposure.

**"Safe for children and pets" is not a claim anyone may make.** It cannot go on
a registered label, and a service company saying it is stating something the
manufacturer is legally barred from stating. The honest version is a set of
specifics: what the material is, where it went, what the label requires by way
of re-entry, and what you should do differently for a day.

## What we actually use, said plainly

We use conventional registered products where they are the right tool for the
job, and we are not going to pretend otherwise in order to sound better.

There are situations where a botanical or a minimum-risk material is a
reasonable choice, and we will use one when it is. There are more situations
where it is not — a German cockroach population in a multi-unit building, a bed
bug introduction, a wasp nest in a wall void beside a doorway in August — and in
those, reaching for a weaker material because it markets better means a longer
problem, more visits, and more total material.

What we do control, and what actually moves the exposure number, is the part
before the product is chosen: whether the species was identified, whether the
threshold was genuinely crossed, whether the building was closed first, how
little goes out, and where exactly it is placed. Those decisions matter more to
what your family is exposed to than the contents of the bottle, and they are the
decisions that never appear in advertising because they are hard to photograph.

## Where the product actually ends up

The exposure conversation usually happens at the level of the bottle. It is more
useful at the level of the surface.

A perimeter application puts a band of material along the base of the exterior
walls and often several feet up and out from them. That band sits on siding, on
concrete, on the soil at the drip line, and on whatever plants are growing
against the house. It is on the path the dog walks, on the ground a toddler puts
a hand on, and on the hard surfaces that drain to a ditch. In this climate it
also sits there through a season of rain.

Which is the local complication nobody mentions. Thirty-five inches of rain,
most of it arriving between October and April, does two things to an exterior
application: it shortens the useful life of the treatment, and it moves whatever
is left somewhere else. An exterior band applied in November is doing less work
and moving further than the same band applied in July. That argues for
exterior work being seasonal and sparing, and for indoor problems being solved
indoors, at the crack, rather than by treating the whole outside of a building
in the wet.

A crack-and-crevice application ends up somewhere quite different: inside a
void, behind a toe kick, in the gap under a threshold, in a wall cavity through
a drilled port. Those are places with no foot traffic, no rain and no contact.
The quantity is measured in fractions of a fluid ounce rather than in gallons of
finished spray.

That is why the placement question matters more than the product question. Two
technicians using the identical material can produce exposure profiles an order
of magnitude apart, and the difference is entirely in where they chose to put
it.

## Non-chemical tools that do real work

Worth naming, because they get treated as marginal when several of them are the
main event.

**Heat.** Whole-room thermal treatment is a serious tool for bed bugs, and it
leaves no residue at all. It is not a soft option — it is a demanding,
equipment-heavy process — but the exposure profile is close to zero.

**Vacuuming and steam.** Genuinely effective on cluster flies at a window, on
overwintering beetles, on carpet beetle and clothes moth material, on spider egg
sacs, and as the first step on a bed bug job. It physically removes the animal
rather than waiting for it to encounter something.

**Trapping.** For rodents, well-placed traps on identified runs are frequently
the whole job and remove the animal rather than leaving it to die somewhere
inside a wall.

**Dusts placed in voids.** Small quantities of material blown into a wall cavity
or a weep line stay dry, stay put and last. Worth noting that a dust is still a
pesticide, however natural its origin sounds — it carries an inhalation hazard
and its own label directions, and the fact that a homeowner can buy it in a
garden center says nothing about how casually it should be used.

**Simply waiting.** Wasp and hornet colonies here collapse with the first hard
frosts. Male spiders walking in October are at the end of their lives. Cluster
flies in a spring window are last autumn's. Time is a control method and it is
free.

## Home remedies, briefly

People ask, so it is worth answering rather than being smug about it.

NPIC's position on homemade pesticide mixtures is not that they are wicked; it
is that nobody has checked them. "Home remedies may not be tested to show
whether they can kill or repel the pest in the way they claim," and — the part
that matters more — "home remedies often do not come with labels that tell you
how to use them, how much, where, and how often. This could lead to overuse and
harm to people, pets, and the environment."

That is the real risk with a kitchen mixture. Not that it is potent, but that
there is no dose, no site restriction, no re-entry interval and no warning
statement, so the natural response to it not working is to use more of it in
more places. A registered product's label is a constraint, and constraints are
protective.

The remedies that do work are mostly not chemical at all: a sealed container, a
door sweep, a bin with a lid, a dehumidifier in the right room, and a vacuum.

## The practical questions, if you have children, pets or a garden

Ask these and you will learn more than any label on a van will tell you.

**What is the product and where is it going?** A name and a location. "Around
the outside" is not a location; "the base of the exterior wall on the north and
east elevations, and into the gap behind the kitchen toe kick" is.

**What does the label require?** The label is the law, and it carries the
re-entry conditions, the ventilation requirements and the site restrictions. A
technician who cannot tell you what their own label says has not read it.

**How long until it is dry, and what changes for that period?** For most interior
work the meaningful interval is until the material has dried, and the practical
advice is to keep children and animals off treated surfaces until then.

**Are there specific warnings for cats, fish or bees?** Several materials carry
them, and an aquarium, a pond or a beehive on the property genuinely changes
what should be used and where. Say so before the visit rather than after.

**Can this be done with a bait instead?** Frequently yes, and the answer tells
you how the company thinks.

**Can this be done with no product at all?** On rodent work in an older house
here, the honest answer is often that closing the building is most of the job.

## Pollinators, and being careful about what we claim

Flowering plants in bloom are where bees are, and applications to blooming
vegetation are the ones with the clearest potential to reach them. Timing,
placement and simply not treating flowering material are the tools, and they
cost nothing.

Honey bees are not a pest and we do not treat them. A honey bee colony in a wall
is a beekeeper's job and gets referred as one. Bumble bees nesting in a bank or
under a shed are a nuisance for a few weeks in one season and then gone, and in
most cases the correct advice is to leave them.

What we will not claim is that any application is harmless to insects other than
the target. Materials that kill wasps kill other insects. The honest position is
that the way to protect the ones you want is to apply less, apply it in the
right place, and apply it at the right time of day — not to describe a broad
application as selective because the brochure says so.

## Where a low-impact approach runs out of road

Being honest about the limits is the other half of being honest about the
benefits.

**Bed bugs.** There is no version of this problem that resolves through
sanitation and a botanical spray. It takes a properly executed treatment,
preparation done thoroughly, and follow-up. Attempts to handle it gently
generally spread it, because disturbed bugs move along wall lines into adjacent
rooms.

**German cockroaches in a shared building.** Sanitation is essential and it is
not sufficient. Baiting works, but it has to be sustained and it has to cover
the whole affected structure rather than the unit that complained.

**An open building under seasonal rodent pressure.** Exclusion is the answer and
exclusion takes time — a proper job on an older house is a real day of work, not
a tube of sealant. In the meantime, in October, with a population already
established, mechanical and chemical control carry the load. Refusing to use
them because the ideal answer is structural means living with the animal until
the structural work happens.

**Established structural infestations.** Where wood-destroying organisms are
active in a building, the material and the placement are decided by the biology
and the construction, not by preference. That work also sits behind a licensing
line worth understanding before hiring anyone for it.

## The trade-off nobody states

Lower impact usually means more work, and most of the extra work lands on the
occupant rather than the technician.

A broadcast application asks nothing of you. A bait program asks you to stop
leaving competing food out, because the bait is competing with whatever else is
available. Exclusion asks you to allow access, clear a crawl hatch and let
somebody spend a day on the building. Habitat work asks you to move the compost,
pick the fruit up and cut the hedge back. Bed bug and cockroach preparation asks
for several hours of genuine effort and largely decides the outcome.

That is a real cost and it is why the low-impact route is not simply the better
route in every household. Somebody managing a rental at a distance, or a person
who physically cannot do the preparation, is making a different calculation, and
pretending otherwise is not honesty, it is just a different sales pitch.

Worth saying out loud in the other direction: if there is a pregnancy, an
infant, someone with asthma, a fish tank, a beehive, or anybody in the household
who reacts badly to chemical exposure, say so before the visit rather than
after. It changes what should be used and where, and it is much easier to plan
around in advance than to correct once the work is done.

## The summary a brochure will not print

Most of what genuinely reduces exposure is not a product and is not sold by the
gallon. It is identification, restraint, closing the building, drying it out,
and putting a very small amount of the right material exactly where the insect
goes rather than a large amount where people walk.

Most of what is sold as green is a substitution at the last step, leaving the
first four unchanged.

And the strongest version of low-impact pest control is the visit where somebody
looks properly, tells you what is going on, and then tells you that you do not
need a treatment. That one is free here, and we would rather do it than sell you
something you can do without.
