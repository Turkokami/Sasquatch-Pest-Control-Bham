## Buildings that never close, holding people who cannot leave

Two facts shape every program in this category, and everything else follows from
them.

**The building never closes.** There is no shutdown, no empty week, no
after-hours in the sense a restaurant has one. Whatever gets done is done
around people who are living or being treated in the building at the time.

**The occupants cannot easily move away from a problem.** A resident in memory
care, a patient in a treatment room, somebody in a bed they cannot get out of
without help — none of them can leave the room while something is dealt with,
and none of them chose the situation.

That combination pushes the whole program toward the same answer: close the
building, monitor it closely enough to find things early, contain what is found,
and reserve anything else for the narrow set of situations that genuinely
require it. It is the approach we would argue for anyway. Here it is not
optional.

## The categories inside one heading

"Healthcare" covers buildings with very little in common, and the pest picture
differs enough to be worth separating.

**Clinics, medical and dental offices.** Predictable hours, a kitchen or break
room that behaves like a small office kitchen, clinical waste on its own
stream, and a public waiting area with food coming in constantly. Structurally
straightforward, and the reputational exposure is high relative to the pest
pressure.

**Veterinary practices and boarding.** Animal food, animal waste, bedding, and
kennels that are warm and continuously occupied. Genuinely higher pest pressure
than human healthcare, and a narrower toolbox because of the animals.

**Assisted living and independent living.** Effectively multifamily housing with
a commercial kitchen attached and a care obligation on top. Shared walls,
shared voids, individual apartments with individual housekeeping, and a
dining operation feeding everyone.

**Memory care.** The same as above with two differences that matter: residents
may not be able to report a problem, and may not be able to leave a room. Both
push the program further toward containment and exclusion.

**Skilled nursing.** Clinical, continuous, heavily inspected, and the most
documentation-dependent of the group. It is also the setting where a single
finding in the wrong room carries the most weight, which is why the monitoring
density is higher and the escalation route shorter than anywhere else we work.

A single operator may run several of these in one building, which is why the
program is scoped by area rather than by site.

## What actually turns up

**Rodents**, and inside Bellingham that means rats — Norway rats at ground level
using foundations, drains and service voids, roof rats where roofline access
exists. Facilities out toward Lynden, Everson and the rural districts see more
mice, off surrounding open ground. The routes are structural and predictable:
loading and kitchen doors, utility penetrations, roof plant, and the ceiling
and service voids that connect wings.

**Cockroaches**, mostly German, arriving in cardboard and deliveries and
establishing in kitchen and laundry voids. In a connected building they travel
through plumbing chases exactly as they do in an apartment block.

**Small flies** from drains and organic film — kitchen, dish area, laundry,
sluice rooms and mop stores. As everywhere, the adults are not the problem.

**Ants**, predominantly odorous house ants, drawn to break rooms, resident
kitchenettes and anywhere food is stored in small amounts in many places.

**Bed bugs**, which are the highest-stakes item in senior living and are dealt
with below.

**Overwintering insects** on sunlit walls in autumn, which generate complaints
disproportionate to any risk.

## Bed bugs in senior living

This is the problem most likely to become a public one, and the handling matters
more than the treatment.

**Scope by building, not by room.** A room treated in isolation, in a building
with connected voids and adjoining walls, is likely to recur — either because
the population had already spread or because it moves during the work. The
correct scope is the reporting room plus the adjoining rooms on all sides,
inspected before quoting.

**They arrive with belongings and with people.** Admissions, returns from
hospital, visiting family, and furniture brought from home are the routes.
A facility that inspects incoming furniture and belongings at admission prevents
more infestations than any treatment schedule.

**Reporting culture decides the size of the problem.** A resident who fears
being blamed, moved or charged will not report early, and late reports are large
infestations. Whatever the policy is, it works better framed as maintenance than
as fault. That is a facility decision rather than a pest control one, and we
will say so plainly because it does more good than anything we apply.

**Dignity is part of the job.** These are people's homes. Work is scheduled with
staff, discussed with the resident where they are able to take part, and done
without an audience.

Bed bug verification carries a fee, credited against treatment if the facility
proceeds. In a connected building that verification is genuine diagnostic work
across several rooms rather than a look at one mattress.

## The kitchen, laundry and sluice

The service areas carry most of the actual pest pressure, and they run harder
than their equivalents elsewhere.

**The kitchen** is a commercial kitchen feeding a captive population three times
a day, every day, with no closed period. Receiving, dry storage, dish area,
drains and waste behave exactly as they do in food service, and the same three
things decide it: drains, deliveries and the back door.

**Laundry** is warm, damp, full of soft material and continuously occupied by
linen. Lint accumulation behind and beneath machines is both harborage and a
maintenance issue.

**Sluice and soiled utility rooms** are drain-and-film environments and produce
small fly complaints that are almost always solved by cleaning rather than
treatment.

**Waste handling**, which in a facility is higher volume, includes clinical
streams on separate schedules, and sits at a loading area that is also the
building's weakest structural point.

## Veterinary and boarding, which is its own problem

Veterinary practices and boarding kennels sit under this heading and behave
unlike the rest of it, so they are worth separating.

**The pressure is genuinely higher.** Animal feed, animal waste, bedding, and
warm kennel runs occupied around the clock make a building that supports a
population far more readily than a clinic does. Rodent pressure in particular
tends to be constant rather than seasonal.

**The toolbox is narrower.** Animals are present, often confined, frequently
unwell, and several species are far more sensitive than people to things that
would be unremarkable elsewhere. Rodenticide in particular has to be treated as
a last resort and, where used at all, contained and placed where no animal can
reach it or reach something that has. Secondary exposure — an animal reaching a
rodent rather than the bait — is the risk people forget.

That pushes veterinary work almost entirely onto exclusion, sanitation and
mechanical control, which is slower and more structural and is the only honest
approach in a building full of patients.

**Food storage is the center of it**, exactly as it is on a farm. Bagged feed
off the floor and in hard containers, spillage cleared on a schedule somebody
owns, and the storeroom sealed as tightly as the building allows.

**Flies are a welfare issue rather than a nuisance one**, particularly around
kennels, runs and waste. As everywhere, they are won at the breeding site — the
bedding, the run drainage and the waste store — rather than on the adults.

## Construction, renovation and the reopened envelope

Healthcare buildings are almost permanently under some form of works, and every
project reopens the envelope.

A wall opened for a cable run, a ceiling grid lifted for ductwork, a new door
cut into an elevation, a roof plant replaced — each of those is a hole in a
building that was sealed, made by a contractor whose job is not pest exclusion
and who will not think to close it in a way that holds.

Two things follow. Works should be notified to the program rather than
discovered on the next visit, because a gap open for three months during a
project is how a population establishes in a building that had none. And the
end of a project is the right moment to check the envelope, while access is
still easy and somebody is still on site who can fix what is found.

Dust and disturbance also move existing populations. A renovation in one wing
routinely produces sightings in the wing next door, which look like a new
problem and are usually an old one relocating. Knowing that in advance saves a
great deal of alarm.

## Working invisibly

Discretion here is not about appearances, it is about not adding to what
residents and patients are already dealing with.

Vehicles and technicians stay unobtrusive. Visits are scheduled around meal
service, shift changes, therapy and clinical schedules rather than around our
route. Placements go where residents, visitors and pets cannot reach them and
where they cannot be seen from a corridor. Mechanical control carries more of
the load than it does elsewhere, because it introduces nothing.

Where something cannot be done without disturbing an area, it is planned with
the facility rather than sprung on it — including telling you when the honest
answer is that a room needs to be vacant for an hour.

## Courtyards, gardens and where residents actually sit

The outdoor spaces at a senior living site are used more heavily than the
equivalent at any other kind of building, and they generate work that is easy to
overlook because it is nobody's department.

**Stinging insects are the clearest safety issue on the property.** August and
September are the heaviest weeks of our year, which is exactly when courtyards
and patios are in constant use. A nest in a patio umbrella, a heater housing, a
handrail void or the eave above a garden door is a different order of risk when
the people beneath it move slowly, may not see it, and may not be able to get
away from it quickly. These are calls we prioritize out of order and we would
rather be called about a suspicion than about a sting.

**Bird feeders and resident gardens.** Both are genuinely good for residents and
both are food sources at the wall of the building. This is one of the few places
we will suggest a compromise rather than a removal — moving a feeder well away
from the elevation, and choosing seed that produces less spillage, keeps the
thing residents value without feeding a rodent population against the
foundation.

**Landscaping held against the building.** Shrubs against siding, deep bark mulch
to the foundation line, and irrigation keeping ground permanently damp are
standard in managed landscaping and all work against the envelope. Pulling
planting back from the wall is cheap and it is resisted on appearance grounds
more often than on cost.

**Smoking areas and the ground around them**, which accumulate litter and are
usually adjacent to a door that gets propped.

## Staff, and the part that changes the outcome

More than in any other setting, the result here tracks a small number of things
staff do, and staff turnover means those have to be said again periodically
rather than once.

- Report a sighting rather than assuming somebody else has, and report it the
  same shift
- Do not prop external doors, particularly the loading and kitchen doors in warm
  weather
- Break down and remove cardboard rather than stacking it in a corridor or a
  store
- Check incoming furniture and belongings at admission, which prevents more bed
  bug work than any treatment
- Keep the mop store, the sluice and the laundry lint traps clean, because those
  are breeding sites rather than housekeeping details
- Tell us about building works, because a contractor has just opened the
  envelope

None of this is complicated and all of it is forgettable, which is why we will
brief a team in ten minutes when it is useful rather than assume it was covered
at induction.

## The record

Facilities are inspected and surveyed by more parties than most operators
outside the sector expect, and the pest program is a small but reliably
requested part of it.

Every visit produces a dated record: what was found and where, what was done,
what was recommended and to whom, and the state of every monitoring device.
Where devices are in use, a map somebody unfamiliar with the building can
follow, numbered consistently with the record. Licenses, product labels and
safety documentation for anything applied.

Across a year that becomes a trend, which is what a surveyor is actually
reading — not whether there was ever a finding, but whether findings were acted
on and whether the same one keeps recurring in the same place.

We are not your surveyor and a pest program does not guarantee any inspection
outcome. It removes one common reason for a poor one and demonstrates a managed
system.

## The year

**Late winter to spring.** The structural window such as it is. Exclusion at the
loading area, kitchen doors, utility penetrations and roof plant, planned around
a building that cannot be closed.

**Spring.** Ants appear in break rooms and resident kitchenettes. Small fly
pressure rises with temperature.

**Summer.** Fly pressure at its peak, waste volume up, doors propped in warm
weather. Stinging insects build toward the heaviest weeks in August and
September, and around a facility they are an access and safety problem at
entries, patios and courtyards where residents sit.

**Autumn.** Rain displaces the outdoor rodent population and the first sustained
cold decides where it goes. A continuously heated building with a loading door
is the best address available. This is when the year's exclusion work proves
itself.

- Access to kitchen, laundry, sluice, storage and mechanical areas on visit days
- Receiving and admissions standards for incoming goods, furniture and

## Starting

We walk the building with somebody who knows the estate and somebody who knows
the kitchen, and on a residential facility ideally somebody who knows the care
operation as well. The walk covers receiving and loading, kitchen and dry
storage, laundry and sluice, waste, mechanical and service voids, resident or
clinical areas as far as is appropriate, and the exterior envelope.

What comes back is a written program: areas in scope, what is sealed and what is
monitored, device map and numbering, visit frequency and timing against the
building's rhythm, escalation route for interior findings, the reporting
package, and the housekeeping and receiving items that will decide the result.

There is no long-term contract requirement. For a walk, call or text.
