## Judged on an ordinary Tuesday

A processing plant is audited on its records over a year. A restaurant is judged
on a walkthrough that arrives without an appointment, looks at what is there
that day, and writes down what it sees.

That difference shapes everything. There is no preparing for it, because the
point of it is that you cannot. So the only strategy that works is a building
and a program that hold on an ordinary day — a Tuesday in February when the
walk-in is being restocked, someone has called in sick, and nobody was expecting
anybody.

Which means the useful question is not "are we ready for the inspector". It is
"what does this building do when nobody is watching it", and the answer is
almost always about three things: the drains, the deliveries and the back door.

## The three that account for most of it

**Drains and the organic film.** The single largest source of recurring
complaints in food service, and the one most often treated at the wrong end.
Small flies — drain flies, phorid flies, fruit flies — do not fly in. They breed
in a film of organic material, and a restaurant produces that film continuously
in drain lines, under equipment plinths, in floor cracks, in a mop sink, behind
and beneath anything that is never moved. Spraying the adults clears the air for
an evening. The population is back inside a week because the breeding site is
untouched.

**Deliveries.** The other end of the building, and the one nobody watches.
Cockroaches arrive in cardboard, produce, beverage crates and returnable
containers. Stored product insects arrive inside dry goods. A restaurant with
excellent housekeeping and no receiving standard will keep importing problems it
then pays to remove.

**The back door and the dumpster.** The back door stands open in a busy kitchen
because the kitchen is hot, and it is the shortest route between the dumpster
enclosure and the food. Whatever the enclosure is supporting has a direct,
regularly opened path inside. On a great many sites we walk, the whole rodent
picture is a dumpster twenty feet from a propped door.

Fix those three and most of the rest becomes manageable. Skip them and no visit
schedule will hold.

## Cockroaches, and the difference between elimination and management

German cockroaches are the defining restaurant pest, and it is worth being clear
about something the trade is often vague on: a German cockroach population can
be eliminated. Not suppressed, not managed — eliminated. If a program is not
aiming at that, you are buying maintenance on a problem that did not have to be
permanent.

Getting there needs a few things done properly.

**Identification first.** German cockroaches, which breed indoors in warm voids
and equipment, are a different job from the larger, slower roaches that come up
from a damp crawlspace, a floor drain or a sewer connection. The second is a
moisture and exclusion problem and baiting alone will not settle it. Deciding
which is on the glue board before quoting is the difference between a program
that ends it and one that repeats.

**Baiting rather than repellent spraying.** Repellents scatter a population into
voids you cannot reach and make the job longer. This is the most common way a
restaurant's own efforts, or a cheap contract, make things worse before we
arrive.

**Getting into the harborage.** They live inside equipment — motor housings,
control panels, the void under a plinth, the gap behind a stainless splashback,
the hollow leg of a prep table. Work that treats the room and not the equipment
treats the wrong place.

**Frequency at the start.** Two or three visits close together at the beginning,
not one a month for a quarter. The population is on a breeding cycle and the
schedule has to be built against it rather than against a billing period.

After that, recurring service is prevention: monitoring, receiving discipline
and keeping the conditions from rebuilding. That is a genuinely different
purchase from the initial clearance, and it should be described as one.

## Rodents in a restaurant

Inside Bellingham the rodent picture is rats — Norway rats at ground level using
foundations, drains and voids, and roof rats where trees, wires or a fence reach
a roofline. Mice are more of a county animal than a city one, which surprises
people.

In a restaurant the places that matter are:

- **The dumpster enclosure and the ground around it**, which is the reservoir
- **The back door and its threshold**, which is the route
- **Drain lines and any abandoned or dry connection**, which is the route nobody
  looks at
- **Voids behind and beneath equipment**, which is the harborage
- **Dry storage and the racking**, which is the reward
- **Ceiling voids and above suspended tiles**, particularly in older downtown
  buildings

The work is exclusion first — the threshold, the penetrations, the gap at a
pipe, the vent with no screen — then trapping to clear what is inside, then
management of the exterior conditions that keep pressure on the building.
Treating without closing produces a permanent contract, which is a good business
model for somebody and a poor purchase for you.

## Bars, breweries and taprooms

Bellingham has an unusual density of breweries and taprooms for a city this
size, and they are not simply restaurants that serve less food. The pest picture
is different enough to be worth its own paragraph.

**Small flies are the dominant problem and beer is the reason.** Sugar residue
gets everywhere a line runs and everywhere a glass is set down: drip trays, the
underside of a bar top, the well, the gap between a cooler and the wall, the
floor beneath a keg cooler, and the drain that takes the rinse. Fruit flies
breed in a film that is thinner than most people picture — a residue nobody
would call a spill is enough. The work is finding the ones that never get
cleaned because they are never seen.

**Drip trays and the soda gun holster** are the two most reliably productive
places on any bar we inspect, and they are on nobody's cleaning rota because
they look self-cleaning.

**Keg and cold rooms** bring the cold-storage problem in miniature: activity
concentrates at the warm side of the envelope, at the door seals and surrounds,
and in the condensate drain, which stays wet permanently.

**Production breweries** add grain. Spent grain is a substantial volume of wet
organic material leaving the building on a schedule, and where it is stored
before collection decides whether it is a fly and rodent problem or just waste.
Malt storage brings stored product insects, which arrive inside the sack rather
than through the door.

Late hours also change the scheduling. A taproom closing at midnight and opening
at noon has a genuinely narrow service window, and the program has to be built
into it rather than fitted around a nine-to-five assumption.

## Patios, decks and outdoor service

Outdoor seating extended the season here and it added exposure, so it is worth
saying what changes.

Wasps are the main one, and August and September are the heaviest weeks of our
year. A nest in a patio umbrella, under a deck rail, in a heater housing or
above a service door is a customer-facing safety problem rather than a nuisance,
and on a patio it is also a liability question. These are the calls we
prioritize out of order.

Waste on a patio is the second. A bin that works indoors is under-specified
outdoors in summer, and an open bin twenty feet from tables is a fly generator
and a wasp attractant in one.

The third is structural: decking creates a covered void at ground level, and
where that void sits against a building with food in it, it is harborage in the
best possible position. Screening the perimeter of a deck is unglamorous and it
does more than anything applied.

## Shared buildings, which change the scope

A great deal of Bellingham's food service sits in connected buildings: downtown
blocks, Fairhaven, strip retail on the arterials, and converted older stock
where a restaurant shares a wall, a chase and a roof void with a neighbor.

In those buildings your pest scope is not your lease. A German cockroach
population in the unit next door has access to your kitchen through the same
plumbing chase that connects your sinks. A rodent problem in a shared ceiling
void belongs to everybody. And a dumpster enclosure shared between six tenants
is managed to the standard of the least careful of them.

We will say plainly when a problem cannot be resolved inside your four walls and
what the building-level scope would be. That is a harder conversation than
treating your own space, and it is the honest one. Where a landlord is
responsible for common areas, a dated record of findings is what turns a request
into something actionable.

## Working around service

Discretion and scheduling are not a nicety in food service, they are most of
what makes a program tolerable.

Visits happen before opening or after close on almost every account. Vehicles
can stay off the frontage. Technicians work in plain uniform. Where daytime
access is unavoidable, back of house comes first and the front is a walk-through
rather than a production.

Placements go where they cannot be seen by guests, cannot be reached by staff
carrying trays, and cannot affect food, packaging or contact surfaces. In a food
setting mechanical control — trapping, monitoring, exclusion — carries more of
the load than it does elsewhere, because it introduces nothing into the
environment.

And where a job genuinely cannot be done safely around food, we will say so and
propose the alternative rather than working around the constraint quietly.

## Grease, waste and the outside of the building

The exterior is where most restaurant pest pressure is generated, and it is
usually somebody else's job, which is why it drifts.

**The grease interceptor.** Organic material, warmth and moisture in a confined
space. On the schedule it is fine. Off the schedule it is a fly breeding site
and an odor complaint waiting to happen.

**The dumpster enclosure.** Whether the lids close, whether the gates close,
whether the ground can be washed and is washed, and how often it is emptied
through summer. This single item moves more rodent pressure than anything inside
the building.

**Cardboard.** Stacked flat against a wall outside, it is harborage and it is
also the thing cockroaches arrived in. Broken down and removed promptly it is
neither. Corrugated board is close to ideal cockroach harborage — warm, dry, dark,
and full of flutes exactly the width of an insect that wants its back and its
belly touching something at once. A stack held for a week is not storage, it is
a breeding site with a delivery schedule.

**Drainage and standing water**, including where a hose is used to wash down and
the water has nowhere to go.

**Vegetation and stored equipment** held against the building, which gives cover
right up to the wall.

## The record, and what to have ready

Restaurants do not face the annual audit burden a processing plant does, but
there is a version of it and it turns up unannounced.

What a program produces is a dated record of each visit: what was found, what
was done, what was recommended and to whom. Where devices are used, a map
somebody unfamiliar with the site can follow. And the licensing and product
documentation for anything applied.

The value of this is not that it satisfies anybody by itself. It is that when
somebody asks whether you have a pest management program, the answer is a file
rather than a sentence, and that the recommendations you have been given exist
in writing with dates on them.

We are not your health authority and a pest program does not guarantee an
inspection outcome. It removes one of the standard reasons for a poor one.

## Staff, and the part training actually changes

Every restaurant has turnover, and pest outcomes track a small number of
behaviors that new staff do not automatically know:

- Breaking down and removing cardboard rather than stacking it
- Not propping the back door, or propping it with a screen in place
- Reporting a sighting rather than assuming somebody else has
- Keeping the mop sink and its surrounds clean, because it is a breeding site
- Rotating dry stock and dealing with a split bag immediately
- Not stacking anything against the wall in dry storage, so the floor can be
  seen

We will brief a team on these when it is useful, in ten minutes rather than an
hour, because the list is short and the return on it is larger than anything
else available for the same money.

## The year in food service

**Spring.** Small fly pressure begins as temperatures rise. Ants appear,
predominantly odorous house ants, and a repellent spray from a cash-and-carry
will split the colony rather than kill it.

**Summer.** Peak fly pressure, patio and outdoor service adding exposure, waste
volume up, and doors open. The dumpster enclosure decides the summer.

**August and September.** The heaviest weeks of our year, driven by stinging
insects, which around a restaurant means nests at entries, patios and waste
areas, where they are a customer-facing safety problem rather than a nuisance.

**Autumn.** Rain saturates the ground and displaces the outdoor rodent
population; the first sustained cold decides where it goes, and a warm kitchen
with a propped door is the best address on the block. This is when rodent calls
spike and when the exclusion done in spring pays for itself.

**Winter.** The quiet season for arrivals and the working season for structure:
thresholds, penetrations, drain work and the exclusion that cannot be done in a
busy summer.

## Starting

We walk the site with somebody who knows the kitchen, ideally outside service.
The walk covers receiving, dry storage, the line, the dish area, drains and the
mop sink, the walk-in surrounds, ceiling voids where they are accessible, the
back door, and the dumpster enclosure — which is usually the most informative
five minutes of the visit.

What comes back is a written program: areas in scope, the exclusion items split
into what we do and what a landlord or contractor has to, visit frequency and
timing around service, the monitoring layout, the documentation package, and the
housekeeping and receiving items that will decide the result.

There is no long-term contract requirement. For a walk before opening or after
close, call or text.
