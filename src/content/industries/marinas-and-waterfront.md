## A marina is not a building

Almost everything that makes marina work different comes from one fact: the
operator is responsible for a site, not a structure, and a large part of what is
on that site belongs to somebody else.

A typical operation here is a yard, a set of shared buildings — restrooms,
laundry, chandlery, offices, a shop — a run of gear lockers or storage sheds,
waste handling somewhere, and then several hundred private spaces in the form of
vessels, each of which is a small building with its own owner, its own contents
and its own housekeeping.

That structure is why a marina pest problem behaves unlike a warehouse or a
restaurant. The pressure is spread across many small spaces rather than
concentrated in one, the operator can only act directly on a minority of it, and
the part they cannot act on is often where the population is actually living.

The job, then, is not just treatment. It is working the parts you control hard
enough that they stop supporting a population, and being clear and documented
about the parts you do not.

## Salt, and why exclusion fails differently here

This is the technical thing most often got wrong on the waterfront, and it is
worth putting near the top because it costs money twice.

Standard exclusion material is galvanized. Galvanized hardware cloth, galvanized
fasteners, plain steel screws into a plate. Inland, at a house in Lynden or
Ferndale, that is entirely appropriate and will last many years. In marine air
it is not. Salt accelerates the corrosion of the zinc coating and then the steel
under it, and a patch that would have lasted a decade three miles inland can be
soft enough to push a finger through in a few seasons at a dock.

There is a second failure that is sneakier. Fasten a galvanized mesh to an
aluminum structure — and there is a great deal of aluminum at a marina — and you
have two dissimilar metals in a salt environment, which is a battery. The
galvanic corrosion that follows attacks the joint specifically, so the mesh is
fine and the fixing is gone, which is the failure nobody inspects for because
the patch still looks present.

So waterfront exclusion is specified differently:

- **Stainless mesh** at the openings that matter, rather than galvanized
- **Stainless or appropriately matched fasteners**, chosen against the substrate
  rather than out of the same box used everywhere else
- **Heavier gauge** where a lighter one would be adequate inland
- **Isolation** at dissimilar-metal joints where it is practical
- **Sealants rated for marine exposure** rather than general-purpose

It costs more. We would rather explain the difference at the quote than install
the cheap version, watch it fail in three winters, and be back doing the same
opening again — which is the pattern we most often inherit at these sites.

## Where the rodents actually live

**Gear lockers and storage sheds.** The center of it on most sites. Lockers hold
line, canvas, cushions, life jackets, sails, foul weather gear and — the part
that matters — food. Snacks left in a locker over winter, dry goods in a plastic
tub, birdseed, dog food. Soft material for nesting and food in the same
unheated, undisturbed box, times two hundred lockers.

**Vessels in layup.** A boat pulled for the winter and covered is a warm, dry,
still, dark structure full of upholstery and often with a galley that was not
emptied properly. It is close to ideal, and it is the space the operator has the
least control over.

**Waste handling.** Dumpsters and the ground around them, particularly at sites
with a restaurant, a fish-cleaning station or heavy seasonal visitor traffic.

**Shared buildings.** Restrooms, laundry, the shop. Warm, plumbed and open to
the public, which means both access and food arriving continuously.

**Under decking and boardwalk, and in the rip-rap.** Structural voids at ground
level, adjacent to a food supply and largely unobservable. On several sites here
this is the reservoir that everything else is drawing from.

**Stored equipment and cradles.** Anything that has stood in one place for a
season, particularly if it is covered.

Norway rats are the species that most often makes a marina problem visible —
they burrow in workable ground near reliable food, and the ground around waste
handling and along a rip-rap edge is exactly that. Mice are the broader
background pressure, especially where the site backs onto vegetation or open
ground rather than water on all sides.

## Gulls and other birds

Bird work at a waterfront is the area where expectations and reality are
furthest apart, so it is worth being direct.

What people want is for the gulls to go. What is available is exclusion and
deterrence: netting over the openings and voids they are nesting in, closing or
modifying the ledges and perches that make a structure attractive, and removing
what is drawing them — which at a marina is very often a fish-cleaning station,
a waste area or people feeding them.

Native birds carry legal protections that narrow the options considerably, and
the protections attach to nests and eggs as well as to the birds. That means
timing matters: work done before a nesting season is straightforward, and the
same work once a nest is active may not be available to you at all. We will tell
you where a species or a situation is protected and we will stay inside that,
which occasionally means saying that the window for this year has closed.

The other half of bird work at a marina is the mess rather than the birds.
Droppings on a walkway are a slip hazard and a cleaning cost, droppings on a
vessel are a customer complaint, and both are what actually drives the
complaints an operator receives. Netting a nesting void and modifying a perch
line addresses that directly and permanently in a way that no repellent does.

## Wasps, spiders and the seasonal nuisance

**Wasps and hornets** are a genuine safety and access problem at a marina rather
than a nuisance one. Nests turn up in equipment sheds, in the eaves over a
working door, inside covered vessels, in electrical enclosures, and in stored
gear that has not moved for a month. August and September are the heaviest
weeks, which is also peak season on the water, so the exposure is highest exactly
when the site is busiest. A nest by a fuel dock or a gate is treated as urgent.

**Spiders** are heavier on the waterfront than almost anywhere else we work, for
the ordinary reason that there is more for them to eat — insects come off the
water in numbers and the structures give endless anchor points. Webbing on
railings, under docks and across covered vessels is largely a cosmetic and
customer-perception problem, and it is managed rather than solved. It is also,
for most marinas, the single most visible pest issue members comment on.

**Cluster flies and overwintering insects** gather on sunlit walls in autumn and
work into the shared buildings through the same gaps everything else uses.

## Fish cleaning, food service and the chandlery

Three parts of a marina behave like different businesses and are worth pulling
out, because each one imports a pest problem the rest of the site does not have.

**Fish-cleaning stations** are the highest-value fix on many sites and the one
most often left alone. Waste, water and a structure, in the open, with gulls and
rats both attending. What decides it is almost never treatment — it is how
quickly waste leaves, whether the station drains and dries between uses, and
whether the surrounding ground and structure can be washed down rather than
merely hosed. A station that stays wet with organic residue is a breeding site
for flies as well as a draw for everything larger.

**Restaurants, cafes and bars on site** bring the whole of food-service pest
control with them: a grease interceptor, a dry store, a dish area, waste
handling on a different schedule from the rest of the yard, and a health
inspection that is nothing to do with the marina's own compliance. These are
usually a separate scope inside the same program, and treating them as part of
the general site work is how both ends get underserved.

**Chandleries and stores** carry packaged goods, and packaged goods bring stored
product insects — beetles and moths that arrive inside the product rather than
through a door. The response is incoming stock inspection and rotation, not
perimeter treatment, and it belongs to whoever manages the stock rather than to
the pest program.

## What we need from the operator

A program at a marina depends more on site management than almost any other
account we run, so these are set out at the quote rather than raised later:

- Waste emptied on a schedule that holds through the busy season, not one that
  works in February
- The ground around waste handling and the fish station kept washable and washed
- Gear lockers subject to a stated rule about food, and a way of enforcing it
- Access to shared buildings, lockers and the yard on visit days, including
  anything normally locked
- A named person who receives the report and can act on what is in it
- Layup guidance going out to slip holders before haul-out rather than after
  complaints in spring

Most operators are already doing several of these. They are listed because a
program where they are absent will underperform no matter how good the technical
work is, and the time to say that is before the first invoice.

## The split-responsibility problem

Almost every dispute we are asked into at a marina lives in the gap between what
the operator controls and what a slip holder does. It is worth designing the
program around that gap rather than discovering it later.

**What the operator can act on:** the shared buildings, the gear storage
structures, the yard and its margins, waste handling, the exclusion condition of
everything the marina owns, and the rules that apply to tenants.

**What belongs to the vessel owner:** the inside of the boat, its stores, its
cover, and the housekeeping that decides whether it becomes harborage over the
winter.

Our part is to be explicit about which is which, in the record, every visit. A
finding inside a locker or a vessel is documented and communicated as a tenant
item; a finding in the shed structure, the yard or the waste area is an operator
item. That distinction is what allows a marina to act on tenant housekeeping
with something more than a general request — a dated, specific record is a
different conversation from a notice on a board.

Where an operator wants it, we will also supply the plain-language material that
goes to slip holders about layup housekeeping: emptying galleys, not storing
food in lockers, and how a cover that traps moisture makes the problem worse.
That is usually worth more than anything we apply.

## The year at a marina

**Haul-out and layup, autumn.** The most important weeks of the year for this
work and the ones most often missed. Vessels come ashore, lockers fill, and the
population that has been living outdoors through summer is being displaced by
the first sustained rain. What happens in these weeks decides the winter.

**Winter.** The quiet season on the water and the working season for us. The
yard is full and static, the sheds can be opened without interrupting anybody,
and exclusion work on the shared buildings is genuinely available. It is also
when a layup problem is most findable, before it has had months undisturbed.

**Spring commissioning.** Covers come off and the results of the winter become
visible — chewed upholstery, wiring damage, nesting in lockers. This is when
complaints arrive, and it is far too late to prevent what caused them.

**Summer.** Peak occupancy, peak visitor traffic, peak waste, and the heaviest
wasp weeks in August and September. The program is maintenance and response
rather than structural work, because there is nowhere to work and nobody wants
scaffolding at a gate in July.

The pattern is the same one that runs everywhere in this county: the useful
window is before the pressure, not during it. At a marina that window is the
autumn haul-out and the winter behind it.

## The record

Marinas are audited and inspected by more parties than most operators expect —
insurers, port or district authorities where one is involved, health authorities
where food is served, and increasingly the environmental conditions attached to
operating on the water.

What a program produces is a dated record of every visit: findings, actions,
device locations, and the recommendations made on your side and on tenants'.
Where devices are used, a map that somebody who has never been on site can
follow. And the split-responsibility notes described above, which on this kind
of account are the part most likely to be needed later.

We are not your auditor and a pest program does not guarantee any inspection
outcome. It removes one common reason for a poor one and produces the evidence
that the site was being managed rather than reacted to.

## Where we work

Waterfront work runs from **Blaine** and the Semiahmoo side, down through
**Birch Bay**, to **Bellingham** — Squalicum, the central waterfront and
Fairhaven — and into the Skagit sites at **Bow** and the water's edge south of
there. Boatyards, dry storage, gear storage, clubs and the commercial buildings
that sit alongside them are all the same category of work.

Blaine has one local peculiarity worth naming. Driftwood accumulates on the
beaches here, and driftwood that has sat wet on a shore for years is carpenter
ant habitat by the time anybody picks it up. A load carried up for a firepit, a
border or a woodshed brings the colony with it and sets it down against a
building. We find carpenter ants started that way more often in Blaine than
anywhere else in the county, and the tell is a nest that is outdoors, recent, and
nowhere near the water damage that normally explains one.

## Starting

We walk the site first, and at a marina that walk takes longer than anywhere
else, because the pressure is distributed rather than concentrated and because
half of the useful information is in the gear storage and the waste area rather
than in the buildings. Bring whoever manages the yard and, if you can, whoever
handles tenant communications.

What comes back is a written program: the structures in scope, what is sealed
and in what material, what is defended rather than sealed, the visit frequency,
the device map, the split between operator and tenant items, and the housekeeping
that will decide the result. Then we run it and adjust it when the site says so.

There is no long-term contract requirement. For a site walk, or a straight answer
