## The building is the unit

Everything that makes multifamily work different comes from one structural fact:
the apartments are not separate spaces. They are compartments inside one
building, connected by wall cavities, floor and ceiling assemblies, plumbing
chases, electrical runs, shared attics and shared crawlspaces.

A tenant experiences their apartment as a sealed box. A rodent, a cockroach or a
bed bug experiences the building as one continuous space with soft partitions.

That is the whole of it, and almost every failure in multifamily pest control
comes from scoping the work to the complaint instead of to the structure.
Treating unit 4 while units 3 and 5 are untouched does not remove a population.
It moves it, briefly, and produces a second complaint from a different door in
about a month — which then looks like a treatment failure and is really a
scoping failure.

So the first conversation on a multifamily account is about the building: how it
is put together, what connects to what, where the shared voids run, and which
units share an assembly with the one that called.

## Bellingham, and the student rental belt

The rental stock here has a shape worth naming, because it changes the work.

A large share of Bellingham's multifamily housing serves students, concentrated
around the university — Sehome, Happy Valley, and the streets running down
toward campus — with more of it spread through the older neighborhoods as
converted single-family houses. That produces a specific set of conditions:

**High turnover on a fixed annual cycle.** Most of the building empties and
refills within the same few weeks. That is both the biggest opportunity in the
year and the biggest risk, because everything that leaves and arrives moves
through the same doors in the same fortnight.

**Housekeeping that varies enormously between units** in the same building, with
no realistic prospect of standardizing it.

**Split responsibility three ways** rather than two — owner, management company
and occupant — which is why documentation matters more here than almost
anywhere.

**Older housing stock**, much of it converted, with the construction problems
that come with a hundred years of alterations: balloon or near-balloon framing
that gives a continuous vertical route from crawlspace to attic, penetrations
drilled oversize and never closed, and additions where two envelopes meet badly.

**Rats rather than mice**, inside the city. This is worth stating because the
assumption runs the other way. In the built-up parts of Bellingham the rodent
work is Norway rats at ground level and roof rats wherever trees, fences or
utility lines reach a roofline. Mice take over toward the greenbelts and out in
the county, not in the middle of town.

## What actually turns up

**Rodents.** The dominant multifamily problem here. Norway rats in crawlspaces
and at foundation level; roof rats using the roofline, the branches and the
utility runs. The route into the building is almost always the same short list:
the crawlspace vent with a corroded screen, the gap where siding meets the
foundation, the utility penetration nobody closed, the door sweep that has worn
through. From there, in a building with continuous wall cavities, they have the
run of the place.

**Cockroaches**, which are a shared-building problem in a way they are not in a
detached house. They move through plumbing chases and shared walls, and a
kitchen or bathroom stack connects units vertically as reliably as a corridor
connects them horizontally. Which species it is changes the work: an indoor
population breeding in warm kitchen and bathroom voids is a building-wide baiting
job that has to include the neighbors, while a larger, slower roach coming up
from a damp crawlspace or a floor drain is a moisture and exclusion problem that
baiting alone will not settle. Identifying which one is on the glue board before
quoting is the difference between a program that ends it and a program that
repeats.

**Bed bugs**, the highest-stakes problem in multifamily and the one most often
under-scoped. See below.

**Ants**, mostly odorous house ants, which is the ant most buildings here
actually have. Worth knowing because it is the species where a repellent spray
causes the colony to bud — fragmenting and relocating rather than dying — so a
well-meaning tenant with a hardware-store bottle converts one nest into several
inside a shared wall.

**Wasps** in soffits, balconies and shared entries, which in a multifamily
setting is an access and liability problem rather than a nuisance one.

**Overwintering insects** on sunlit walls in autumn, which generate a lot of
complaints and very little actual risk.

## Bed bugs, and why scope discipline matters

Bed bug work in a shared building is the clearest case of the general rule, and
the most expensive one to get wrong.

A single unit treated in isolation, in a building with continuous voids and
adjoining walls, is close to guaranteed to recur — either because the population
had already spread before anyone reported it, or because it moves during the
treatment. The correct scope is nearly always the reporting unit plus the
adjoining units on all six sides, inspected before quoting rather than after.

The other half is process rather than treatment. Bed bugs travel with belongings
and with people, so turnover is the highest-risk moment in the year for a
student building — furniture arriving from unknown sources, items left behind
and reused, and a compressed window in which everything moves at once.

Bed bug verification carries a fee, which is credited against treatment if you
proceed. It is the one service on this site that is not a free visit, and the
reason is that verification in a shared building is genuine diagnostic work
across multiple units rather than a look at one mattress.

For tenants and managers, the practical detail worth circulating before turnover
is simple: do not take upholstered furniture off a curb, and do not move
belongings into a unit that has not been checked.

## Turnover is the window

If there is one operational point on this page worth acting on, it is this one.

A multifamily building can be treated while occupied indefinitely and never
actually be fixed, because the work that fixes it needs empty rooms. Exclusion
inside a unit, sealing at penetrations, work on the floor and wall assemblies,
proper attention to a kitchen or bathroom chase — none of that happens
comfortably around a tenant's belongings and none of it happens at all if access
is negotiated one door at a time.

Turnover solves that, and in Bellingham the turnover window is short and every
trade in the county wants it. Which means it has to be booked early and planned
against, rather than treated as a period when things happen to be quiet.

What belongs in a turnover window:

- Unit-level exclusion at penetrations, thresholds and any assembly opened
  during works
- Bed bug inspection of vacated units before anything is moved in
- Crawlspace and attic work where those are shared
- Common area and waste area work that is disruptive when occupied
- Anything requiring the building to be quiet

The rest of the year is program and response. The year's actual progress is made
in those few weeks.

## Common areas, waste, and the parts nobody feels responsible for

In a house, everything belongs to somebody who lives there. In a multifamily
building there is a category of space that belongs to everyone, which in
practice means it belongs to nobody, and a disproportionate share of the
population lives in it.

**Waste handling** is the first and largest of them. A dumpster enclosure for
thirty units generates far more than thirty times the pressure of a single
household bin, because volume, moisture and continuous availability are all
present at once. Where the enclosure is, whether the gates close, whether the
ground around it can be washed, and how often it is emptied through summer will
do more for the building than anything applied to it. On several buildings we
work, the entire rodent picture is a waste enclosure thirty feet from a
foundation.

**Shared crawlspaces and attics** are the routes that connect everything. On an
older converted building these are frequently continuous across units that have
otherwise been carefully separated, so a population under one apartment has
access to the floor of all of them. They are also where nobody goes, which is
why a problem there is usually months old by the time it is reported upstairs.

**Laundry rooms, mail rooms and storage lockers.** Warm, plumbed, full of soft
material, and used by everybody at odd hours. Storage lockers in particular
accumulate exactly what a rodent wants — cardboard, textiles, and food somebody
forgot they had put there.

**Entries, stairwells and balconies**, which are where wasp nests turn up and
where a nest becomes a liability question rather than a nuisance one.

**Landscaping held against the building.** Shrubs against siding, bark mulch
piled to the foundation line, and irrigation keeping ground damp are all
standard in managed landscaping and all work against the building. This is the
easiest thing on the list to change and the one most often defended on
appearance grounds.

## Talking to residents, and why it decides the outcome

A multifamily program succeeds or fails on communication more than on
technique, and it is the part most often left to whoever happens to be on site.

Three things reliably matter:

**Notice that arrives early enough to be acted on.** Preparation for a treatment
— clearing under a sink, emptying a closet, being home — takes a resident time
they have to be given. Notice pinned up the day before produces a building where
half the units are not ready, which turns a scoped job into a partial one and a
partial job into a recurrence.

**A route for reporting that does not embarrass anybody.** Under-reporting is
the norm in shared housing, particularly for cockroaches and bed bugs, because
residents reasonably fear being blamed or charged. A building where problems are
reported late is a building where problems are large. Whatever the reporting
route is, it works better when it is framed as maintenance rather than as fault.

**Plain guidance at turnover.** The single highest-value message in a student
building is a short one: do not take upholstered furniture off a curb, and do
not move belongings into a unit that has not been checked. We will supply that
material for managers to circulate, because it prevents more bed bug work than
any treatment we perform.

We are not the ones who should be writing your resident communications, but we
will tell you which findings need to reach residents and when, and we will put
them in the record so a manager is not reconstructing them from memory.

## HOAs and community associations

Community associations are a related account with a different decision
structure, and the difference is worth planning around.

The pest work is largely the same — shared structures, common landscaping, waste
handling, and a set of individually owned homes the association does not control
the inside of. What changes is that decisions are made by a board on a meeting
cycle, budgets are set annually, and the association's authority over an
individual owner is narrower than a landlord's over a tenant.

Practically that means two things. Structural recommendations need to arrive in
a form a board can act on — scoped, costed, and separated into what is common
property and what belongs to an owner — rather than as a note to a manager. And
timing has to respect the budget cycle, because work proposed in the wrong month
waits a year regardless of merit.

The most common conversation we have with associations is about landscaping and
about waste enclosure siting, both of which are common property, both of which
drive the population, and both of which are far cheaper to change than to treat
around indefinitely.

## The record, and the three-way split

Multifamily is the account type where documentation earns its place fastest,
because there are three parties and each of them has a different question.

**The owner** wants to know whether the building's condition is being addressed
or merely managed, and what the capital items are.

**The manager** wants to know what happened, what was recommended, and what they
need to chase.

**The tenant** wants to know that their complaint was acted on.

Every visit produces a dated record: what was found, in which unit or common
area, what was done, and — the part that resolves most disputes — whether the
cause was structural or housekeeping. That distinction is not about assigning
blame. It is about routing the work, because a structural finding goes to the
owner's maintenance list and a housekeeping finding goes to the tenant, and a
program that does not separate them sends everything to whoever answered the
phone.

We are not the people to advise you on your lease or on landlord-tenant law. We
are the people who can make sure the argument is not about what happened.

Bellingham's rental registration and inspection program is worth planning
against here. It puts a periodic inspection with a checklist on the building,
and pest access points are on that checklist — foundation openings, screens,
gaps at penetrations. The practical effect is that exclusion stops being
discretionary maintenance and becomes a compliance item with a date. A landlord
sealing a crawlspace ahead of it is buying two things at once.

## The year in a rental building

**Late winter to spring.** Structural work while nothing else competes for it.
Rodent activity is high and visible, which makes it the best diagnostic period
of the year.

**Spring.** Ants, and the annual arrival of tenant-applied sprays that make the
odorous house ant problem worse. Worth getting ahead of with a note to
residents.

**Summer.** Wasps in soffits, balconies and entries, building to the heaviest
weeks in August and September. Lower rodent pressure indoors.

**Turnover.** The working window described above.

**Autumn.** Rain displaces the outdoor rodent population and the first cold snap
drives it inside; overwintering insects arrive on sunlit walls at the same time.
This is when the complaints spike, and it is far too late to prevent what caused
them.

## Starting

We walk the building, not a unit. That walk covers the crawlspace and attic
where they are shared, the common areas, waste handling, the exterior envelope,
and enough units to understand the construction rather than to inventory the
complaints.

What comes back is a written program: buildings and areas in scope, the
exclusion items separated into what is being done now and what is a capital
item, visit frequency, common area treatment, the turnover plan, and how
findings are routed between owner, manager and tenant. Then we run it and revise
it as the building changes.

There is no long-term contract requirement. For a walk, or a straight answer
about whether a scope somebody else has quoted is the right one, call or text.
