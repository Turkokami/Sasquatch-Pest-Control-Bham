## The feed room decides everything

Every dairy pest program in this county is a feed program wearing a different
name. That is not a simplification for the sake of a first paragraph — it is
the thing that decides whether the work holds.

A rodent population is limited by food, water and harborage. On a farm, water is
free and harborage is everywhere, so food is the only one of the three anybody
can move. And a feed room with grain, pellets or supplement in open storage,
with the inevitable spillage under the auger and around the base of the bins, is
an unlimited food supply. It will support a population indefinitely, and it will
rebuild that population faster than any treatment schedule can knock it down.

We have walked farms where a great deal of money had been spent on a monthly
service, the technician was diligent, the devices were placed correctly, and the
population had not moved in two years. The reason was forty feet away, under a
feed bin, in a pile of spilled ration that nobody had swept because sweeping it
was not anybody's job.

So the first conversation on a dairy is rarely about products. It is about where
the feed is, what it sits in, what happens to what falls on the floor, and which
of those things can change.

## What is actually different about a working farm

Residential pest control is a building problem. Agricultural pest control is a
system problem, and the building is one part of it.

**The buildings are not sealable and pretending otherwise is dishonest.** A pole
barn on a gravel pad with roll-up doors that stand open through the working day
cannot be closed against rodents. Neither can a free-stall barn. Quoting a farm
as though the envelope can be made tight is how a customer ends up paying for a
result nobody could have delivered, and it is the single most common thing we
are called in to replace.

**The pressure comes from outside and it is continuous.** Field margins, ditch
banks, hedgerows, manure storage, silage faces and neighboring ground all
produce rodents. A farm is not defending against an occasional intrusion; it is
sitting inside the population's range permanently.

**There are animals and product in the way.** Every decision about where
something is placed and what is used has to account for livestock, feed, milk
and the people handling all three. That constrains the toolbox in ways a
restaurant does not.

**The clock is not yours.** Milking, feeding and scraping happen on a schedule
that does not move, and a program that ignores it will be canceled inside six
months no matter how good the technical work is.

**Somebody is going to audit it.** Processor requirements, herd health
protocols, and the documentation that goes with them mean the record of what
was done is part of the deliverable rather than an administrative afterthought.

## Where the rodents actually are

The mouse pressure in this part of the county is heavier than most farm owners
expect, and heavier than it is inside the city. Bellingham is largely a rat
picture. Out through Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas it is mice first, in
volume, because the surrounding ground is field and hedgerow and there is no
edge to push them back from. Deer mice come in off that ground alongside house
mice, and they arrive in numbers rather than singly.

That changes the work. A rat problem is usually a small number of animals using
a small number of routes, and closing the routes ends it. A mouse problem on a
farm is a continuous supply, and the goal shifts from elimination to a defended
core: the feed room, the shop, the milk house, the electrical, the offices.

**The feed room and feed storage.** The center of it, always. Bulk bins,
supplement, mineral, bagged product, and every square foot of floor under and
around them.

**The shop and equipment storage.** Wiring is the expensive one. A rodent-chewed
harness on a tractor or a mixer is a repair bill and a day lost in the middle of
whatever season it happens in, and it happens because the shop is warm, dry,
quiet at night and full of soft material.

**Electrical panels and control rooms.** Small, warm, undisturbed and full of
places to nest. Also the place where a failure is worst.

**The milk house and anywhere product is handled.** Different standard entirely,
and the one that gets the most attention from anyone inspecting.

**Calf housing and hutches.** Milk replacer, warmth, bedding, and young stock
that cannot move away from a problem.

**Around chicken coops, on any property that has them.** Backyard poultry is
common on smallholdings out here and it is a reliable rodent magnet — not the
birds, the feed. The spillage under a feeder and the coop floor together make a
heated, covered, food-filled structure sitting in open ground. It is worth
treating as its own small feed room.

## What we do about it

**Close what closes.** The milk house, the shop, the feed room where its
construction allows, the offices, the control rooms. Hardware cloth at the right
gauge, sheet metal where something will be chewed at directly, and proper
patching at masonry gaps — not foam, which a rodent opens in an evening and
which has a legitimate role only as a backer behind something they cannot chew.

**Defend what does not close.** For the pole barns and the open structures, the
work is a monitored perimeter, controlled placements in structural voids where
livestock and product cannot reach them, and attention to the margins that are
feeding the population — the vegetation held against a wall, the pallet stack
against the outside of the shop, the equipment parked in the same spot for two
years.

**Deal with the population that is already there.** Trapping and, where it is
appropriate to the setting and the label, targeted rodenticide placement in
structural voids and locked stations. This is the part that is visible, and it
is the part that does the least good on its own.

**Write down what we found and what we did.** Every visit. See the record
section below, because on a farm this is not paperwork, it is the thing that
gets produced when somebody asks.

## Rats, where they do turn up

The section above is about mice because mice are the volume problem out here,
but Norway rats are the ones that do the structural damage when a farm has
them, and they concentrate differently.

Rats want a burrow in workable ground near a reliable food and water source, so
on a livestock operation they are found along manure storage, at lagoon margins,
under concrete slabs and feed pads where a void has opened, in the banks of
ditches running the property, and beneath any structure with a gap between the
slab and the ground. They are not spread across the farm the way mice are — they
are in a handful of places, in numbers, and those places are usually visible
once somebody knows to look at the ground rather than at the buildings.

That difference is useful, because a rat problem is more tractable than a mouse
problem. A small number of burrow systems supporting a population can be dealt
with directly, and closing the slab voids and the structural gaps they are using
does more than any amount of work above ground. The tell is burrow entrances
with worn, greasy runs between them, and fresh spoil at the mouth of a hole that
somebody filled in last month.

## What the program asks of you

Every farm program has items on the customer's side, and we would rather set
them out at the quote than raise them as excuses at month four. Typically:

- Feed spillage cleared on a schedule somebody owns, rather than when it is
  noticed
- Bagged product off the floor and, where possible, in hard containers
- A scrape and bedding routine that does not leave wet organic material sitting
- Vegetation and stored material pulled back from the walls of the buildings we
  are defending
- Access to the buildings in scope on the visit day, including the shop and any
  locked control rooms
- Somebody named who receives the report and can act on the recommendations

None of these are unusual and most farms are already doing several. They are
listed because a program where they do not happen will underperform, and the
honest time to say so is before the first invoice rather than after the third.

## Flies, which are won at the breeding site

Fly control on livestock operations is the clearest example of a problem that
cannot be treated at the point where it is noticed.

Adult flies are the symptom. They are also the only part anybody sees, which is
why the reflex is to spray them and why spraying them never holds. A house fly
goes from egg to adult in about a week in warm weather, and the population you
knocked down on Tuesday is replaced from the breeding sites by the following
Tuesday. Treating adults on a farm is a maintenance task at best and a way of
spending money at worst.

The breeding sites are moist organic material, and on a dairy that means:

- Manure that sits rather than moving — under equipment, in corners the scraper
  misses, behind gates, around the base of posts
- Wet bedding, particularly where it has been pushed aside and forgotten
- Silage faces and spoiled feed at the edge of a bunker
- Spilled feed that has taken up moisture
- The margins of lagoons and any standing water that stays for more than a few
  days
- Calf hutches and the ground under them

The work is finding those, and then changing the ones that can be changed —
scrape frequency, drainage, where bedding is stacked, how quickly spoiled feed
is dealt with. Where a site cannot be removed, larvicide applied to it is worth
more than any amount of adult knockdown, because it works on the supply rather
than on the output.

We will also be direct about what is not achievable. A working dairy in July
will have flies. The goal is a manageable population that does not affect the
animals or the people, not a fly-free barn, and anybody promising the second one
is selling you a visit schedule.

## Other things that turn up

**Cluster flies**, which are not a livestock issue at all but arrive every
autumn on south and west walls and work into the buildings through the same gaps
everything else uses. They are an overwintering nuisance rather than a health
matter, and the treatment is exterior and seasonal.

**Stored product insects** in grain and bagged feed — beetles and moths that
arrive inside the product rather than through a door. Worth knowing because the
response is inspection of incoming stock and stock rotation, not perimeter
work.

**Wasps and hornets**, which on a farm are mostly an access and safety problem:
nests in equipment sheds, in the eaves over a working door, inside a piece of
machinery that has stood still for a month. August and September are the weeks
for it.

**Birds** in open buildings, which is a real problem on some sites and one where
what is legally and practically available is narrower than people assume. We
handle it as exclusion — netting, and closing the perches and ledges that make a
building attractive — rather than as control.

## The record, which is part of the job here

On residential work the documentation is a courtesy. On an agricultural account
it is half of what is being bought.

What a program produces is a dated record of every visit: what was found, what
was done about it, where the devices are and what was in them, and what was
recommended on your side. That last one matters more than it sounds. When a
recommendation about feed storage or scrape frequency exists in writing and
dated, it is evidence of a managed system. When it was mentioned to somebody in
a yard in March, it is nothing.

A device map is part of it wherever devices are in use, drawn so that somebody
who has never been on the farm can follow it. That is the single most common
thing an auditor asks for and the single most common thing a cheap program
cannot produce.

We are not your auditor and we will not tell you a pest program guarantees an
audit result. It does not. What it does is remove one of the standard reasons
for failing one, and produce the history that shows the system was being
managed rather than reacted to.

## The year on a Whatcom County farm

**Late winter into spring.** The quiet-looking part of the year and one of the
most useful. This is when the exclusion work on the sealable buildings actually
gets done, because the weather allows it and nothing else is competing for the
time. It is also when a rodent population that moved in over the autumn is most
visible, because the evidence has been accumulating in a shop nobody has been
in.

**Spring.** Fly breeding starts as temperatures come up and moisture is
everywhere. Getting ahead of it here is worth more than anything done in July.
Rodent activity is still high from the winter.

**Summer.** Fly pressure at its peak, and the season where a program either
holds or is exposed. Wasp and hornet work builds through it. Rodents are largely
outdoors and living well, which is exactly why this is the window to close
buildings.

**August and September.** Our heaviest weeks across the whole business, driven
by stinging insects, and the last practical window for exterior sealing before
the weather closes it.

**Autumn.** The rain saturates the ground and displaces the outdoor rodent
population, and then the first genuinely cold snap decides where it ends up. On
a farm the answer is the feed room, the shop and the calf housing. Cluster flies
arrive on the walls at the same time.

## Starting

We walk the farm first, and on anything larger than a hobby operation that walk
takes a while, because most of what matters is not where the activity is — it is
where the feed is and where the access is. Bring whoever knows the feeding and
scraping routine, because half the answers are in that.

What comes back is a written program: the buildings in scope, what is sealed and
what is defended, the visit frequency, what happens on each visit, the device
map, and the items on your side that will decide the outcome. Then we run it and
adjust it when the farm tells us to.

There is no long-term contract requirement. Programs on working farms are
recurring because the pressure is recurring, not because the agreement traps
anybody.
