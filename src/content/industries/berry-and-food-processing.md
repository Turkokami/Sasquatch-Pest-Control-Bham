## What this county actually processes

Whatcom and Skagit are food-producing counties, and the processing that follows
the fields is a real part of the commercial work here. Berry receiving, freezing
and packing. Dairy processing. Potato and vegetable handling on the Skagit side.
Seafood at the water. Bakeries, commissaries, small manufacturers and the cold
storage that serves all of them.

These sites have more in common with each other than with any other commercial
building, and what they share is the thing that shapes the program: they are
judged on their record as much as on their condition.

A restaurant fails an inspection because of what an inspector sees on the day. A
processing plant fails an audit because of what it cannot show over the past
twelve months. That difference decides how a pest program on a food site has to
be built.

## Harvest is a different building

The single most local feature of processing work here is that the plant is not
one building through the year. It is two.

For most of the year it is a controlled envelope. Doors shut, traffic
predictable, waste on a steady schedule, staff settled, and a program that can
be tuned and left largely alone.

Then harvest arrives, and for six to ten weeks:

- **Doors that are normally closed stand open**, because throughput matters more
  than the envelope and everybody knows it
- **Raw product arrives on trailers** carrying field insects, and sometimes
  rodents, straight to the intake
- **Waste volume multiplies**, and culls and rejected fruit are the most
  attractive material the site will produce all year
- **Hours extend** and the building runs when it is normally shut
- **Traffic increases** — more vehicles, more pallets, more people, more doors
  opened
- **Temporary storage appears** in places that are not normally storage

A program scoped for February will be under-scoped in July, and the failure is
predictable enough that it should be planned rather than discovered. On sites
with a defined season we build harvest as its own operating state: increased
visit frequency, additional monitoring at intake and waste, a tighter loop on
findings, and agreed exterior work done *before* the season rather than during
it, because during it there is no access and no appetite.

The corollary is the useful one. **The window for structural work on a
processing site is the off-season**, and specifically late winter into spring.
That is when doors can be closed, when a dock leveler can be worked on, when
exclusion actually gets installed. Everything deferred from that window becomes
a monitoring problem in July.

## Where the pressure comes from

**Intake and receiving.** The one door the building cannot control, because
product has to come through it. Field insects arrive with the crop, and on
trailers and pallets that have been standing in a yard.

**Dock doors and levelers.** The classic structural weakness on any food
building. Seals that have compressed, corners that no longer meet, and the void
under a leveler pit, which is a warm covered space at ground level connected to
the inside of the building.

**Waste and culls.** The highest-value fix on many sites. Volume, moisture and
sugar in the same place, outdoors, in summer. Fly breeding starts here and so
does most rodent pressure.

**Drains and wet floors.** Drain flies and small flies breed in the organic film
inside drain lines and under equipment plinths. This is a sanitation problem
wearing a pest problem's clothes, and treating the adults does nothing at all.

**Dry storage and packaging.** Stored product insects, arriving inside the
product. See below.

**Voids and equipment plinths.** Under and behind fixed equipment, in insulated
panel joints, in the gap between a plinth and the floor. Warm, undisturbed,
often with organic residue, and almost never opened.

**The yard margins.** Vegetation against a wall, pallet stacks, standing
equipment, drainage that keeps ground damp. Most of what is inside the building
was living outside it first.

## Stored product insects, which arrive in the box

Worth separating out, because the instinct is to treat the building and the
building is rarely the source.

Beetles and moths in dry ingredients, packaging, or finished goods generally
come in with a delivery. A single infested pallet placed at the back of a store
will seed a population that then appears throughout the site, and treating the
perimeter of the building has no effect on it whatever.

The work is:

- **Incoming inspection** of dry goods and packaging on receipt, with a stated
  rejection standard
- **Stock rotation** actually enforced, because the population is always in the
  oldest thing on the racking
- **Pheromone monitoring** placed to locate a source rather than to prove there
  is one
- **Finding the forgotten pallet** — the split bag behind the racking, the
  damaged case nobody wrote off, the pallet moved to make room in 2023
- **Cleaning the spillage** in racking, under pallets and in the corners where
  product accumulates

Once the source is out, the population goes. Until it is out, nothing else
matters.

## Flies, on a food site

Fly control in processing is won at the breeding site, and on a food site the
breeding sites are usually inside the sanitation program rather than outside the
building.

**Small flies** — drain, phorid and fruit flies — breed in organic film. Drain
lines, the underside of equipment, the gap at a plinth, a cracked floor holding
residue, a mop store, a wet corner behind a tank. The tell is that they are
present in a specific area and stay there. Spraying them accomplishes nothing;
finding and removing the film accomplishes everything.

**House flies and blow flies** are an exterior problem coming in: waste, culls,
standing water in the yard, and open doors. The work is waste handling, drainage
and door discipline, supported by placement rather than led by it.

We will map where flies are actually breeding and say so plainly, including when
the answer is a piece of equipment that needs lifting or a drain that needs
proper cleaning rather than anything we can apply. That is often not the answer
a site wants, and it is the only one that works.

## Cold storage and freezing

Freezing is a large part of what happens to this county's fruit, and cold
storage behaves unlike the rest of a plant.

The cold itself does a lot of the work — insect activity effectively stops, and
a freezer is not a place a population establishes. What it does instead is
preserve a problem that arrived with the product and hold it until the pallet
comes back out. Stored product insects that went in alive at ambient
temperatures do not breed at freezing, but nothing kills them either on the
timescales most product is held, so the response is at intake and at
despatch rather than inside the room.

Where cold storage does generate its own pressure is at the edges and the
services. Rodents are drawn to the warm side of an insulated envelope, so the
places to look are the panel joints, the door seals and the surrounds, the
condenser and plant spaces, and the void above a suspended ceiling in the
adjoining corridor — all warm, all undisturbed, and all immediately outside a
room nobody is going to find them in. Condensate is the other one: a drain or a
drip tray that stays wet is a small-fly breeding site in a building people
assume is too cold to have one.

The practical consequence is that a cold store is monitored around rather than
inside, and the intake standard for what goes in matters more than anything done
after the door closes.

## Rodents on a processing site

Rodents are the finding that closes a line, so they are worth their own account
even though the general principles are the same everywhere.

**Where they get in** is almost never the wall. It is the dock — a leveler pit
with a gap into the building, a door seal compressed flat over years of trailer
impacts, corners where a seal no longer meets the frame. It is also utility
penetrations at the back of the building, the roof line at a mechanical
platform, and any point where the building was extended and two envelopes meet.

**Where they live** is the void. Under and behind fixed equipment, in insulated
panel joints, in the space under a plinth, above a suspended ceiling in an
office corridor, and inside the leveler pit itself. These are warm, undisturbed,
frequently have organic residue in them, and are almost never opened. A site can
be visibly clean and be feeding a population in six places nobody has looked at
in years.

**What they do** on a food site is disproportionate to their number. One rodent
in a production area is a contamination event with a documented response
attached, not a nuisance. That asymmetry is why the program is built on
exclusion and early detection rather than on response, and why a monitoring
device that has never caught anything is still doing its job.

**How we work it.** Interior detection first, because the interior is where the
consequence is: monitoring placed to the building's geometry rather than at
even spacing, and to the voids and the traffic routes rather than to the walls.
Exclusion at the dock, which is where the money is best spent on almost every
plant we walk. Exterior management to reduce the pressure arriving. And a
standing rule that any interior finding is escalated the same day rather than
appearing in a monthly summary.

## Birds, and the intake canopy

On a food site the driver for bird work is contamination risk rather than mess.
Roosting over an intake, a dock or a waste area puts droppings where product and
packaging move, which is an audit finding regardless of whether anything is
contaminated.

The available work is exclusion and deterrence: netting the voids and canopies
they are using, closing or modifying ledges and roost points, and dealing with
what makes the structure attractive — which on a processing site is usually
spilled product in the yard, standing water, or waste that stays open.

Native species carry legal protections that narrow the options, and those
protections attach to active nests, so timing changes what is available. Work
scheduled before a nesting season is straightforward; the same work once a nest
is active may not be lawful. We will tell you where that line falls rather than
discovering it mid-job.

## What the site has to do

A processing program depends on sanitation and housekeeping more than on
anything we apply, so these are agreed at the quote:

- Waste and culls on a schedule that holds through the season, with containers
  that close and ground around them that can be washed
- Drains on a documented cleaning program, because small flies are a drain
  problem before they are a pest problem
- Dry storage rotated and racking kept clear of spillage, with a rejection
  standard for incoming goods
- Equipment voids and plinths opened for us periodically rather than never
- Dock seals and door closers maintained as building assets, not deferred
- Yard margins kept clear of vegetation, pallets and standing equipment
- A named contact who receives findings and can escalate an interior one the
  same day

Most plants are already doing most of this. It is written down because a program
where these lapse will produce findings that look like pest control failures and
are not, and because when an auditor asks, the record needs to show the
recommendations were made.

## The record, which is the deliverable

On a processing site the documentation is not administration around the service.
It is a substantial part of what is being bought.

A program produces, every visit: what was found, what was done, what was
recommended and to whom, and the state of every monitoring device. Across a year
that becomes a trend, which is what an auditor is actually reading — not whether
there was ever a finding, but whether findings were acted on and whether the
same one keeps recurring.

Alongside it: a device map drawn so somebody who has never been on the site can
follow it, numbered consistently with the records; the licenses and categories
under which the work was performed; and the labels and safety documentation for
anything used.

The most common failure we are called in to fix on a food site is not a pest
problem. It is a program that could not produce its own paperwork when somebody
asked for it.

We are not your auditor and we will not tell you a pest program guarantees an
audit outcome — that depends on a great deal we do not control. What it does is
remove one of the standard reasons for a poor one, and demonstrate a managed
system rather than a reactive one.

## What the setting constrains

A food-handling environment narrows the toolbox, and that is a feature rather
than an obstacle.

Placements go where product, packaging and food-contact surfaces cannot be
affected. Interior work in production and packaging areas belongs in sanitation
windows and shutdowns rather than during a run. Materials are chosen for the
setting and the label, and mechanical control — trapping, exclusion, monitoring
— carries more of the load than it does in a warehouse, because it introduces
nothing.

Where a job cannot be done safely in a food environment, we will say so and
propose the alternative rather than working around the constraint quietly.

## The year

**Late winter to spring.** The structural window. Dock seals, door sweeps,
exclusion at penetrations, drainage correction in the yard. Everything that
needs the building calm.

**Spring.** Fly breeding begins as temperatures rise and moisture is everywhere.
Getting ahead of it here is worth more than anything done at the peak. Rodent
activity is still elevated from the winter.

**Harvest, summer into early autumn.** The second operating state described
above. Increased frequency, intake and waste monitoring, response rather than
construction.

**August and September.** Our heaviest weeks generally, driven by stinging
insects, which on a food site are an access and safety problem at doors, waste
areas and intake.

**Autumn.** Rain saturates the ground and displaces the outdoor rodent
population; the first sustained cold then decides where it goes. On a processing
site that is the leveler pit, the equipment void and the dry store. This is also
when the year's records get reviewed before an audit cycle.

## Starting

We walk the plant first, with somebody who knows the sanitation program and
somebody who knows the building, because the answers are split between them. The
walk covers intake, docks, waste, dry storage, drains, equipment voids, the yard
margins and the areas that change at harvest.

What comes back is a written program: areas in scope, what is sealed and what is
monitored, device map and numbering, visit frequency, the harvest scope if the
site has a season, the documentation package, and the sanitation and housekeeping
items that will decide the result. Then we run it and revise it when the site
changes rather than when the calendar does.

There is no long-term contract requirement. For a site walk, call or text.
