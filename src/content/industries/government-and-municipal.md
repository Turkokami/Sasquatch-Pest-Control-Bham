## One program, a dozen different buildings

A municipal estate is not a building type. It is a portfolio, and almost every
category on this site is somewhere inside it.

City hall and administrative offices. A library. A community or senior center.
Recreation facilities and a pool. Public works and the maintenance yard. Fleet
and equipment storage. Parks, shelters, restrooms and concession buildings.
Sometimes a museum or a historic structure with its own constraints. Sometimes
a kitchen. Sometimes housing.

Each of those has a different pest picture, a different access pattern and a
different audience, and the program has to hold across all of it while being
purchased as one thing. That is the actual challenge in municipal work, and it
is a scoping problem rather than a technical one.

## What public buildings have in common

**They are open to the public**, which means access is continuous, food comes in
constantly, and doors are used by people who have no reason to care whether they
close.

**They are answerable in public.** A complaint about a pest in a public building
can become a question at a council meeting, an email to an elected official, or
a story. That raises the cost of a slow response far above the cost of the
problem itself.

**They are bought through a formal process.** A quote is a bid, the scope has to
be comparable against other bids, and variations are harder than in the private
sector. This is why scope discipline and a schedule of rates matter more here
than anywhere else.

**They are chronically deferred on maintenance.** Public buildings carry long
lists of things everybody knows need doing. Pest exclusion is almost always on
somebody's list already, and a dated finding from an outside contractor is
frequently what moves it from the list into a budget.

**They are staffed by people who are not maintenance staff.** A librarian, a
recreation supervisor or a clerk is the person who will see the problem first
and needs a route to report it that does not require knowing who to call.

## The buildings that actually generate the work

**Public works, the shop and the maintenance yard.** Almost always the largest
pest problem on an estate, and the least visible. Open bay doors through the
working day, equipment standing for months, material stored outdoors, waste, and
a building nobody is trying to keep sealed. Rodent damage to vehicle and
equipment wiring is the expensive part.

**Parks buildings, shelters and restrooms.** Seasonal use, minimal supervision,
open structures, and food waste in summer. Wasp nests in shelters and restroom
voids are the recurring safety item, and they arrive at exactly the point in the
year when use is heaviest.

**Concession stands and event kitchens.** Food service that operates a few weeks
or weekends a year, closed the rest of the time, which is the worst possible
pattern — a food building that sits idle accumulates a population and then opens
with the public in it.

**Libraries and community centers.** Low pest pressure structurally, high
consequence socially. Also the buildings where a bed bug report is most likely
to become a public conversation, dealt with below.

**Offices and administration.** Break rooms, deliveries, cardboard, and the
ordinary problems of any office.

**Storage and archives.** Undisturbed, paper-rich, rarely entered. Rodent damage
here is discovered late and can be irreversible — paper records, historic
material and stored equipment are not replaceable the way stock is, and a
population can work in a records room for a year before anybody opens the door.

## Bed bugs in a public building

This is the item most likely to become a public story, and it is worth setting
out plainly because the reflex response is usually the wrong one.

Bed bugs do not establish in libraries, offices or community centers the way
they do in housing. Those buildings do not offer the sustained overnight human
contact a population needs. What a public building almost always has is an
introduction — an item brought in, an insect on a bag or a coat — rather than an
infestation of the building.

The right response is inspection to confirm, targeted treatment where warranted,
and proportion. Closing a library for a week over a single specimen does far more
damage than the specimen does, and it teaches everybody that reporting causes
chaos, which produces later reports next time.

Upholstered furniture in public seating areas is the place to focus, and a
periodic inspection of it is cheaper than any response.

## Wasps, parks and the public

Stinging insects are the clearest safety issue on a municipal estate and the
season is unhelpful: our heaviest weeks are August and September, which is peak
park and facility use.

Nests turn up in shelter roofs and rafters, restroom voids, playground
equipment, bleachers, sign structures, utility cabinets, and ground nests on
field margins and trail edges. A ground nest beside a mown path is a different
order of risk from one in a hedge, because somebody will run a mower over it.

Two things make this manageable. A grounds walk before the season rather than a
response during it, and a reporting route that lets a parks employee or a member
of the public flag a nest without deciding whether it is important enough to
mention. Nests near play equipment, entrances and mowing lines are prioritized
out of order.

## Rodents across an estate

Inside Bellingham the picture is rats. Norway rats at ground level working
foundations, drains, service voids and the ground around waste; roof rats where
roofline access, trees or utility runs allow it. Facilities out through the
county see more mice off surrounding open ground, and any building against a
greenbelt, a park margin or a field is on the county pattern regardless of which
side of a line it sits.

Across an estate the routes are consistent: bay and loading doors, utility
penetrations, crawlspace and foundation vents on older buildings, roof plant, and
the service voids that connect wings. The work is exclusion first, monitoring
where the activity actually is rather than at even spacing, and management of the
exterior conditions — waste, vegetation against walls, stored material — that
keep pressure on.

The estate view is what makes this efficient. Findings across a dozen buildings
show a pattern: which structures are generating problems, which are quiet, and
where the capital money should go. A program that produces twelve identical
monthly slips tells a facilities director nothing they can act on and cannot
support a budget request.

## Historic and older public buildings

A good share of the civic stock in this county is old, and older buildings here
carry the problems old buildings here have: continuous wall cavities from
crawlspace to attic, penetrations drilled oversize over a century of
alterations, board sheathing that has shrunk, and vented crawlspaces that stay
damp through a wet season.

Two consequences. Sealing is a more careful job than on a modern building, and
it sometimes runs into heritage constraints on what may be altered or how it may
look. And moisture is the driver behind a large share of the pest activity, so
the honest recommendation is frequently drainage or roof and gutter work rather
than anything a pest company applies.

Where that is the answer, we say so and put it in writing, because in a public
body a dated recommendation from an outside contractor is what turns a deferred
maintenance item into a funded one.

## Fleet, equipment and the wiring problem

The maintenance yard deserves more than a line, because the damage there is the
most expensive thing rodents do on a public estate and it is almost never
counted as a pest cost.

Vehicles and equipment standing idle are ideal harborage. They are enclosed,
warm for hours after use, full of soft insulating material, and undisturbed for
days or months — a plow truck between seasons, a mower over winter, a spare unit
kept for peak. Rodents nest in engine bays, cabs, air boxes and under seats, and
they chew wiring.

A chewed harness is a repair bill, a vehicle out of service, and on seasonal
equipment a failure discovered at the exact moment the equipment is needed. A
plow that will not start in the first snow event is a rodent problem that
presents as a fleet problem, and the invoice is written against fleet
maintenance where nobody connects it to anything.

What works is unglamorous. Equipment stored inside a building that is actually
defended rather than merely covered. Nothing stored with food residue, feed,
seed or fertilizer beside it. Monitoring in the bays rather than only around the
building perimeter. Periodic inspection of long-idle units rather than
discovery at start-up. And keeping the yard margins — pallet stacks, material
piles, vegetation, disused equipment — clear, because that is the reservoir the
building is drawing from.

## Public housing and shelters, where they exist

Some public bodies hold housing, transitional accommodation or shelter capacity,
and where they do it is a different service inside the same estate.

Everything on the multifamily page applies: the building is the unit, shared
voids connect apartments, and treating one door while its neighbors are
untouched relocates a population rather than removing it. What is added is a
resident population that may be transient, may arrive with belongings from
unpredictable places, and may have every reason not to report a problem.

Bed bugs are the central item and the handling is the same as in senior living,
for the same reasons: scope by building, inspect adjoining units before quoting,
and build a reporting culture where an early report is treated as maintenance
rather than as fault. A body that charges residents for treatment, or moves
them as a consequence of reporting, will receive late reports and large
infestations, and will pay considerably more overall.

Intake is the leverage point. Inspecting furniture and belongings at intake, and
having a defined process for treating items rather than refusing them, prevents
more work than any treatment schedule.

## Buying it, and what a bid should contain

Public purchasing rewards clarity, so this is how we structure it.

**A base program scoped by building**, with each building's areas, visit
frequency, monitoring layout and reporting stated separately rather than pooled
into an estate total. That makes the award comparable and lets a body add or
remove a building without renegotiating.

**A schedule of rates** for work outside the base scope — exclusion, a
wildlife or stinging insect callout, a bed bug response, a one-off treatment.
This is the item most often missing from municipal pest bids and the one that
causes the most friction later, because without it every finding becomes a
negotiation.

**Documentation and licensing** attached rather than referenced: WSDA license
numbers and categories, insurance certificates naming the entity where required,
and the reporting format the program will produce.

**An honest statement of capacity.** We are a small local company. Across a
municipal estate in Whatcom or Skagit County we can hold a genuine standard with
the same crew, who will know your buildings. For an award spanning several
counties, or one requiring crew numbers we do not carry, we are not the right
answer and we will say so at the bid rather than win it and underperform. That
has cost us work and it is the correct trade.

## Wildlife, and where the line falls

Public estates generate more wildlife calls than any other category, because
they own the ground between buildings — parks, trail corridors, drainage,
riverbank, and whatever the boundary fence backs onto.

It is worth being direct about what is and is not available, because
expectations here are further from reality than anywhere else on this site.

**We are a pest control company, not a wildlife removal operation.** Where a
situation calls for a licensed wildlife control operator, or for the state, we
will say so and stay out of it rather than take work we should not.

**Native species carry protections**, and those protections frequently attach to
nests and dens as well as to animals, which makes timing decisive. Work
available in February may not be available in May.

**What we do well is exclusion.** Closing the ways into a building — the
crawlspace, the roof void, the shelter rafters, the space under a raised
structure — is the durable answer and it is the part that does not depend on
removing anything. A structure that cannot be entered stops being a problem
regardless of what is living in the park.

**Harborage management is the other half.** Woodpiles, disused equipment,
vegetation against walls, and open space under decking and shelters are all
things an estate controls directly, and all of them decide how much pressure
arrives at the buildings.

For a public body the useful framing is that wildlife on the grounds is usually
not a problem to be solved, and wildlife inside a building always is. The
program addresses the second.

## The year on a public estate

**Late winter to spring.** Structural work, while facilities are quieter and
before the season. This is when exclusion at the shop, the parks buildings and
the older stock actually gets done.

**Spring.** Grounds walk before the season starts. Ants in break rooms.
Preparation of seasonal buildings before they open.

**Summer.** Peak facility and park use, peak waste, doors open, and stinging
insects building to the heaviest weeks in August and September. Response rather
than construction.

**Autumn.** Rain displaces the outdoor rodent population and the first sustained
cold decides where it goes — the shop, the storage buildings, the crawlspaces of
the older stock. The exclusion done in spring proves itself here.

**Winter.** Interior work, archives and storage, and the planning window for the
next year's budget submissions, which is the right moment to have the year's
findings summarized.

## Starting

We walk the estate, or a representative sample, with facilities and with parks
if the grounds are in scope. The walk covers the shop and yard first, because
that is usually where the work is, then the staffed buildings, seasonal and park
structures, storage and archives, waste handling, and the exterior of each.

What comes back is a written program: buildings and areas in scope, what is
sealed and what is deferred to a capital list, monitoring layout and numbering,
visit frequency per building, the escalation route, the per-site and estate-level
reporting, a schedule of rates for out-of-scope work, and the housekeeping items
that will decide the result.
