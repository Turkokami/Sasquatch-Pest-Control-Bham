## The statute shapes the program

Most commercial pest work is shaped by the building. School work is shaped by
the law first and the building second, and that is not a complaint — it produces
better pest control than the alternative.

Washington requires notice before a pesticide application at a school, posting
at the treated location, and records kept afterwards. It also exempts insect and
rodent baits that children cannot access from those notification and posting
requirements.

That exemption is the hinge, and it is worth understanding rather than working
around. A program that leans on spraying generates a notification every time it
runs, which means letters, questions, a posted sign and a facilities manager
fielding calls. A program built on exclusion and contained bait does the same
job — better, in our view — and does not generate any of it.

So the statute pushes schools toward exactly the approach we would argue for
anyway: close the building, monitor it, use contained bait where bait is
warranted, and reserve anything else for the situations that genuinely need it,
scheduled into a break.

The detail of what the statute requires — the notice period, the sign sizes,
what the sign has to say, the emergency exemption, and who is covered — is set
out with the sources linked on our
[pest control in Washington schools](/guides/pest-control-in-washington-schools/)
guide. It is worth reading before signing any pest contract, because a
contractor who is vague about it will make it your problem later.

## What a school building actually is

A school is several different buildings pretending to be one, and the pest
picture changes between them.

**The kitchen and cafeteria** is a food service site with all of food service's
problems, on a compressed schedule, feeding hundreds of people in ninety
minutes. Receiving, dry storage, dish area, drains and waste all behave exactly
as they do in a restaurant.

**Classrooms** are low-risk structurally and high-risk socially. Food is present
constantly — snacks, class parties, projects, things in lockers and desks — and
it is present in small amounts in a hundred places rather than in one store.

**Locker rooms, gyms and their storage** are damp, warm, full of soft material
and rarely emptied.

**Custodial and mechanical spaces** are where a population lives. Warm, quiet,
undisturbed, with access to the whole building through the service runs.

**Portables and modular classrooms** are their own category and usually the
worst of it. They sit on a skirted crawlspace, often with damaged skirting, they
have gaps where the utilities come in, and they are outside the main envelope
entirely. On most campuses we walk in this county, the portables are the rodent
problem.

**Grounds** — the fields, the landscaping, the waste enclosure, the loading
area, and whatever the campus backs onto.

## The rodent picture on a campus

Whatcom County schools sit in two different situations and it changes the work.

Campuses inside Bellingham face the city picture: rats, Norway rats at ground
level and roof rats where trees or roof lines give access. Campuses out through
Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas, and the rural districts face the county
picture, which is mice in volume off surrounding field and hedgerow, with no
edge to push back.

Either way the routes are the same short list and they are almost all
structural: portable skirting, crawlspace vents with corroded screens, utility
penetrations, the gap at a loading door, roof access points where a mechanical
platform meets the deck, and the ceiling voids that connect corridors.

The work is exclusion first because exclusion is exempt from the notification
question entirely — a sealed gap needs no notice, no sign and no letter. It is
the cheapest compliance available.

## Cafeterias, and receiving

The kitchen is the highest-consequence part of a school and it is run harder
than most commercial kitchens, so it deserves specific attention.

**Receiving** is where problems arrive: cockroaches in cardboard and crates,
stored product insects inside dry goods. A district taking deliveries across
several sites has several front doors, and a standard applied at one and not the
others produces a recurring problem at whichever one is weakest.

**Dry storage** is usually the largest single food store in the district and the
easiest to under-rotate. Split bags, damaged cases and pallets shuffled to make
room are what feeds a stored product insect population.

**Drains and the dish area** produce the small fly complaints. As everywhere,
those flies breed in organic film rather than flying in, so treating them is
finding the film.

**Waste and the enclosure**, outdoors, in volume, on a schedule that has to hold
through the warm end of the school year.

## Ants, which are the most common classroom call

By volume the thing a school actually rings about is ants in a classroom, and it
is worth setting out because the instinct is to treat it and the instinct is
wrong.

The ant is almost always the odorous house ant — "sugar ants" locally — which is
the ant most buildings in this county have. It does no structural damage. What
makes it a school problem is that classrooms are full of small food sources in
a hundred places: snacks in desks and cubbies, a birthday party last Thursday,
a recycling bin with juice cartons in it, something in a backpack that has been
there since term started.

The critical point is that a repellent spray makes this species worse. It causes
the colony to **bud** — fragment and relocate rather than die — so one trail
becomes three in different rooms. A well-meaning staff member with a can from a
hardware store on the way to work is the most common cause of an ant problem
escalating in a school, and it is worth saying that out loud to staff once a
year rather than discovering it.

The correct response is baiting and patience, plus finding the food source,
which is nearly always a five-minute conversation with whoever uses the room
rather than a technical exercise. And because contained bait that children
cannot access sits inside the statute's exemption, it is also the version that
requires no letter home.

## Older buildings, moisture and crawlspaces

A good share of the school stock in this county is old enough to have the
problems old buildings have here, and moisture is the one that drives pest
pressure.

Crawlspaces under older wings, boiler rooms and tunnels, and additions where two
envelopes meet badly all produce the conditions this county specializes in:
standing damp, wet framing, and the moisture ants and decay that follow. Where a
crawlspace is shared or continuous under a wing, a population under one
classroom has the floor of all of them.

Roof and gutter condition matters more than it sounds. Water not being carried
away from a building is the origin of a large fraction of what we are later
called about, and on a school estate that maintenance is usually visible on a
deferred list somewhere. When we find it, it goes in the record as a structural
recommendation, because a written, dated finding is what turns a deferred item
into a funded one.

## Fields, grounds and the outdoor edge

The grounds are half a campus and they generate their own work.

**Moles and voles** on playing fields are a groundskeeping and safety issue
rather than a building one — a mole run under a playing surface is a trip
hazard before it is anything else. It is honest to say that this is a management
problem rather than something solved permanently.

**The vegetation line against the buildings.** Shrubs held against walls,
bark mulch piled to the siding, and irrigation keeping ground damp are all
standard in school landscaping and all work against the envelope. Pulling
planting back from the wall is one of the cheapest interventions available on a
campus.

**Waste enclosures and the loading area**, which on a school site are usually at
the back of the kitchen and adjacent to the field margin, which is the worst
combination available.

**What the campus backs onto.** A school against woodland, a greenbelt or open
field has continuous pressure arriving; one in the middle of a residential
block does not. It is the single best predictor of how much work a site needs.

## What integrated pest management actually means here

The phrase appears in every school pest contract in the country and it is worth
saying what it means in practice rather than as a slogan, because a district is
entitled to check whether it is getting it.

It means the building is closed before anything is applied. It means monitoring
tells you where the problem is rather than a schedule telling you when to spray.
It means a treatment is chosen for a specific finding rather than applied
routinely across a site. It means the least disruptive effective option comes
first, which in a school also happens to be the one that avoids a notification.
And it means the conditions that caused the problem — the food source, the
moisture, the gap — are written down and pursued, because otherwise the same
finding recurs forever and the program becomes a subscription.

A district can test any contractor's IPM claim with one question: what have you
recommended we change, and is it in writing? A program that has never asked the
school to do anything is not running IPM, it is running a route.

## Bed bugs, and the reason to be careful

Bed bugs are the school problem most likely to become a public one, and the
handling matters more than the treatment.

They do not establish in classrooms the way they do in housing. Schools do not
have the sustained overnight human contact a population needs. What a school
finding almost always is, is an introduction — brought in on a bag or a coat
from a home where there is a population — rather than an infestation of the
building itself.

That changes everything about the response. It should be quick, confirmed by
inspection rather than by a description, targeted, and quiet. A rumor moves
faster than any treatment and does considerably more damage than the insects
will, and it lands on a family who did not choose it.

We will work with whatever protocol a district has, and where there is not one,
we will say plainly what a proportionate one looks like.

## Licensed childcare, which is not a small school

Childcare and preschool settings sit under the same notification requirements
and they are not simply schools at a smaller scale. Three things differ enough
to change the program.

**The occupants are on the floor.** Crawling and floor-level play means surface
contact is continuous in a way it is not in a classroom of eleven-year-olds.
That justifies a more conservative approach than the statute alone requires, and
it pushes almost everything toward exclusion, sanitation and contained devices
placed out of reach.

**Food is everywhere and constant.** Snacks, bottles, spills, nappy and diaper
waste, and a kitchen that may be domestic rather than commercial in scale but
runs all day. The waste stream in particular is larger and wetter than an
outsider expects.

**The building is often a converted house.** A great many licensed providers
here operate out of residential stock, which brings the whole residential
picture with it: a vented crawlspace, a real attic, wood siding, and the
penetrations of a home rather than the sealed envelope of an institution.

Practically that means a childcare program looks more like careful residential
exclusion with commercial documentation attached than like a scaled-down
district contract. Providers should also assume they are covered by the
notification requirements rather than assuming their size exempts them.

## Wasps, and the grounds

Stinging insects are the school pest with the clearest safety edge, and the
season is unhelpfully timed: our heaviest weeks are August and September, which
is exactly when campuses reopen.

Nests turn up in play equipment, under handrails and deck edges, in portable
skirting, above doorways, in ground-level voids on the field margins, and inside
anything hollow that has stood still through the summer. A ground nest beside a
field is a different order of risk from one in a hedge.

The practical answer is a walk of the grounds before the term starts rather than
a response after somebody is stung, and a route for staff to report a nest that
does not depend on somebody deciding it is important enough.

## Working around the school year

**Summer break.** The working window, and the reason a school program is planned
a year ahead. Buildings are empty, structural work is possible, portables can be
skirted properly, kitchens are down, and anything requiring notification can be
done without competing with the school day.

**Before term starts.** Grounds walk for stinging insects, kitchen readiness,
and confirmation that summer's work held.

**Term time.** Monitoring, contained bait servicing, exterior work, and
response. Low disruption by design.

**Autumn.** The rodent push. Rain displaces the outdoor population and the first
sustained cold decides where it goes, which on a campus is the portables, the
mechanical rooms and the kitchen. This is the season the summer exclusion pays
for itself.

**Winter break and spring break.** Secondary working windows for anything that
needs an empty building.

## The record

Schools keep records because the statute says so, and a district with several
sites needs those records organized by building rather than pooled.

What a program produces is a dated record per site: what was found, where, what
was done, what was recommended, and the state of every monitoring device. Where
an application was made that required notification, the record shows what was
applied, where, when, and that notice was given.

For a district, the version that matters is the roll-up: which buildings are
generating findings, which are quiet, and what the pattern says about where the
capital money should go. A program that produces twelve identical monthly slips
tells a facilities director nothing they can act on.

## What we ask of the district

- A named contact per site, and one at district level who sees the roll-up
- Access to mechanical, custodial and kitchen spaces on visit days
- Receiving standards applied at every site rather than the best one
- Portable skirting treated as a maintained asset rather than as trim
- Waste enclosures that close, on a collection schedule that holds in June
- A route for staff to report sightings that does not depend on escalation
- Summer works planned far enough ahead that pest work is in the schedule
  rather than fitted around it
