## Start with the likely answer

If you are reading this because a large dark bee is working around your deck,
your eaves or your fence in Whatcom County, the probability sits heavily on one
side: it is a bumble bee.

That is not a hedge and it is not a way of talking you out of a service call. It
is the distribution. The USDA Forest Service places the genus *Xylocopa* across
the southern United States from Arizona to Florida and up the eastern seaboard
as far as New York. That is where the volume of carpenter bee content comes
from, and it is where the volume of carpenter bee damage is. Northwest
Washington is not in the middle of that map.

What this corner of the state does have, in real numbers, is bumble bees. WSU's
home garden bulletin counts at least twenty-three species in Washington and
names the black-tailed, fuzzy-horned and Vosnesensky bumble bees among the
common ones on this side of the mountains. The university's Hortsense sheet puts
the state total higher still, at twenty-nine. Either way, the large fuzzy bee in
front of you belongs to a genus that is genuinely abundant here, and the one you
read about online belongs to a genus that mostly is not.

So the first job on this page is the identification, and the second is telling
you honestly what to do in the minority of cases where it really is a carpenter
bee.

## The one difference that settles it

Ignore size. Both are large, both are dark, both are loud in flight, and neither
comparison works without the other insect present.

**Look at the abdomen — the rear section.** A bumble bee is covered in dense hair
along its whole length, banded in yellow, black, white, orange or brown
depending on species. A carpenter bee has a furry thorax and then an abdomen
that is bare, black and glossy. UC IPM puts it as abdomens shinier than a
bumble bee's, sometimes with a metallic blue cast. Penn State reduces the whole
thing to shiny against fuzzy.

It is visible in a phone photograph from a few feet away, and it does not need a
specimen or a lens. If the back half throws a highlight like a polished shoe,
that is a carpenter bee. If it looks like a scrap of felt, it is not.

**Two secondary checks**, useful when the light is bad. Male carpenter bees carry
a pale patch on the face, which bumble bees do not. And the behavior differs: a
bumble bee goes about its business among the flowers, while a male carpenter bee
holds a fixed patch of air near the nest wood and confronts whatever enters it,
including you.

**Where it is nesting resolves the rest.** WSU describes bumble bee colonies in
abandoned rodent burrows, old bird nests, house insulation and similar cavities
where dry fluffy material is already available. They occupy a hole. They do not
make one. A round tunnel bored into solid timber is not bumble bee work.

## If it is a bumble bee

Then the answer is to leave it, and that is the whole answer.

WSU's management line for the genus is short — none recommended — and the
reasoning behind it is not sentimental. Bumble bees are among the most effective
pollinators available to a garden here, they buzz-pollinate tomatoes and
blueberries in a way honey bees cannot, and several Washington species are in
documented decline.

Temperament is not the problem people expect either. WSU describes Pacific
Northwest bumble bees as very mild and unwilling to attack unless a nest is
seriously disturbed or threatened. A bee working flowers along a walkway is not
a hazard.

And the colony ends by itself. Bumble bee colonies are annual: a queen starts
one in spring, it builds to anywhere from a dozen to a few hundred workers, and
it declines and dies out somewhere between early summer and October, leaving only
next year's queens overwintering elsewhere. A nest under a shed floor in June is
not a permanent tenant. It is a seasonal one that will resolve without anybody
spending money.

Where a nest sits somewhere genuinely unworkable — a doorway, a play area, a
household with a diagnosed sting allergy — that is a real conversation and there
are things worth doing. Everywhere else, the honest recommendation is a wide
berth until autumn.

## What an actual carpenter bee hole looks like

Now the minority case.

**The entrance.** One hole, essentially a perfect circle, slightly under half an
inch across — Ohio State gives it as roughly the diameter of the bee's own body.
The regularity is the striking part. It looks drilled, and people frequently
assume it was, until they see the second one.

**The turn.** The female bores straight in across the grain for an inch or so,
then makes an abrupt right-angle turn and runs the gallery **along the grain**
from there. That right angle is diagnostic. Nothing else in local timber does it.

**The pace and the length.** Ohio State puts single-season excavation at four to
six inches, at about an inch every six days. Penn State gives roughly an inch a
day for a working female. Either way it is slow, and one season of one bee is a
few inches of tunnel.

**The chambers.** Inside, the gallery is divided into cells, each provisioned
with pollen and sealed off with a partition the female builds from chewed wood
and saliva. The Forest Service describes the result as very similar to
particle board, which is a good description of what you find if a board is
opened.

**The sawdust.** Coarse, pale, and dropped directly below the entrance. It is
excavated material rather than digested material — carpenter bees do not eat
wood any more than carpenter ants do — so it looks like sawdust because that is
effectively what it is.

**The staining.** A dark streak below the hole is common and it is not decay. It
is waste, and it is often the thing that makes a fascia board look worse than it
is.

## The wood they choose, and the wood they ignore

Bare and weathered softwood, on horizontal or downward-facing surfaces.

Ohio State lists cedar, redwood, cypress, pine and fir as preferred, with
straight grain, and notes that they avoid timber that is well painted or still
carrying bark. In this county that puts the risk squarely on unfinished fascia,
deck rails and joists, handrails, exposed beam ends, fence rails, arbors,
outbuilding framing and the underside of anything left bare.

It also explains why the exposure here concentrates in the farm country and
around the lake rather than in a Bellingham grid neighborhood. Barns,
outbuildings, fence lines and stored lumber in Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and
Sumas offer unlimited unfinished softwood. So do the weathered decks around
Sudden Valley and Silver Beach, and the salt-weathered exterior timber at Blaine,
Birch Bay and Semiahmoo, where a finish does not last as long as it does inland.

The corollary is the whole prevention strategy: a maintained coat of paint or
solid stain is the deterrent. Clear finishes and semi-transparent stains do less,
because what discourages a female is a hard continuous film rather than a color.

## The male that charges you

Worth its own heading because it drives more phone calls than the damage does.

The bee that hovers a foot from your face, drops in behind you as you walk past
the deck, and generally behaves as though it means it, is a male holding
territory near a nest. **He has no sting.** Not a mild sting, not a reluctant
sting — male bees do not possess the anatomy. The display is entirely bluff.

Females are equipped and, per UC IPM, use it only when provoked or handled
roughly. In practice that means grabbing one, trapping one against skin, or
putting a hand over an occupied gallery.

There is also no colony behind either of them. This is a solitary bee, or at
most a mother and her daughters sharing a nest, which the Forest Service notes
is why some researchers describe them as primitively social. Nothing swarms out
in defense. Compared with a yellowjacket nest in the same rail, the risk profile
is not in the same category.

## They are pollinators, and that is load-bearing

This matters to the verdict, so it is not a courtesy paragraph.

The Forest Service describes carpenter bees as among the largest native bees in
the country and as buzz pollinators — they grip a flower and use the thoracic
muscles to sonicate pollen loose from the anthers, which makes them effective on
tomatoes, eggplant and other crops that will not release pollen otherwise. Penn
State places native bees, this genus included, behind roughly fifteen percent of
agricultural crop pollination.

They are not uniformly virtuous. On tubular flowers such as salvias and
penstemons they cut a slit at the base of the corolla and take nectar without
pollinating anything, which is worth knowing if you grow them.

But the balance is clear enough that killing a bee working a fence line well away
from the house is a bad trade. Given how rarely the genus turns up here at all,
it is a worse trade than it would be in Ohio.

## When the damage is cosmetic, and when it stops being

Both halves of this are true and the industry usually only tells you one.

**Cosmetic, in most cases.** UC IPM is direct that carpenter bees rarely cause
severe damage, and describes the effect as unsightly holes and staining. One
season, one female, four to six inches of gallery in a fascia board is a
maintenance item. It is not a structural event, it is not urgent, and it does not
justify anybody talking about your building's integrity.

**Not cosmetic, when it accumulates.** The mechanism is reuse. New adults
overwinter inside the old gallery and emerge from it the following spring, and
Penn State describes the same nests being used year after year and growing more
elaborate with each generation. UC IPM gives the far end of that: tunnels
extending as much as ten feet into a timber where many bees have worked it over
the years.

So the question to ask about a board is not how many holes are in it this year.
It is how long this has been going on. A fascia with one fresh hole is nothing.
The same fascia with a dozen entrances at different ages, some weathered and dark
and some pale, is carrying a network you cannot see and has probably lost real
section along its length.

**The other escalation is water.** An open gallery in a horizontal exterior
member is a channel for rain into the middle of the board. In this climate that
is how a nuisance becomes decay, and the decay will take more out of the timber
than the bees did.

## The year, as it runs here

Both insects on this page are seasonal, and a lot of unnecessary work gets booked
because somebody called at the wrong point in the cycle.

**Late winter and early spring.** WSU describes bumble bee queens searching for
nest sites in February and March, flying slowly and low around buildings and
gardens, working along foundations and under sheds. A single large bee cruising a
crawl vent in March is a queen shopping, not an infestation, and she will
generally settle somewhere that is nobody's problem.

**Spring.** Carpenter bees that overwintered inside a gallery emerge, mate, and
females begin excavating or extending. Ohio State puts development from egg to
adult at about seven weeks, with one generation a year at this latitude. This is
the wrong moment to fill anything.

**Late spring and early summer.** Peak nuisance, entirely because of the males.
The hovering behavior is at its most obvious now, which is when the phone rings.
Bumble bee colonies are also building toward full size through this period.

**Late summer and autumn.** The season's carpenter bees have emerged; bumble bee
colonies decline and die out, somewhere between early summer and October
depending on species and site. This is the window for repair, for filling
galleries, and for putting a finish on the timber before winter.

The practical version: identify in spring, act in autumn. Very little that gets
done in May improves on what the same work achieves in September, and a good deal
of it goes wrong.

## Why so much of the advice you will find is wrong for here

Worth naming, because it is the reason this page exists at all.

The carpenter bee content that dominates search results is written for regions
where the insect is genuinely abundant — the Southeast, the mid-Atlantic, the
lower Midwest. In those places a single property can carry dozens of galleries
across a barn or a porch, the damage compounds fast, and an annual treatment is a
defensible product.

Copy that material onto a Pacific Northwest site without checking the
distribution and three things go wrong at once. It implies readers here will
routinely meet the insect, when the Forest Service's own range description says
otherwise. It sells treatment for an animal that most callers do not actually
have. And it teaches people to read a large fuzzy bee as a structural threat,
which in this county means teaching them to worry about a declining native
pollinator.

We would rather be the site that says plainly that this one is uncommon here.
Nobody loses anything by knowing it, and a household that correctly identifies a
bumble bee and leaves it alone has been served better than one that paid for a
treatment.

## What to send before anybody drives out

Identification does almost all of the work on this species, and it can be done
from a phone.

**A photograph from behind or above**, showing the rear half of the insect. That
single frame separates shiny from fuzzy and usually ends the question.

**A photograph of the hole with something for scale** — a coin, a fingertip, a
tape. Half an inch and circular is one answer; a pencil-lead hole with powder
below it is a completely different one and lives on the
[anobiid page](/pest-library/anobiid-powderpost-beetle/).

**A wider shot of the board**, so the number and the age spread of the openings
are visible. One fresh hole and fifteen weathered ones are not the same finding.

**Where it is.** Fascia, deck rail, fence, barn, shed, or a hole in the ground
under a shed step — that last one is a bumble bee entrance and settles it before
anyone looks at the bee.

## Woodpeckers, which do the visible damage

The most dramatic damage attributed to carpenter bees is usually not theirs.

Woodpeckers detect larvae and overwintering adults inside galleries and open the
wood up to reach them. Ohio State describes birds riddling the wood in search of
immature and adult bees, particularly targeting overwintering adults, and
producing extensive damage in the process. A neat half-inch hole becomes a torn
strip of fascia.

If you have fresh woodpecker work on a board, there is frequently a gallery
behind it, and the torn timber is now an open water path as well. That is the
scenario where a carpenter bee finding does turn into a genuine repair, and it is
worth dealing with before a winter runs through it.

## What we would actually do

The verdict on this page is *rarely warranted*, and it is honest.

For most of what gets reported to us as carpenter bees, the work is
identification and then nothing — because it is a bumble bee, and the answer is
to leave it alone until the colony ends in autumn.

For a genuine carpenter bee finding, the honest description of the job is that
**this is a fascia repair and a coat of paint**. Not a treatment program, not a
recurring plan, not a structural intervention. Where the galleries are active and
numerous enough that repair alone would just be sealing bees into a board,
treating the gallery first is a legitimate step — and then the repair still has
to happen, or the same board gets chosen again next spring.

The order that works:

**Confirm the species**, from the abdomen, from the shape of the hole, or from a
photograph sent before anybody drives out.

**Establish whether the gallery is occupied.** Fresh sawdust below an entrance,
traffic in and out, a male on station nearby.

**Wait for the right window if you can.** Late summer or autumn, once the
season's bees have emerged, is the clean moment. Filling in spring while a
female is provisioning cells is both cruel and ineffective — she will simply cut
a new exit.

**Then plug and finish.** Ohio State's method is to fill the tunnel thoroughly
with plastic wood, steel wool or copper gauze and seal the opening with putty or
a glued dowel. UC IPM suggests steel wool and caulk to the same end. Then paint
the surface, because an unfinished repair is an invitation.

**Replace what has lost section.** A fascia board with a decade of galleries in it
is joinery work, and it belongs to a carpenter rather than to us. So does
anything the woodpeckers have opened up.

## What we will not sell you

**A recurring plan for a bee that is uncommon here.** The genus is not abundant
in this county, the damage is slow, and an annual subscription against it is not
a defensible product.

**Treatment of a bumble bee nest that will end on its own in a few weeks.** It is
a protected-in-practice pollinator with a documented decline, WSU recommends no
control, and we agree.

**Filling an occupied gallery on the first visit**, which traps the bees, wastes
the filler and produces a second hole.

**A treatment where the honest answer is a paintbrush.** If the board is bare and
the bees are gone, finishing the timber does more for you than anything we could
apply to it.
