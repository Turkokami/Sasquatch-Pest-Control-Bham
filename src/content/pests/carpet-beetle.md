## You are looking at the wrong life stage

Nearly every carpet beetle call starts with beetles on a windowsill, and nearly
every one of those beetles is harmless.

UC IPM puts it plainly. Adults of all three common species seek out pollen and
nectar, and they are generally found outdoors. The varied carpet beetle in
particular is typically seen near windows, on sills, on drapes or on the panes
themselves. Kentucky's fact sheet says the same thing from the other direction:
adult carpet beetles feed on flower pollen and do not damage woolens and other
fabrics.

So the insect a household finds, photographs and kills is a pollen feeder trying
to get back outside to a flower. Removing it accomplishes almost nothing, which
is exactly why so many people report that they have been killing beetles for
months and the rug keeps thinning.

The damage is done by larvae, somewhere else in the building, in the dark. Find
those and the problem becomes solvable. Keep working the windowsill and it does
not.

That inversion is the single most useful thing on this page, and everything below
is a way of acting on it.

## It came in with something, or it was already here

Before any of the searching, it is worth settling where this problem comes from,
because the answer changes what money is worth spending.

This is not a building failure. A carpet beetle problem does not mean a house is
poorly sealed or poorly kept, and no amount of caulking prevents one. What
happens is that a food supply and an egg-laying female end up in the same still,
dark place — and the food supply came in through the front door inside something
somebody bought, inherited or brought home.

A secondhand wool rug. A box of textiles out of a parent's attic. A taxidermy
piece, a feather trim, a sheepskin, a natural-bristle brush, a bag of vintage
clothing. Adults also fly in on their own from flowers outside, which is how a
brand new house with a new wool carpet acquires them.

Or the supply was already inside the structure and nobody knew: a nest in a
soffit that has been there since before the current owners, or a pocket of dead
insects in a wall cavity.

Either way, the work is finding the source. Treating the room where the beetles
were noticed, without finding what they are eating, is the most expensive
mistake available on this species and it is made constantly.

## What the adults and larvae actually look like

Worth separating properly, because they look like two unrelated animals.

**Adults.** UC IPM gives the varied carpet beetle at about one tenth of an inch,
black with an irregular pattern of white, brown and dark yellow scales. The
furniture carpet beetle is slightly larger and rounder with a mottled look from
black spots interspersed among white and orange scales, and the black carpet
beetle runs from one eighth to three sixteenths of an inch, shiny black to dark
brown with brownish legs. Kentucky's summary is oval beetles of one sixteenth to
one eighth of an inch, in black or various mottled patterns.

They are small, round, hard and slow. Nothing about them looks dangerous, and
nothing about them is.

**Larvae.** Kentucky describes them at one eighth to one quarter of an inch, tan
to brownish, densely covered with hairs or bristles. UC IPM adds that varied
carpet beetle larvae are slightly longer than the adults, covered in dense tufts
of hair, and that when disturbed they raise those tufts upright into a round
plume. They carry alternating light and dark brown bands, broad at the rear and
narrow at the front — the furniture species is shaped the other way round, and
the black carpet beetle larva is smooth and hard rather than fuzzy, reaching as
much as five sixteenths of an inch.

The word people reach for is caterpillar, and the movement is right for that:
slow, deliberate, tapered, curling when touched. The bristles are what makes it
distinctive, and they matter again further down this page.

## The cast skins are the evidence

If you remember one search instruction from this profile, make it this one.

Carpet beetle larvae molt repeatedly as they grow, and each molt leaves the old
skin behind intact. UC IPM describes those skins as brown, shell-like and
bristly-looking, and says that finding them together with an absence of webbing
is usually a good clue that carpet beetles rather than moths are responsible.
Kentucky simply notes that shed skins are left behind.

They are far easier to find than the larvae. A larva hides and moves away from
light and disturbance. A cast skin sits where it was abandoned and stays there
until something removes it, so skins accumulate at the feeding site and record
months of activity that nobody witnessed.

What that gives you is a map. Run a light along carpet edges, lift the rug where
it disappears under the sofa, pull out the bottom of the closet, check the
baseboard line behind furniture and look inside heating vents. Where the hollow
bristly shells pile up is where the larvae have been eating.

They also sit at the center of the bed bug question later on this page, so it is
worth learning to recognize one.

## Keratin, and the soiling exception

The diet is the same protein the clothes moth is after, and the list is short
enough to memorize.

Kentucky gives wool, felt, fur, silk, feathers and leather, plus lint, hair and
debris, plus bird nests, animal carcasses and dead insects. UC IPM adds hair
brushes with natural bristles, pet hair and feathers, and notes that these
larvae occasionally feed on stored products such as certain spices and grains.

Cotton, linen and synthetics are not on that list, and a larva put on clean
polyester has nothing to work with. The exception is soiling: sweat, body oils
and food residue deposit digestible material onto fibers that had none, which is
why a blended or synthetic item that was put away dirty can be damaged while a
clean one beside it is not.

That single rule explains the pattern of loss in a house. It is why a wool rug is
grazed only at the edge that lives under furniture, why the damage in a wardrobe
is at collars and cuffs, and why an item worn regularly is effectively safe while
one folded away in a box since a house move is not.

It also explains the difference in damage style between this beetle and a moth.
Beetle larvae tend to graze irregular patches and thin the pile of a carpet
rather than punching a clean hole, and they will work in areas with more light
than a moth larva will tolerate.

## The rash that gets read as bites

This is why the [bed bug](/pest-library/bed-bug/) profile sends people here, and
it deserves careful handling rather than a confident one.

Penn State Extension documents carpet beetle dermatitis as a real clinical
entity. The larvae of several dermestid genera, *Anthrenus* among them, carry
lancelet-shaped hairs with sharp ends, and *Anthrenus* species in particular
carry modified spear-shaped hairs clumped on the rear abdominal segments. The
reaction involves those hairs and the insect's hemolymph, and Penn State
describes what people present with as itching, pruritic, papulovesicular
eruptions — with some also reporting irritation of the respiratory tract and
eyes. It is characterized as an acquired allergic reaction, which is to say
sensitization builds over long exposure rather than appearing on first contact.

Now the honest part, and it is the same principle the bed bug page is built on.

**A mark on skin does not identify what caused it.** That is true of bed bugs, it
is true of fleas, and it is true here. Somebody waking with itchy welts cannot
reason backward from the welts to the animal, and neither can a photograph of
them, and neither can we. A reaction is the body's response, and different bodies
respond differently to the same cause and identically to different ones.

What can be settled is physical evidence. Bed bugs leave dark fecal spotting at
mattress seams and frame joints, smooth oval cast skins, cemented eggs and,
sometimes, the insects. Carpet beetles leave bristly hollow skins in carpet and
closet floors and no spotting on bedding whatsoever. Those two sets of findings
are easy to tell apart and they are what the search is for.

So the useful sequence is: search the bed properly first, and if nothing is
there, search the floor. Welts plus larval skins in the carpet plus a clean
mattress is a coherent picture. Welts alone is not a diagnosis of anything, and
treating a bedroom on the strength of it is how households spend a lot of money
on the wrong problem.

If the reaction is severe, spreading or not settling, that is a physician's
question. Our part is finding out what is in the room.

## The source nobody thinks of

When there is a beetle problem and no wool anywhere, the answer is almost always
something dead.

Dermestids are scavengers by trade — in nature they are the insects that clean
carcasses and old nests. Kentucky's food list names bird nests, animal carcasses
and dead insects explicitly, and includes cluster flies, lady beetles, stink bugs
and wasps among the dead insects in question.

In a Whatcom County house that translates into a short list of places.

**An old bird nest** in a soffit, gable vent, dryer vent or eave. Feathers,
dropped food, insect remains and sometimes dead nestlings. One abandoned nest
feeds a population for years and sends new adults into the living space every
spring.

**A rodent nest, or a rodent that died in a void.** Same material, same result.

**A wasp nest in a wall.** Old comb, dead brood and dead adults.

**An accumulation of dead overwintering insects** in a cavity. Cluster flies and
conifer seed bugs come into wall voids by the hundred here, and what dies there
becomes food.

The reframe that resolves these calls is to stop asking which fabric is being
eaten and start asking what died in the building and where. It also explains a
pattern people find baffling: beetles appearing every spring in a house with wood
floors, tile and not a scrap of wool in it.

## When they show up here

Development is slow, and our climate makes it slower.

UC IPM's life-cycle table is worth reading for the scale of it. The varied carpet
beetle spends 220 to 630 days as a larva. The furniture species runs 70 to 94
days, and the black carpet beetle 166 to 330. Eggs hatch in roughly one to three
weeks and pupation takes eight to seventeen days, but the larval stage dominates
everything, and adult females live only a matter of weeks.

Whatcom County has no hard freeze to interrupt any of that and a long cool damp
season that pushes every figure toward the slow end of its range. Unheated
storage — a garage in Blaine, a crawlspace in Sudden Valley, an uninsulated attic
over an older Bellingham house — holds larvae comfortably through winter without
killing them.

The visible event is spring. Kentucky notes that adults often appear on
windowsills in springtime, and that is when the calls arrive: a household sees
beetles at the glass in April and assumes the problem started in April, when in
fact the larvae have been feeding since the previous year.

The practical consequence is about patience rather than urgency. Against a larval
stage measured in hundreds of days, a quiet fortnight after a cleaning is not
evidence of anything, and a clear result has to be judged across a season.

## Carpet beetle or clothes moth

They eat the same protein and damage the same items, so the separation is done on
leftovers.

**Webbing means moth.** Silk patches, tubes or a matted crust of fiber and frass
stuck to the surface of the material. UC IPM's clothes moth guidance describes
the webbing species as producing patches of silk webbing that accumulate waste
and fabric particles.

**Bristly hollow skins with no webbing means beetle.**

The damage differs too, though less reliably. Moth larvae work in genuinely dark
undisturbed places and tend to produce defined holes or a grazed patch under silk.
Beetle larvae thin the pile of a carpet across an irregular area and are less
fussy about light.

Finding both is common in an old house with wool and a nest in a void, and where
that happens the two need addressing together. The
[webbing clothes moth](/pest-library/webbing-clothes-moth/) profile covers the
other half.

## The cleaning that does most of the work

For a straightforward fabric case, the household does more than any application
does, and it is worth being specific about the order.

**Vacuum thoroughly and repeatedly.** UC IPM calls frequent thorough vacuuming an
effective way of removing food sources along with eggs, larvae and adults, and
Kentucky notes that vacuuming floors, carpets and inside heating vents removes
larvae. This is not tidying — it is the physical removal of the population and
the lint, pet hair and dead insects feeding it. Work carpet edges, under and
behind furniture that never moves, closet floors, baseboard lines and vents, and
empty the machine outdoors afterward.

**Launder or dry clean what can take it.** Kentucky's line is that dry cleaning
or hot laundering kills any eggs or larvae present, and UC IPM says thorough
laundering in hot water or dry cleaning kills all stages.

**Freeze what cannot be washed.** UC IPM gives two weeks below eighteen degrees
Fahrenheit for an object sealed in a plastic bag, and ten to fourteen days for
trophies and mounted specimens. Kentucky gives zero degrees for at least a week,
or seventy-two hours at minus twenty. The Pacific Northwest handbook, writing
about material in storage, gives three or four days in a freezer before
discarding. Bag it, press the air out, and give a valuable item a second freeze
after it has warmed fully through.

**Heat, where the object tolerates it.** UC IPM gives at least thirty minutes at
one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit or higher in an oven. That suits very few
household textiles and a lot of them will be damaged by it, so freezing is the
safer default.

**Then store properly.** Clean items only, into containers that actually close.

## When treatment is warranted, and when it is not

The verdict here is *depends*, and it splits cleanly in two.

**A cleaning job.** Damage traceable to identifiable fabric, a findable feeding
site, no unexplained recurrence. Vacuum hard, launder, freeze, re-store, and
check again in a few months. Most households in this situation do not need to pay
anybody, and we would rather say so than book a visit that adds nothing.

**A treatment job.** Fixed carpet or a large rug where the feeding site is under
furniture that cannot practically be moved. A collection with real value at risk.
Repeated seasons of damage after genuine cleaning. And, most often, a source
inside the structure.

That last case is the one where calling makes obvious sense, because the food is
somewhere a vacuum does not reach. Getting an old nest or a dead animal out of a
soffit or a wall void is the actual work, and the beetle treatment around it is a
minor part of the job. Where a rodent is involved that runs through
[rodent control](/services/rodent-control/), and the
[exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) side matters because sealing an
entry without removing what is inside leaves the food supply in place.

It is also the reason we decline to treat overwintering insects inside wall
voids. A few hundred dead flies in a cavity is precisely the material this beetle
develops in, and killing them there creates the next problem.

Where we do apply anything, it goes on harborage — carpet edges, under fixed
furniture, closet structure, baseboard voids. Not on textiles, and not broadcast
across a floor people live on.

## Where they turn up around the county

The pattern here follows housing stock and bird habit more than it follows any
map of climate.

**Older city housing** — the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York, South
Hill. Wool rugs over fir floors, a century of lint and hair packed under
baseboards and behind trim, and eaves that have hosted nesting birds through
several owners. This is where the cases turn out to be structural rather than
textile, and where the search goes upward rather than into the closet.

**Lake Whatcom and the wooded edges** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor.
Cedar construction with generous soffits, second homes where textiles sit
untouched for months, and heavy bird and rodent nesting pressure against the
building.

**Rural properties** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Barn and outbuilding
material, working wool, feed storage, and animal remains at a scale a town lot
does not produce.

**Any house with feeders.** Feeding birds brings birds close to the structure,
and birds close to a structure eventually nest in it. That is a slower and less
obvious route into a beetle problem than a bag of seed is into a pantry one, but
it is the same principle.

**Newer construction is not exempt**, because the adults fly in from flowers and
because a new wool carpet is as edible as an old one.

## What we will not do

**Broadcast product across the floor of a room people live in.** Application
belongs in harborage, and a treated floor does not reach a larva under a rug edge
anyway.

**Treat around a nest instead of removing it.** Leaving the food in place while
killing what is currently eating it produces a repeat, and charging for the
repeat is not a business we want.

**Spray a valuable textile.** Where the right answer is a conservator rather than
a pest control company, that is what we will say.

**Call a single visit finished.** With a larval stage that UC IPM measures in
hundreds of days, one clean visit is not proof of anything, and a follow-up is
part of the honest plan rather than an upsell.

**Sell a house-wide treatment for a localized problem.** If the answer is one rug
edge, one closet, or one nest in a soffit, that is what gets dealt with.

## Where to start

Collect a specimen if you can. A larva or a cast skin on clear tape, or a sharp
photograph next to a fingertip for scale, settles the identification faster than
any description.

Tell us where the larvae and skins are rather than where the beetles are. Skins
in a rug edge point at the site; adults at a window point at the exit.

If there is no wool in the house at all, say so early, because it moves the
search off the contents and into the structure.

And if the reason you are reading this is welts rather than fabric damage, look
at the [bed bug](/pest-library/bed-bug/) profile alongside this one and search
the bed properly before assuming either answer.
