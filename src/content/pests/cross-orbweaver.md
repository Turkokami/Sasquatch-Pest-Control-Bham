## The one you walked into

Somewhere in the first half of September, in a great many Whatcom County
households, somebody walks out to the car in the dark and puts their face
through a web. Then they look up and find something the size of a grape hanging
in the middle of it, patterned, motionless, and apparently enormous.

That is a cross orbweaver, *Araneus diadematus*, and this page exists mostly to
tell you cheerfully that nothing needs doing about it.

It is harmless. It is temporary. It is eating a steady volume of the flying
insects you would rather not have. And it is very likely to be gone from that
exact spot within a couple of weeks whether or not anybody intervenes.

## Recognizing one

The mark that gives the species its name is a set of pale spots on top of the
abdomen, arranged into a rough cross. Penn State Extension describes four
elongated spots toward the front of the abdomen forming the shape, on a
background that runs anywhere from yellow through brown to dark gray, with a
carapace carrying three darker lengthwise bands.

Size is the other thing people notice. Females run roughly 6.5 to 20 millimeters
in the body — the largest, in a good year, genuinely substantial — while males
come in at 5.5 to 13 millimeters and are usually seen only when one is hanging
about at the edge of a female's web hoping to be tolerated.

Color is close to useless for identification here. Individuals vary enormously,
younger animals tend darker and adults lighter, and two spiders on adjacent
shrubs can look like different species. Go by the cross of pale spots and by the
web, which is a much easier thing to identify than its occupant.

## The web is the giveaway

A cross orbweaver builds the textbook spider web: a vertical wheel with radiating
spokes and a spiral of sticky capture silk, up to about 40 centimeters across,
strung between whatever anchor points are available. Doorways, gaps between
shrubs, the space between a downspout and a railing, fence lines, the frame of a
window, the gap between a wing mirror and a hedge.

The thing worth knowing about it — and the reason knocking one down achieves so
little — is that the web is disposable by design. These spiders build a new one
regularly, taking down the old one and consuming the silk along with whatever it
caught before spinning a replacement. Silk is expensive protein and the animal
recycles it rather than abandoning it.

So the web across your door in the morning is not a structure that took weeks to
build. It took part of a night, and if you clear it, another one can be there
tomorrow. That is not persistence or spite. It is the ordinary operating cycle of
the animal.

It also means the web is a snapshot rather than a monument. A large orb web
maintained night after night in one location is telling you that the location
reliably catches insects. Which is the genuinely useful thing this spider has to
say about your property.

## What the web actually costs the spider

The scale of the thing is worth sitting with for a moment, because it explains
why removing one is such a mild act.

Washington's NatureMapping fact sheet for the species records webs reaching
around 40 centimeters across, spun using three pairs of spinnerets, and rebuilt
on a daily cycle — the old web taken down and consumed along with whatever it
caught, then a new one put up.

Silk is protein, and protein is expensive. An animal that eats its own building
every night and puts up a replacement is running a tight budget, which is why
the placement is never random. A web goes where the returns justify the outlay,
and it moves when they stop doing so.

That is also the entire reason the broom works. You are not destroying weeks of
labor or provoking anything. You are changing the return on a site the spider is
already reassessing nightly, and it will conclude on its own that a doorway
people walk through twice an evening is not worth the silk.

If you have never watched one build, dusk in the first half of September is the
time, and it takes about as long as a cup of tea. It is the most persuasive
argument anybody has ever made to a person who is frightened of spiders, and it
is free.

## The web as a food-supply reading

This is the practical part, and it applies to every spider on the property rather
than just this one.

Spiders are predators. They cannot manufacture their own food supply and they do
not settle where there is nothing to catch. A large, well-maintained orb web in a
particular spot is a measurement: something is putting a lot of flying insects
through that airspace every night.

Nine times out of ten in this county the answer is a light. Extension guidance
across the region ties orb weavers to buildings with exterior lighting, and the
mechanism is not subtle — a bright white fixture near a door pulls in flying
insects from a considerable distance all night long, and an animal that eats
flying insects sets up directly in the flight path. USU Extension's management
note for orb weavers specifically suggests changing the outdoor bulb type as a
control measure, which is a striking recommendation to find in a pest guide,
because it is not aimed at the spider at all.

The other answers are standing water, damp ground, poor drainage, a wet
crawlspace venting into the airspace under the deck, compost, and vegetation
holding moisture against the wall. All of them grow insects, and the spider count
follows.

So if orb webs are appearing in numbers around one particular corner of a
building, the reading is that the corner is producing insects. Deal with the
insects and the spiders relocate, without anybody treating anything. Deal with
the spiders and the insects carry on unchecked, which is a strange outcome to pay
for.

## Why September

The season is a life-cycle effect rather than a weather effect, and it is the
same story that drives every spider question here in autumn.

Adults are on show from late summer through fall. The females have spent the
whole season growing — hatched in spring, tiny and unnoticed through the summer,
eating steadily — and by late August they have reached full size at exactly the
moment their prey is most abundant. A spider that was invisible in July is a
conspicuous animal in a conspicuous web by the first week of September.

Nothing arrived. Nothing moved in. The same population has been on the property
since spring at a size nobody notices.

Then, in the second half of September, the females leave the webs and go looking
for somewhere sheltered to lay. Penn State Extension puts the clutch at 300 to
900 eggs in a domed silk cocoon, tucked under dead bark, into a crevice, behind a
shutter, under the lip of a windowsill. The adults die off with the weather. The
eggs sit through the winter, hatch in spring, and the spiderlings disperse —
several of them on threads of silk carried by the wind, which is how a spider
covers ground it could never walk.

That annual reset is why the "problem" solves itself every year without
assistance, and why it comes back every year regardless of what was done about
it. You are not looking at a population building up over time. You are looking at
one cohort reaching adult size on schedule.

## It does not live in your house

Worth stating clearly, because it is the fear behind most of the calls: this is
not a spider that moves indoors.

It needs anchor points wide enough for a large orb web and a stream of flying
insects to intercept, and the inside of a house supplies neither. USU Extension's
note on the group is that they are often found around buildings and homes but do
not typically invade interior spaces, preferring exterior locations near
lighting.

One will occasionally wander in through an open door in autumn, the same way a
leaf does. It will not establish, it will not build a working web in a hallway,
and it will not still be there in a fortnight. If you find one indoors, a glass
and a piece of card puts it back on the porch, where it will get on with what it
was doing.

The autumn indoor wave that drives the phone calls in this county is a different
group of spiders entirely — the funnel weavers, whose mature males walk in search
of females in exactly these weeks and who are perfectly capable of living inside.
Orb weavers are the ones you meet on the way to the car. Funnel weavers are the
ones you meet in the bathtub.

## Bites, briefly

Washington's NatureMapping fact sheet describes the bite as slightly unpleasant
and harmless to people. Utah State Extension records orb weavers as not known to
be a health hazard, and lists them as beneficial. Penn State's entry, which is
the most cautious of the three, notes that verified bites can produce pain,
swelling and redness, with occasional broader discomfort, and reactions varying
between individuals.

Read together: getting bitten by one would be unpleasant and inconsequential, and
you are unlikely to manage it. This is an animal that sits in the middle of a web
waiting for something small, and it has no interest in an object the size of a
person. The Burke Museum's arachnologist, who has handled tens of thousands of
spiders across a career, records three bites in forty-four years, all trivial.

The Washington State Department of Health's most useful contribution on the whole
subject of spider bites is a warning that runs the other way: unexplained wounds
are often misdiagnosed as spider bites, and that misdiagnosis can leave the
actual condition untreated. If you have a wound that concerns you, the answer is
a physician rather than a spider hunt.

## What to do about a web in the way

A web across a doorway, a gate, a path or the space where you park is a genuine
nuisance and you are entitled to remove it. There is no conservation argument for
walking into silk twice a day.

Take it down with a broom or a stick. Do it in the evening, before the spider has
invested a night in rebuilding, and do it two or three evenings running. Orb
weavers relocate when a site keeps failing, and a spot that gets destroyed every
night stops being worth the silk. The spider moves a few feet to somewhere out of
the traffic and everybody gets what they wanted.

If you would rather move the animal than the web, the glass-and-card method works
on an orb weaver as well as anything else. Put it in a shrub twenty feet away.

What is not worth doing is treating for it. Applying a product to a doorframe to
kill a spider that was going to leave anyway, and whose replacement will arrive
from the garden next week, buys nothing. It also costs something — the flying
insects the web was intercepting carry on into the house instead.

## Why our verdict is that we do not treat these

The verdict at the top of this page says we do not treat for cross orbweavers.
That is a real position rather than a rhetorical one, and here is the reasoning
behind it.

WSU's own Hortsense fact sheet classifies spiders in the home landscape as
beneficial natural enemies rather than pests, notes that Washington has more than
800 spider species and that a typical pesticide-free garden supports twenty to
twenty-five of them, and lists them as predators of aphids, mites, caterpillars,
flies, beetles, leafhoppers, whiteflies, thrips and moths. Its management line —
the field where a pest guide would normally tell you what to apply — reads: none
recommended.

Oregon State's public guidance says much the same in plainer language: remove
spiders found indoors, leave spiders outdoors alone. The regional handbook that
WSU, Oregon State and the University of Idaho publish jointly advises against
pesticide treatments for spiders on effectiveness grounds before anybody gets to
the ethics of it.

We are a pest control company saying, on our own website, that there is no
product we would sell you for this. The animal is harmless, it is temporary, it
is doing unpaid work on your flies, and the professional consensus among the
people with nothing to sell is unanimous.

Where we do get involved is where webbing has genuinely accumulated on a
building — eaves, entries, porches, outdoor living areas, covered moorage, a
seasonal property that has stood empty through the wet months. That is cleaning
work, we are happy to do it, and it is honest to call it what it is rather than
dressing it up as spider control.

## The other big webs you will see here

A few other web-builders turn up in Whatcom County gardens in the same weeks, and
knowing the differences saves a certain amount of alarm.

**Other orb weavers.** Several related species build the same wheel-shaped web at
various sizes. All of the local ones are harmless, all of them are seasonal, and
all of them are covered by everything above.

**Funnel weavers.** A flat horizontal sheet with a tube at one edge, low down —
window wells, foundation lines, ground vegetation, the corner of a shed. This is
the giant house spider and its relatives, and it is the group whose males come
indoors in autumn.

**Cellar spiders.** Loose, messy, three-dimensional tangles in the corners of
garages, crawlspaces and ceilings, with a very small body on very long thin legs.
Permanent residents indoors, harmless, and they eat other spiders.

**Sheet-web weavers.** Small horizontal hammocks of silk strung in grass and low
shrubs, spectacular on a heavy dew morning across a lawn. Nothing to do with the
house at all.

None of these is a reason to reach for a product, and the web shape identifies
the group far faster than trying to photograph something that is moving.

## Where you meet them around this county

The distribution around a property follows the food and the anchor points, and it
is fairly predictable once you know what to look for.

**Doorways and porches with a light above them.** The single most common
location, and the one that generates the calls. A fixture over a back door is a
nightly insect trap and the web goes straight into the flight path between the
light and the door.

**Between shrubs, along fence lines and across gates.** Anywhere with two
anchor points about a yard apart and open air between them.

**Eaves, downspouts and window frames**, particularly on the sheltered side of a
building where wind will not tear a web apart overnight.

**Gardens and vegetable beds**, where WSU's landscape guidance would file them
firmly under beneficial. A cross orbweaver over a bean row is doing exactly what
a gardener would pay for if it came in a bottle.

**Against the treeline** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor, the Lake
Whatcom shore, anywhere with woods or water on one side. Insect pressure off a
wooded lot or a body of water in late summer is heavy, and the orb weaver density
tracks it exactly.

**Rural fence lines and outbuildings** around Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and
Sumas, often in numbers that startle visitors and that nobody who works the
property has ever considered a problem.

**Waterfront and seasonal property** at Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo, where a
house standing empty for weeks accumulates webbing across every opening because
nothing is walking through it.

What you will not find is one established indoors, in a wall cavity, in an attic
or in a crawlspace. It is an outdoor animal with an outdoor requirement.

## Names, and what people mistake it for

The common names are a small mess and it causes some confusion.

*Cross orbweaver*, *cross spider*, *European garden spider*, *diadem spider* and
*garden spider* are all this animal. It is a European introduction, now
widespread from the Pacific Northwest across to New England and eastern Canada.

"Garden spider" is the one that causes trouble, because it also gets used for the
big black-and-yellow *Argiope* orb weavers that are far more familiar to people
who grew up elsewhere in the country. Those are a different genus with a
different look — heavier black and yellow banding, often a zigzag ribbon of white
silk down the middle of the web — and they are also harmless.

The other regular mix-up is with funnel weavers, which happens indirectly: people
who have read about hobo spiders assume any large spider in autumn might be one,
and then apply that worry to an orb weaver. The two groups are not close
relatives and they are trivially easy to separate by the web alone. A wheel with
spokes, hung vertically in the open air, is an orb weaver. A flat sheet with a
tube at one edge, low down and out of the wind, is a funnel weaver. You do not
need to see the spider at all.

Neither group carries a medical risk in this county. The regional extension
handbook and Washington's health department both put the hobo spider firmly in
the not-dangerous column, and no orb weaver has ever been in the conversation.

## Living with the September spider

For most households the proportionate response to all of this is a broom, a
different porch light, and a slightly better September.

Clear the webs that are in your way and leave the ones that are not. Move the
outside light away from the door, or put a warmer bulb in it, or wire it to a
motion sensor — that single change reduces flying insects, moths, the spiders
that eat them, and the funnel weavers indoors, all at once and permanently.

If there are children in the house, this is one of the better animals in the
Pacific Northwest to be curious about. An orb weaver rebuilding a web is visible,
predictable and entirely safe to watch from a foot away, and a kid who has
watched one at close range in September is generally not the adult who calls a
pest control company about a spider in a bathtub.

And know that it ends. The cohort in your garden right now will be gone by the
time the weather turns properly, without anybody spending a dollar on it, and the
one in the web next September hatched from an egg sac somebody never found.
