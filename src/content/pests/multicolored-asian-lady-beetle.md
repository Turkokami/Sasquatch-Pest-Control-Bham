## The ladybug that is not a ladybug

Every October the same call comes in from all over this county. Hundreds — sometimes
thousands — of orange beetles covering a wall that gets the afternoon sun, working
their way into the gaps around trim and vents, and then turning up indoors on the
warm days of late winter.

People call them ladybugs, and understandably: they look like ladybugs, they are
the right size and shape, and everything anybody has ever been told about ladybugs
is that they are charming and lucky and eat aphids.

This is a different insect. *Harmonia axyridis*, the multicolored Asian lady
beetle, was introduced to North America as a biological control agent for aphids
and is now established across most of the continent. Outdoors it does exactly the
job it was brought here to do and does it well. The difficulty is entirely about
what it does in October, and about two habits that native lady beetles do not
share.

It bites. And it leaves a stain.

Neither of those makes it dangerous. Both of them make the standard reassurance —
that these are completely harmless — wrong in a way that matters to somebody
standing in a bedroom with a hundred of them on the ceiling.

## Recognizing one

Do not look at the spots. The name is literal: this beetle ranges from pale
yellow-orange through to deep red, and carries anywhere from no spots at all to
eighteen. Two individuals from the same wall can look like different species.

The identification is on the pronotum — the segment directly behind the head.
On this species it is whitish with a black marking that reads as an **M** or a
**W** depending on which way round you are looking at the insect. On darker
individuals the marking can be filled in almost solid. That marking, plus the
size at around seven millimeters and the domed oval shape, is what separates it
from the native lady beetles that share the same gardens.

The other identification is behavioral, and it is the one most people actually
use: **native lady beetles do not gather on your house in the hundreds.** If
there is a mass of them on a wall in October, it is this species.

## Why they are on the south wall

The behavior is specific enough to predict, and understanding it is what makes
the timing of any treatment work.

From about the beginning of October, on a warm sunny afternoon following a cold
night, the beetles leave the fields and gardens and go looking for somewhere to
overwinter. What they are orienting to is **sunlight reflecting off a
prominent, light-colored, sun-facing surface** — in practice the south and
south-west elevations of buildings.

That is not an accident of suburbia. In their native range they aggregate on
south-facing rock cliffs and outcrops, and a pale house wall catching the low
autumn sun is the same signal. Your house is not attracting them because
anything is wrong with it. It is attracting them because it looks like a cliff.

Once they land, they work into whatever gaps are available — around window and
door trim, under siding laps, at soffit and fascia junctions, through attic and
gable vents, around utility penetrations. Behind that they settle into wall
voids and attics, and Penn State records aggregations running to tens of
thousands in a single structure.

Then they go dormant, and for most of the winter nobody hears from them.

## What actually happens indoors

Nothing, is the honest answer, until it warms up.

They do not feed indoors. They do not breed indoors. They do not eat wood,
fabric, stored food or anything else in the building, and they do no structural
damage of any kind.

What they do is wake up. On a mild day in January or February, or when the
heating raises the temperature in a wall, dormant beetles become active,
orient toward light, and a proportion of them come out on the wrong side of the
wall — into a bedroom, a bathroom, a light fitting.

This is why the number appearing indoors seems wildly out of proportion to any
opening anybody can find. They are not coming in from outside in February. They
came in months ago, and the house is simply leaking a few of them back out of
its own walls.

**The whole population indoors was set in October.** Nothing done in February
changes it. That single fact decides every sensible decision about this insect.

## The bite

Native lady beetles are, for practical purposes, incapable of bothering anyone.
This one is different, and the extension literature is consistent about it.

University of Minnesota Extension states plainly that some can bite hard enough
to break human skin, causing minor and short-lived discomfort. Penn State
records occasional biting of varying intensity.

What that means in a house: an occasional sharp nip, usually when one is
trapped against skin — under a sleeve, in bedding, when somebody picks one up.
There is no venom, nothing is injected, and there is nothing to treat
afterwards. It is a pinch that surprises you.

It is worth being straight about this rather than repeating the harmless line,
for two reasons. Somebody who has been bitten and then been told the insect
cannot bite stops believing the rest of the advice. And a parent whose child has
been nipped deserves to be told what happened rather than being told it did not.

## The stain, and why not to squash them

When disturbed, the beetle releases a yellow fluid from its leg joints. The term
for it is reflex bleeding, and it is the insect's defense.

It smells. It stains — paint, plaster, curtains, carpet, light-colored
upholstery — and the marks are difficult to remove once they have dried. Penn
State notes that the secretion can raise localized welts on some people.

The practical consequences run in one direction: **do not crush them.** Do not
sweep them into a pile and brush them. Do not swat them against a wall. Every
one of those turns a nuisance into a mark on the decoration.

Vacuuming is the correct method indoors, and both Minnesota and Penn State say
so. Minnesota's specific tip is worth passing on: fit a nylon stocking into the
vacuum hose as a bag so the beetles are captured without being killed or drawn
into the machine, then tie it off and empty it outside away from the building.
Change or empty it promptly, because a bag of dead beetles in a cupboard is its
own problem.

## Allergies, and the part nobody mentions

Continued exposure to these beetles inside a building can produce genuine
allergic responses in some people. Penn State lists rhinoconjunctivitis, asthma
and facial angioedema. Minnesota notes that exposure to dead beetles in a
building can cause allergic reactions.

This is not common and it is not a reason for alarm. It is a reason to take a
large indoor aggregation seriously rather than treating it as purely an
aesthetic problem, particularly in a household where somebody already has
asthma. It is also another argument against the approach in the next section.

## Pets

Penn State records that animals which consume these beetles may sustain
chemical burns inside the mouth.

In practice this is a dog that has eaten a mouthful off a windowsill rather
than one that has crunched a single beetle. Signs would be drooling, pawing at
the mouth, or obvious discomfort. That is a call to a veterinarian, not to a
pest control company, and we would tell you the same on the phone.

## Why we will not spray your wall voids

This is the most important practical point on the page and it is the one that
costs us work.

Once the beetles are in the wall voids and the attic, Penn State advises
directly against using an insecticide on them. The reasoning is straightforward
and it is worth understanding rather than taking on trust.

A large aggregation might be several thousand beetles inside a void. Kill them
in place and you have several thousand carcasses in an inaccessible cavity.
Dried insect bodies are excellent food for **carpet beetles**, whose larvae go
on to eat wool, silk, feathers, stored fabric and taxidermy — and which then
emerge into the living space as a fabric pest in their own right.

So the offer to "treat the infestation in your walls" is a trade: a seasonal
nuisance that damages nothing, exchanged for a fabric-destroying insect with a
year-round life cycle. We are not going to sell you that, and anybody who
offers it either does not know or is hoping you do not.

The same logic applies to fogging or spraying rooms repeatedly through the
winter. It kills the ones that have already emerged, does nothing about the
thousands still dormant behind the plaster, and has to be repeated every time a
warm day wakes another batch.

## What does work, and when

Two things, and both of them happen before the beetles arrive.

**Sealing is the durable half.** Everything that got in used an opening.
Caulking around window and door trim, closing gaps at siding laps and
transitions, screening attic, gable and soffit vents, repairing damaged screens
and closing utility penetrations all reduce the supply permanently. It is
unglamorous work, it is finished when it is finished, and it also handles the
stink bugs, the cluster flies, the spiders and a fair amount of the rodent
pressure at the same time. That is the best value available on this problem.

**Exterior treatment is the timed half.** A correctly applied exterior
application to the elevations they gather on, put down **in late September or
early October before they begin entering**, reduces how many make it inside.
Minnesota is specific about the timing and about concentrating on the south and
west sides, around doors, windows and the roof line.

The timing is what people get wrong, in both directions. Applied too early it
has degraded before the flight; applied once they are already on the wall, most
of the benefit has gone. The window moves by a fortnight or more from year to
year because it is driven by the first cold nights rather than by a date, which
is why this is something to have arranged in advance rather than to react to.

## So why is our answer "depends"

Because the honest recommendation varies with the building, and this site
labels every species with what it actually warrants rather than with what we
would like to sell.

**Where a treatment is worth it:** a building that gets a heavy annual
aggregation, a household with somebody asthmatic or allergic, a property where
the walls are pale and the staining is doing visible damage, or a place where
the sheer number indoors in late winter has become genuinely difficult to live
with. In those cases exterior work at the right moment, combined with sealing,
makes a real difference and is worth paying for.

**Where it is not:** a handful of beetles on a windowsill in February. That is
a vacuum cleaner and five minutes, and any company quoting a treatment program
for it is selling you a schedule rather than a solution.

**Where the answer is neither:** if what you actually want is for them to stop
landing on the wall, nothing achieves that. They are orienting to a large
sun-facing surface, and the surface is your house.

## Around this county

This is one of the two insects the owner of this company names when asked what
autumn looks like here, the other being the brown marmorated stink bug, and the
two arrive on the same walls within days of each other.

The pressure is heaviest where there is a lot of open ground producing them and
a building standing exposed to the afternoon sun — which describes most of the
agricultural edge of Whatcom County. Properties around Lynden, Everson,
Nooksack, Sumas and out along both forks of the Nooksack toward Deming and Acme
get it hardest. A sheltered street in the middle of Bellingham gets noticeably
less.

Aspect matters more than address. Two houses on the same street can have
completely different experiences of this insect depending on which way the long
elevation faces and how much sun it holds into the afternoon.

## What it is doing the rest of the year

Worth saying, because it changes how people feel about the insect and because
it is true: for eight months of the year this beetle is doing something useful.

It is a predator, and an effective one. Adults and larvae both eat aphids in
substantial numbers, along with scale insects and other soft-bodied pests, and
it is a significant natural control in gardens, orchards and crops across the
region. UC's Statewide IPM Program lists it in its natural enemies gallery
rather than its pest gallery, which tells you how it is regarded when it is not
on somebody's ceiling.

It was brought here deliberately for exactly that reason. The overwintering
behavior came with it, and that is the whole of the complaint.

None of which helps in October. But it is the reason we are not going to
recommend anybody try to eliminate them from their property, and the reason the
work is about the building rather than about the insect.

## The short version

It is not the ladybug you remember; it is an introduced relative that
overwinters in buildings. It gathers on sun-facing walls in early October and
gets in through ordinary gaps. It does not breed indoors, eat anything, or
damage the structure.

Unlike native ladybugs it will bite, and it stains surfaces yellow if disturbed
or crushed, so vacuum rather than squash. Heavy indoor exposure can cause
allergic reactions in some people, and a pet that eats a mouthful can get mouth
irritation.

Do not let anybody spray the aggregation inside your walls — the carcasses feed
carpet beetles, which are a worse problem than the beetles were.

What works is sealing the openings, and exterior treatment timed to late
September or early October, before they arrive.

## One more note on timing

The window for exterior work is genuinely narrow and it moves with the weather
rather than the date, so it is worth booking on a forecast rather than on a
calendar. The trigger is the first run of cold nights followed by a warm, still
afternoon, which is when the beetles gather on sunlit walls in numbers. Treat
before that and the residual has weathered; treat after it and most of the
population is already in the building.
