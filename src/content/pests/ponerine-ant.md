## The ant you get stung by without seeing

Most ant problems announce themselves. There is a trail, or a mound, or a
scattering of soil on a patio, and the insect responsible is available for
inspection.

This one is different, and the way it presents is the reason it has a page.

Somebody is stung indoors — often in a basement, a ground-floor room or a
utility space, often near a window or a light fitting. The sting hurts more than
it should for something that small. They look for what did it and find, at most,
a few tiny winged insects at the glass. There is no trail, no nest, no
accumulating population, and nothing that behaves like an infestation.

That combination is close to diagnostic for a ponerine ant.

## What is actually happening

The colony is underground, outside the living space, very often beneath a
concrete slab — a basement floor, a garage, a patio, a path, the pad under an
addition.

The workers stay there. They are small, they forage in soil, and they have no
reason to come up into a room. Almost nobody ever sees one.

What does come up is **winged females**, emerging from the nest and drawn toward
light. Where the slab meets a wall, where a service pipe passes through, at an
expansion joint or a crack, they find a way into the building and head for the
brightest thing in it.

So the insect a householder encounters is a reproductive on a one-way trip, not
a forager from an established indoor population. That is why nothing is found
when people go looking, and why the problem often seems to resolve itself and
then recur.

## Recognizing them

Small and unremarkable, which is part of the difficulty.

**Size and color.** Two to three millimeters, yellow to brown, monomorphic —
every worker the same.

**A thick, broad node** in the waist, which is the structural feature the
extension key uses.

**A sting.** This is the character that matters most practically. Many ants in
this region either have no functional sting or one that cannot penetrate skin.
This one stings, and it is described in the regional guidance as quite painful.

**Winged females indoors near lights**, in the absence of any trail. Honestly,
in this county the circumstances identify the species more reliably than the
insect does.

## Why the sting is the whole story

An ant sting at this size ought to be nothing, and this one is not.

The practical consequences are worth being clear about. For most people it is a
sharp pain out of proportion to the insect, a small welt, and then it is over
within a day.

The concern the extension guidance raises is allergic response — a pronounced
welt, or in a sensitive individual respiratory symptoms. That is the same
caution that attaches to wasp stings and it deserves the same seriousness.
Anybody who carries an adrenaline autoinjector for insect stings should treat
this as one of the things it is for.

What it is not is dangerous to a typical person, and nothing on this page should
be read as saying otherwise. It is a small painful event that people find
alarming largely because they cannot identify what caused it.

## The slab problem

Here is the honest difficulty, and it is the reason this species has a
reputation among people who do this work.

The nest is under concrete. There is no access to it, no way to place a bait
where the colony will find it reliably, and no structural repair that reaches
it. The extension guidance says as much directly: nests under concrete slabs are
problematic because they are difficult to treat.

That means the realistic approach is usually not "find and destroy the colony".
It is:

**Close the route into the building.** Where the emerging females are getting
through — a crack at a slab edge, a gap around a service penetration, an
expansion joint, the junction between slab and wall plate — that is a sealing
job, and it is the one intervention that reliably reduces what reaches the
living space.

**Manage the light.** They are coming toward it. Reducing bright interior light
visible from ground level during the emergence period, and shifting exterior
lighting to warmer, lower, more directed fittings, changes how many arrive.

**Deal with what is there.** The insects that get in are a small number of
short-lived reproductives, and a vacuum handles them without any product at all.

**Treat only where treatment can reach.** Where there is genuine access — a
crack that can be treated, soil at a slab edge, a void that connects — targeted
work has a place. A general perimeter spray on a foundation does very little
about a colony under a floor.

## Why potted plants get a mention

The extension guidance includes a specific and slightly unexpected piece of
advice: inspect potted plants for the possible introduction of these ants into a
residential area.

That is worth taking seriously, because it is how a subtropical species ends up
established in a northern city in the first place. A pot is a portable piece of
soil with a colony in it, moved from a warm nursery or a warm region into a
building or a garden that would not otherwise support the species.

Practically, for a household: look at the root ball and the drainage holes of
anything coming in, particularly from a heated greenhouse or a supplier further
south. For a nursery or a business receiving plants, that is a receiving
procedure rather than a treatment, and it is the same advice given on our ghost
ant page for the same reason.

## Telling them apart from the small ants that matter more

Four small indoor ants get reported similarly and the responses differ.

**Thief ant** — 1.3 to 1.8 mm, yellowish, no meaningful sting, forages in a
visible trail to a food source. If there is a trail on a counter it is very
likely this rather than a ponerine ant.

**Pharaoh ant** — about 2 mm, yellow, many queens, budding, and the one where
spraying makes things worse. Also foraging in trails.

**Moisture ant** — small and yellow, and a report about wet decaying wood rather
than a pest in its own right.

**Ponerine ant** — no trail, no foraging indoors, winged females at lights, and a
sting that hurts.

The presence or absence of a foraging trail does most of the sorting here. An
ant problem with no trail is unusual, and it points at this.

## Around this county

The species is established in urban areas across the Pacific Northwest, which
includes ours, and the circumstances that produce a call are specific.

Ground-floor and basement rooms over slabs. Garages and utility spaces. Buildings
where a slab was poured against an existing structure. Newer construction on
slab-on-grade rather than crawlspace, which is less common in this county than
in many but is not rare.

The seasonal note is that emergence is when it happens, and a household may get
several incidents across a short period and then nothing for a long time. That
pattern — a cluster of stings with no visible cause, then silence — is what
brings people to a page like this one months later when it happens again.

## What we would actually do

**Establish what stung you**, which usually means the circumstances rather than a
specimen, because the insect is rarely retained. Where there is a specimen, it
settles it quickly.

**Find where they are entering.** Slab edges, expansion joints, service
penetrations, the base of a wall in a basement. This is the productive part of
the visit.

**Look at the lighting**, because it is a genuine lever and it costs nothing.

**Say plainly what treatment can and cannot reach.** A colony under a slab is not
something we can promise to eliminate, and we will not sell a program built on
implying otherwise.

**Seal.** Which is exclusion work, it is permanent, and it is the thing that
actually reduces the stings.

## What we will not sell you

We will not promise to eliminate a colony under a concrete slab. It may be
possible where there is access; where there is not, saying so is the only
honest position, and a recurring treatment sold against an unreachable nest is a
subscription rather than a solution.

We will not treat a whole house for a handful of winged insects at a window when
the answer is a sealed joint and a vacuum.

We will not dismiss the sting either. It is a real sting, it is genuinely
painful, and somebody reporting it deserves to be told what it probably was
rather than being told ants do not sting.

And where the answer turns out to be a crack at the edge of a garage slab and a
different bulb by the back door, that is what we will tell you.

## Why this species is hard to name for a customer

There is an awkwardness in explaining this ant to somebody who has just been
stung, and it is worth acknowledging.

The genus has no useful common name. "Ponerine ant" is a subfamily label rather
than a species name — it describes a whole group of ants worldwide, most of
which have nothing to do with buildings. The regional key uses it because
*Hypoponera punctatissima* is the only member of that group anybody here is
likely to meet indoors.

So a technician saying "it was probably a ponerine ant" is giving an accurate
answer that sounds evasive, and a customer searching for it finds mostly
entomological material about tropical predatory ants.

That is part of why this page exists in the form it does. The circumstances —
tiny winged insect, indoors, at a light, painful sting, no trail, no visible
colony — are more useful to a householder than the name, and somebody who
recognizes the circumstances has effectively identified it.

## What makes them different from every other ant here

Two things, and both run against the pattern the rest of this library
establishes.

**No foraging trail.** Almost everything else in these pages is diagnosed partly
by the trail: where it enters, where it leads, how thick it is, whether it
persists. This species gives you none of that. The workers are underground and
they stay there.

**The reproductives are the problem, not the workers.** For every other ant on
this site, winged forms are an event and the workers are the issue. Here it is
reversed. Nobody complains about the workers because nobody sees them; the
entire customer experience of this insect is the winged females.

That inversion is why the standard approach — find the trail, bait it, follow it
back — does not apply, and why a technician working from habit will look for
things that are not there and conclude the customer imagined it.

## What to do in the moment

If winged insects are emerging into a room:

**Vacuum them.** They are short-lived, there are not many, and a vacuum handles
them without applying anything in a living space. Empty it outside.

**Do not spray the room.** There is no population in the room to suppress. The
insects present are arriving from below and will keep arriving until the route
is closed, and a treated surface achieves nothing against that.

**Note where they are appearing.** The concentration point is the clue. Two feet
of baseboard, one corner of a garage, the base of a particular wall — that is
where the route is, and it is the only durable thing to act on.

**Turn the light off.** Genuinely. If they are coming to a light, removing the
attractant for the evening reduces the number that make the trip.

**Keep one if you can.** In a bag, under tape, in a container. It settles the
identification and it costs nothing.

## Slab-on-grade, and why this is a construction question

The reason this species has a page rather than a paragraph is that the buildings
it exploits are a recognizable category, and knowing that lets somebody predict
the problem rather than react to it.

**Slab-on-grade construction.** A concrete floor poured directly on prepared
ground, with no crawlspace beneath it. Common in garages everywhere, and in this
county in newer construction, additions, shops and outbuildings more than in the
older housing stock.

**Additions and extensions.** A slab poured up against an existing building
creates a joint, and that joint is a route from the soil beneath into the
structure above. It is one of the more reliable places to find the entry.

**Patios and paths against a wall.** The same joint, outside rather than inside.

**Anywhere with an expansion joint** that has opened, or a service penetration
that was cored and never sealed.

For a homeowner none of that is worth acting on speculatively. For somebody
building, extending or having a slab poured, sealing the perimeter joint
properly at the time costs almost nothing and closes the route before it exists.

## What the emergence season looks like

The pattern people report is a cluster rather than a season, and it is worth
setting expectations about it.

Winged females emerge over a short period. A household may see them for several
consecutive evenings, or on and off across a couple of weeks, and then nothing
at all for a long time. Somebody stung in that window frequently gets stung more
than once before it stops.

Then it stops, and because it stops without anybody doing anything, the whole
episode gets forgotten — until it happens again, at which point the household is
starting from scratch on the identification.

That is why we would rather somebody kept a specimen the first time. It costs
nothing, and it turns a recurring mystery into a known thing with a known
answer.

## What a visit costs and what it should produce

We would rather set the expectation for this one in advance, because it is a
species where a customer can easily pay for a result nobody can deliver.

The assessment is free, as it is for everything except bed bug verification. On
this species that visit is mostly investigation: confirming what stung
somebody, working out where the insects are entering, and looking at the slab
edges, joints and penetrations that could be the route.

What it should produce is a clear answer about the entry point and an honest
statement about the nest. If the colony is under a slab with no access, we will
say that rather than implying it can be eliminated.

What it should not produce is a treatment plan aimed at a colony nobody can
reach. Sealing is the work worth paying for here, and it is a finite job with a
permanent result.

If somebody has already sold you a recurring program for this, the question
worth asking is what exactly is being treated each visit, and where the nest was
determined to be.

## The short version

Tiny winged insects indoors, at a window or a light, in a room over a slab. A
sting that hurts far more than the size of the thing suggests. No trail, no
foraging, nothing found when anybody goes looking.

The colony is outside and underneath, very probably under concrete, and it is
not reachable. The insects reaching you are short-lived reproductives on a
one-way trip toward the light.

Vacuum what is there. Find and seal the joint or penetration they are coming
through. Change what is attracting them at night. Keep one in a bag so the next
time it happens nobody has to work it out from scratch.

And if somebody offers to eliminate the colony, ask where they have determined
it to be.
