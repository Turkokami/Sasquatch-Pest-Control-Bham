## The ant nobody names correctly

There is a good chance that if you have this insect, somebody has already told
you that you have carpenter ants.

It is an easy mistake and an old one. This is a big, dark, wood-associated ant
that turns up in the same places, moves into the same buildings, and produces
the same alarm. Almost all the pest writing available to a homeowner in this
region is about carpenter ants, so that is the answer that gets reached for.
Ours is one of very few pages that treats the velvety tree ant as a species in
its own right rather than a footnote, and the reason is that treatment aimed at
the wrong ant costs a season.

The identification is not hard once somebody tells you what to look at. It just
requires knowing that there is a second answer available.

## The three-part body

Start with color, because this is the one feature that settles it across a room.

Utah State Extension's description is a brownish-black head, a yellowish-red
thorax, and a velvety black abdomen. Held on a fingertip in decent light, the
insect reads as banded: dark at the front, distinctly rust or brick in the
middle, dark again at the back. Nothing else likely to be walking along a
Whatcom County deck rail has that arrangement.

Compare it against the ant it gets called. WSU's carpenter ant sheet gives
*Camponotus modoc* as black except for reddish legs. The red on a carpenter ant
is in the legs, not in the body. The red on a velvety tree ant is a band across
the middle of the body, and the legs are unremarkable. Once you have made that
comparison once you will not make it again.

The rest of the structure is genuinely similar to a carpenter ant, which is why
color has to do the work. Utah State records a single node on the waist and an
evenly rounded thorax — the same smooth unbroken arc that separates carpenter
ants from moisture ants. Both genera are polymorphic, so a colony produces
workers of several sizes from the same queen and a column will contain small
individuals and large ones together. The Pacific Northwest handbook puts the
regional Liometopum species between about an eighth and a quarter of an inch,
which overlaps the lower half of the carpenter ant range and is another reason
size alone is not a check.

## The velvet in the name

The abdomen is covered in very fine hairs, which Utah State names directly and
which the Pacific Northwest handbook describes as a glistening abdomen composed
of dense, fine hair.

In practice, on a live ant in sunlight, the rear section has a soft matte sheen
rather than the hard gloss of many ants. It is subtle and it is not the check to
lead with, but it is real, and under a phone camera at full zoom on a specimen
held still it is obvious. It is also where the common name comes from, which
helps people remember the species exists.

## The smell

This is the fast confirmation and it takes ten seconds.

Crush several workers between finger and thumb and smell them. The Pacific
Northwest handbook records that when crushed they give off a distinct odor
similar to that of an odorous house ant. That means sharp, sour and organic —
the coconut-and-blue-cheese register described on the
[odorous house ant](/pest-library/odorous-house-ant/) page rather than the faint
vinegar of formic acid you get from a crushed carpenter ant.

Use several ants rather than one. A single small worker does not release enough
to register, and people who test with one conclude the smell is absent.

There is a larger-scale version of this that matters on structural jobs. Utah
State notes that structural infestations result in a foul-smelling odor. Where
a substantial colony is established in a wall void, the room can carry a faint
persistent smell that nobody can place and that does not correspond to anything
stored there. It is worth mentioning to us if you have noticed it, because it
narrows the search to a wall rather than a building.

## The four checks that separate it from a carpenter ant

Any one of these is usually enough. Together they are conclusive.

**Color banding.** Three tones along the body against a uniformly black ant with
red legs.

**Smell.** Sharp and sour against faint or nothing.

**The excavation.** Carpenter ant workings are clean, smooth-sided galleries
with a heap of coarse fibrous frass below the opening, often mixed with insect
parts. Velvety tree ants mine wood less extensively, per the Pacific Northwest
handbook, so the workings are shallower, less regular and produce far less debris
for you to find.

**The insulation.** The Pacific Northwest handbook records that this species
also infests foam insulation. Tunneling in rigid foam board, in the foam sleeve
around a line set, or in sprayed material at a rim joist is not carpenter ant
behavior and is a strong pointer toward this genus.

There is a fifth, weaker tell that is worth knowing about because people offer
it and it does not work. Aggression is not diagnostic on its own. Both species
will defend a nest, and a homeowner who got bitten opening a wall has learned
nothing about which ant did it.

## Where it lives outdoors

The species is named for trees and it means it.

Utah State puts the nests in the hollows of trees, stumps and logs. That covers
a great deal of what a Whatcom County property has going on around its edges: a
big-leaf maple with a rot cavity where a limb came off, a cedar stump left from
a clearing in the seventies, an alder that died standing along a creek margin, a
cottonwood with a hollow butt, a fallen log at the back of a lot, a fence post
with a soft heart, a stack of rounds that has been waiting three years to be
split.

Two features of that habitat matter for buildings.

The first is that the nest sites are often in living trees. A tree can be
entirely healthy in every visible respect and still carry a dead cavity that
holds an established colony. That means removing dead wood from a property does
not necessarily remove the source, and it means the tree you are least worried
about can be the one supplying the building.

The second is the distance. The Pacific Northwest handbook records these ants
nesting primarily outdoors and running trails extending several hundred meters
to reach structures. That is a considerably wider radius than most people
picture, and much wider than the thirty-foot world a pavement ant occupies. The
tree that matters is not necessarily in your yard, on your lot line, or anywhere
you would have thought to look.

They travel those distances the way ants do — along edges. Fence rails, retaining
walls, irrigation lines, utility drops, the seam where a driveway meets grass,
and above all any branch or vine touching the building. A limb resting on a roof
is a bridge that skips every ground-level measure anyone has taken.

## How it gets into a building, and where it goes

Utah State describes indoor nests as temporary sites built for foraging, sited
in wall voids and areas with high moisture such as water leaks, and also under
loose bark and rocks and in or under insulation.

The practical list on housing here comes out looking a lot like a carpenter ant
list, which is exactly why the two get confused:

**Wall voids on the wet side of a building**, especially where a downspout has
been discharging against the wall for several winters.

**Rim joists and sill plates** over a crawlspace with bare soil.

**Around windows and doors** at the bottom corners, where a replacement unit
went into an old opening and the flashing behind it was never corrected.

**Boxed soffits and roof edges**, downstream of a gutter that overflows.

**Insulation**, which is the divergence from carpenter ants worth remembering.
Foam board at a rim joist, foam sleeving on a heat pump line set, and rigid
insulation in a crawlspace are all materials this ant will tunnel in. A gallery
in foam is easy to miss and produces almost no debris.

**Attached garages and outbuildings**, particularly with firewood stacked
against a shared wall — which on this species is close to putting the nest in
the building deliberately.

## Many queens, many nests

Here is the structural fact that decides how much work a job is.

The Pacific Northwest handbook states plainly that a colony of these ants may
have many queens and numerous nest sites. That is a different animal from the
pavement ant, which runs one queen in one place, and it changes the arithmetic
in the way a polygyne species always does.

It means the nest you find is unlikely to be the whole population. It means a
treatment that resolves one site can be followed by activity from another six
weeks later without anything having gone wrong with the work. And it means the
useful question on a first visit is not "where is the nest" but "how many places
is this operating from, and which of them are in the building".

It also means the outdoor half of the job is not optional. A colony network
anchored in a hollow tree three hundred yards away, using a wall void as a
satellite site, is not resolved by anything done inside the wall.

Food is honeydew and insects rather than wood. Utah State lists other insects,
honeydew from aphids, mealybugs and scales, scavenging on dead animals, and a
particular attraction to sweets; the Pacific Northwest handbook says the same in
shorter form. The practical consequence is the same as it is for carpenter ants:
because the ants forage for sugars and protein and share what they collect
through the colony, material placed where they are already feeding can be
carried inward. An insect that genuinely digested cellulose would never do that.

The aphid connection is a real control point and almost nobody mentions it. A
heavy aphid or scale year on the maples, the birches, the roses or the fruit
trees keeps a large population close to the building. Managing that infestation
is managing the ants.

## The trail is better evidence than the frass

With carpenter ants, the heap of coarse shavings below a gallery opening is the
find that organizes the whole job. With this species that evidence is thinner,
because the ants remove less material, and a household waiting to see a frass
pile before taking the problem seriously can wait a long time.

What you get instead is a trail, and it is unusually good evidence because these
ants run long, persistent, well-defined routes rather than wandering. The
Pacific Northwest handbook's observation about trails extending several hundred
meters to reach structures is what makes them followable: a route that long is
established infrastructure, and established infrastructure stays put for weeks.

Three things make it worth working with.

**It runs at night.** Follow it after dark with a flashlight rather than at
midday. The column will be denser, wider and much easier to trace along a fence
rail or a foundation edge than it is in the afternoon.

**It follows edges.** Look along the top of a retaining wall, the seam of a
path, a fence line, a cable run, an irrigation line, and every point where
vegetation touches the building.

**Both ends are informative.** One end will finish at a resource — an aphid
colony on a maple, a bin, a pet bowl. The other will disappear into the
structure or into a cavity in wood, and that is the end that matters.

Leave it alone until somebody has seen it. Spraying a trail on this species
removes the single best piece of diagnostic information the property is offering
and buys about a day of quiet.

## They defend the nest, and it hurts

Utah State's description is unusually blunt for an extension publication: the
species is aggressive, inflicts a painful bite, and sprays secretions onto
intruders when disturbed.

That has practical consequences, and they are the reason this section exists
rather than being folded into a list.

Anyone pulling trim, opening drywall, lifting insulation or cutting into a stump
where this species is nesting should expect a defensive response in numbers and
within seconds. It is not a sting and there is no venom of household
consequence, but a bite from a large worker combined with a sprayed secretion
into the wound is genuinely unpleasant and it is a poor thing to discover while
balanced on a ladder.

It also affects arborists and anyone taking down a tree with a cavity in it. If
you are having work done on a hollow-butted maple or a standing dead alder and
ants have been seen on the trunk, say so to whoever is doing the cutting.

For a household, the sensible version is short: do not open the nest to see what
is in it. Take a photograph, collect a few workers on tape from somewhere they
are already walking, and leave the wall alone.

## Whatcom County, specifically

This is a western species and this county gives it a great deal to work with.

The pattern we see follows the forest edge rather than the town center. The
Lake Whatcom shoreline, Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, the wooded lots on the
Chuckanut side, the properties backing onto greenbelt in Edgemoor and Alabama
Hill, the rural country toward Deming, Acme and the South Fork valley, and the
second homes along Birch Bay and Semiahmoo that sit empty for months. What those
have in common is standing dead wood, big deciduous trees with cavities, and
buildings tucked into the middle of it.

The climate contributes in the way it contributes to everything else here.
Without a hard freeze, and with wood that stays damp from October through April,
the dead-wood resource this species depends on never gets scarce and never dries
out. A cedar stump in this county rots slowly and stays hospitable for decades.

The seasonal shape is close to the carpenter ant's. Activity builds through
spring, peaks in the warm months, runs at night more than by day, and falls away
from November. What that means for diagnosis is the same as it means there:
large ants moving about indoors in the cold months are living indoors rather
than visiting.

## Which Liometopum you actually have

Worth saying, because the honest answer is that it is sometimes not settled in
the field.

The Pacific Northwest handbook records two species in the region — the red and
black one described on this page, and an entirely dark brown species commonly
called the pine tree ant. Both nest in wood, both trail long distances, both
smell when crushed, and both are managed the same way.

If you have a uniformly dark brown wood-nesting ant with the right smell and the
right habits, the identification may be the second species rather than the
first. It does not change what needs doing. It does change what you should
believe if you go reading, because most of the published photographs are of the
banded one.

We would rather record that ambiguity here than pretend a field call is more
precise than it is.

## Why the verdict on this page is "treat"

Three reasons, and they are the same reasons carpenter ants get the same
verdict.

**It nests in the structure.** Utah State and the Pacific Northwest handbook
both describe indoor nesting in wall voids and insulation. That is not an insect
passing through a kitchen on the way to somewhere else.

**It does damage, slowly.** Less than a carpenter ant does, and slower, but
tunneling in framing and insulation is still material being removed from an
assembly, and it concentrates in exactly the damp members where decay is already
running.

**It does not resolve on its own.** The population is anchored in a resource
outdoors that will still be there next year, and nothing about the seasons ends
it. Waiting improves no variable.

The qualification we owe you is that a colony working a stump at the back of a
lot, with nothing coming into the building, is a different situation and it
often needs nothing. Ants on the woodpile are not a structural finding. The
verdict on this page is about ants in the structure.

## What competent work involves

**Identify properly, and under magnification if the specimen is ambiguous.**
This is the whole reason the page exists. The response for this species overlaps
with the carpenter ant response and diverges from the odorous house ant response
almost completely.

**Look for nests rather than trails.** Sounding suspect timber, moisture
readings, checking foam and rigid insulation for tunneling, and following trails
at night, since that is when they run.

**Work the trees.** The Pacific Northwest handbook's advice is to remove dead
branches near the home, and the wider version of that is to survey the standing
dead wood, the hollow trunks and the stumps within a radius that is far larger
than most people expect. Where a nest tree is on land we cannot enter, the
honest description of the work changes to keeping a building defended against
pressure from a source we do not control, and we will say so rather than imply
otherwise.

**Cut the bridges.** Branches touching the roof or the walls, ivy on siding,
firewood stacked against the building.

**Correct the water.** Wall voids get chosen because they are damp. Gutters,
downspouts, flashing, grade and crawlspace ground cover all matter more than
anything applied to the ants.

**Use non-repellent materials and baits where they fit.** The Pacific Northwest
handbook records that treatments similar to those used for carpenter ants have
been effective against this species, and it is direct elsewhere that
over-the-counter sprays are highly repellent to ants and can scatter them
through a structure. Contact killers on a visible trail remove the members the
colony can most easily spare.

**Treat at both ends where the network is known**, and expect more than one
site.

**Come back and check.** Activity commonly rises for a period after baiting
begins, because recruitment to the material is the mechanism working rather than
failing.

## Telling it from the other two it gets confused with

**Carpenter ants** are covered above. The color banding and the smell settle it
in under a minute, and the state of the excavation confirms it. The reason to
care is that the two do not get the same treatment plan and a carpenter ant
program aimed at a Liometopum network with several outdoor nest sites will
under-deliver.

**Odorous house ants** share the smell and nothing else that matters. They are
small — around three millimeters, all workers the same size — brown-black
throughout with no banding, and they carry a single flattened node hidden
beneath the abdomen. They damage nothing structurally. If the ants are uniform,
tiny and running a dense two-way column across a counter, that is the answer and
this page is not.

**Moisture ants** are small, yellow to amber, and concentrated in wet decaying
timber rather than trailing anywhere. Their thoracic outline carries a notch
where both this species and the carpenter ant carry an unbroken curve. Finding
them is a statement about water rather than about ants.

## What we will not tell you

**That it is definitely a carpenter ant.** Where the specimen is ambiguous we
will say so and get it looked at rather than picking the familiar answer.

**That one visit resolves a polygyne network.** A colony with many queens and
numerous nest sites is a piece of work with follow-up in it, and anybody
describing it otherwise is describing an outcome they do not control.

**That the tree has to come down.** Sometimes removing a hollow stump is the
highest-value thing on the job. Sometimes the nest is in a mature maple that is
structurally sound, worth keeping, and better managed than felled. That decision
belongs to an arborist and to you, not to a pest control company that would
rather the ants were gone.
