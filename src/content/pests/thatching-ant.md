## What you are looking at

A low dome in a sunny part of the yard, built out of dried grass, conifer
needles and small stems rather than soil, with large ants working over the
surface. The mound may be a foot across or it may be waist high. It is usually
in an open spot that gets sun, on a bank, at a woodland edge, along a fence
line, or in rough ground beside a driveway.

That is a thatching ant nest. The ants are *Formica*, the guide the owner of
this company works from lists them among the twelve key ant pests of this
region, and they are one of the more misunderstood insects we get called about.

They rarely have anything to do with your house. What they do have is a bite
that people remember.

## Recognizing them

PNW 624, the WSU, OSU and University of Idaho extension key for this region,
gives the identification in a few lines and they are worth quoting closely
because the details are what separate this ant from the one people fear.

**Size and build.** Large ants, 4 to 9 millimeters, and **polymorphic** —
meaning the workers in one colony come in a range of sizes rather than all
matching. A trail with obvious big and small workers in it is a useful early
clue.

**Color.** Many species here are **bi-colored, red and black**. Some are
uniformly brown or black, which is where the confusion with carpenter ants
begins.

**The thorax.** This is the one that settles it. Thatching ants possess **a
notch or depression on top of the thorax**. Viewed from the side, the upper
profile dips. A carpenter ant's is evenly rounded and smooth.

**The antennae.** In the extension key, *Formica* keys out on antennae inserted
close to the margin of the clypeus — the plate at the front of the head. That is
a hand-lens character rather than a driveway one, but it is what a specimen gets
identified on.

## The mound, and what it is for

The thatch is the defining behavior. The ants gather plant material — most often
grass, and needles where there are conifers — and pile it over a nest excavated
in soil, or over a rotten log.

It is not decoration. The thatch sheds rain, holds heat, and lets the colony
regulate temperature by moving brood up and down through it as the sun moves.
Ants can be seen rearranging the surface constantly, which is why a mound never
looks settled.

Colonies frequently have **multiple queens**, which matters for two practical
reasons. A colony with many queens is not going to collapse because one is
killed, and colonies with multiple queens can be very long-lived and very large.
The extension literature notes a *Formica* colony in Japan estimated at 348
million workers spread over many acres — an extreme case, and a useful sense of
what this genus is capable of.

Nests also occur without an obvious mound, in soil or in rotten logs, which is
why not every thatching ant complaint comes with a dome to point at.

## The bite, which is the actual complaint

Almost every call we get about these ants is really a call about being bitten,
and the mechanism is worth explaining because it is unusual.

Thatching ants **do not sting**. They have no sting to use. What they do is bite
with the mandibles and then curl the abdomen forward and spray **formic acid**
into the wound they have just made.

PNW 624 describes exactly that: they can bite quite hard and usually spray the
area they have bitten with formic acid to produce a painful sensation.

The result feels like a sting, which is why people report it as one. It burns
out of proportion to the size of the mark, and it happens when somebody kneels
in a bed, sits on a bank, or puts a hand down near a mound without noticing it.

This is not a medical emergency and it is not comparable to a yellowjacket
attack. It is, however, genuinely painful, and it is the reason a mound in the
wrong place is worth doing something about.

## Where they actually live, and why not your walls

The extension guidance is explicit that these ants **rarely nest in homes**, and
that single sentence resolves most of the anxiety we hear.

They nest in soil and in rotten logs, in open sunny ground, at edges rather than
in the middle of dense forest or the middle of a mown lawn. What brings them
into contact with people is foraging and swarming rather than nesting.

They come indoors after sweet material. They are attracted to the same kitchen
spills and pet food as anything else, and a trail into a house is a foraging
trail from a nest outside it.

Reproductive swarms come in **late summer to early fall**, later than the
carpenter ant flights people are used to, and winged ants indoors at that time of
year are usually reproductives that wandered in rather than proof of a nest in
the structure.

## Telling them from what actually damages buildings

This is where the money is, because the two ants get confused and the
consequences are entirely different.

**Carpenter ants** excavate galleries in wood and can genuinely damage a
structure over years. Our common one is large and essentially black, with a
smooth, evenly rounded thoracic profile. A carpenter ant problem is a moisture
and structure problem, and it deserves the full treatment described on its own
page.

**Thatching ants** build a mound outdoors, do not tunnel in sound structural
wood, and rarely nest in a house at all. The thoracic notch and the bi-colored
red-and-black pattern are your field marks.

**Moisture ants** are smaller, usually yellow, and are a report about wet
decaying wood rather than a pest problem in themselves. The extension key
separates them from thatching ants on a round epinotal spiracle, which is a
microscope character — in practice the size and color difference does it.

**Velvety tree ants** are dark with a reddish thorax, smell strongly when
crushed, and nest in trees and occasionally in structures.

Getting this right in the first minute changes everything that follows. We would
rather look at a specimen or a clear photograph than treat on a description.

## Aphids, honeydew, and the reason they are on your plants

If the complaint is ants streaming up a shrub or a fruit tree, the ants are the
symptom.

Thatching ants **attend aphids for honeydew** — they protect aphid colonies from
predators and parasites in exchange for the sugary excretion. A heavy ant trail
into a plant almost always means a well-established aphid population being
farmed.

Two things follow. Spraying the ants off the plant achieves nothing durable,
because the aphids are still there and the colony will send more. And dealing
with the aphids collapses the ant traffic without touching the ants at all.

That is a horticultural job more often than a pest control one, and where that
is the answer we will say so rather than quote for the ants.

## They are predators, and that is worth something

A thatching ant colony takes an enormous number of other insects. In forestry
literature these ants are treated as beneficial for exactly that reason, and
mound-building *Formica* species are protected in parts of Europe on those
grounds.

We are not going to pretend that makes a mound beside a child's swing set
acceptable. But it does mean the default answer to "there is an ant mound in the
back of my property" should be to leave it, and that destroying a large colony
is a real loss rather than a neutral act.

Where the mound is well away from the traffic on a property, our advice is
usually to mark it, tell whoever mows, and let it be.

## When it genuinely warrants action

There are real conflicts and we would rather name them than pretend the answer
is always no.

A mound in a lawn that has to be mowed. A mound beside a path, a patio, a play
area or a sandpit. A mound hard against a foundation, where foraging trails run
into the building constantly. A colony in ground somebody with a known reaction
to insect venom has to cross. Persistent indoor foraging traced to a specific
nest.

Those are situations where the ants and the use of the ground cannot both
continue, and in those cases treatment is warranted and we will carry it out.

What we will not do is treat a mound at the back of two acres because somebody
saw it.

## What we would actually do

Identification first, from a specimen or a good photograph, because the answer
for thatching ants, carpenter ants and moisture ants are three different jobs.

Then location. Where is the nest, where are the trails, and what is the actual
conflict — the mound itself, foraging indoors, or ants farming aphids on a
plant. Those three have three different responses and only one of them involves
the mound.

Where the mound is the problem and it has to go, the work is direct treatment of
the nest rather than a perimeter spray around a house that the ants are not
living in. A residual band on a foundation does very little about a colony
sitting forty feet away in the open.

Where the problem is foraging indoors, the answer is finding the trail, closing
the entry it uses, and baiting rather than spraying the trail — a sprayed trail
frequently splits a colony and makes the following month worse.

Where the problem is aphids, we will tell you it is aphids.

## Around this county

Thatching ants are ordinary here without being a common service call, and the
pattern follows the terrain rather than the town.

We see them on rural and forest-edge property — the places already described on
our Deming, Acme and Meadowview pages, where standing timber meets open ground
and there are sunny banks and rough margins for a mound to sit in. Cleared land
with regrowth, powerline corridors, gravel pits and old logging edges all suit
them.

In town they turn up on properties backing onto greenbelt, along the sunnier
edges of a lot, and occasionally in a neglected corner that has been left to
rough grass.

What we very seldom see is a thatching ant problem inside a house, and that is
consistent with what the regional guidance says to expect.

## Living with a mound

For anybody who would rather keep it, which is a reasonable position:

Mark it so it does not get hit with a mower — a cane, a flag, an upturned pot.
Tell whoever cuts the grass. Keep the immediate area out of the routine path
across the property, and warn anybody gardening nearby.

Do not disturb the surface for fun. A poked mound produces a great many
defensive ants very quickly, and the bite is the reason this page exists.

And take the aphid trails as information rather than as a second problem. A
colony working a shrub is telling you the shrub has aphids.

## What we will not sell you

We will not treat a thatching ant mound that is not in anybody's way. It is a
predatory colony doing useful work, and killing one to produce an invoice is not
something we are prepared to do.

We will not sell a recurring perimeter program for an ant that rarely nests in
buildings. If the foraging is genuinely persistent, the answer is the trail and
the entry point, and that finishes.

We will not call a thatching ant a carpenter ant. The words are worth money in
this trade, which is exactly why they should be used accurately, and the
difference is visible in profile to anybody with a hand lens.

And if what you actually have is aphids on a plum tree, we will say so, and it
will not cost you anything.

## Hitting a mound with machinery

The situation that turns a harmless colony into an afternoon nobody enjoys is
mechanical, and it is the same story we tell about ground-nesting wasps for a
different reason.

A mower, a strimmer or a tractor across a thatch mound opens it, and the
response is immediate and coordinated. Thousands of workers come out, they climb
whatever is in reach, and the bite-and-spray described above happens repeatedly
rather than once. Anybody in shorts on a ride-on mower has a bad few minutes.

The prevention costs nothing. Find the mounds before the first cut of the
season, mark them with a cane or a flag, and tell whoever does the mowing where
they are. On a property where the work is contracted out, say it at the start of
the year rather than after somebody has found one the hard way.

Where a mound genuinely sits in ground that has to be mown, that is a real
conflict and one of the cases where treatment is the right answer. But it is
worth establishing that it truly cannot be mown around before deciding.

## Mounds on farm ground and open acreage

On rural property the calculation is different again, and usually easier.

Pasture, field margins, fence lines and the rough ground around outbuildings are
where mounds turn up on the farms and acreages we work across this county. Most
of them are nowhere near anybody, most of them are doing useful predatory work
on the insects around a crop, and most of them warrant nothing at all.

Where they matter on farm ground is narrower than people expect: a mound in
ground that gets worked, a mound where stock congregate, or a colony farming
aphids on something being grown for sale. Those are specific problems with
specific answers rather than a reason to treat a field.

The other rural note is that thatching ant mounds are frequently mistaken for
something worse at a distance. A dome of dried plant material in a pasture looks
alarming if you are expecting a nest. Walk up to it in daylight, look at what it
is made of, and the identification is usually done from two feet away without
anything being disturbed.

## What a mound tells you about the ground

One last thing worth taking from a thatching ant mound, since it is on your
property whether or not you do anything about it.

The mound is a fair indicator of what that patch of ground is like. These ants
want sun, drainage and a spot that stays warm — the same conditions that suit a
dry border, a bank that will not hold turf, or the strip beside a driveway that
never quite grows grass properly.

So a mound in a lawn is frequently sitting in the part of the lawn that was
struggling anyway. If the ants are being removed and the ground restored, that
is the moment to deal with the drainage or the shade problem that made the spot
attractive, rather than laying turf over it and finding another colony there in
three years.
