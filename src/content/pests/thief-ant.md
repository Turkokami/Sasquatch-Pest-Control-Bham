## The ant people are not sure is an ant

A great many thief ant calls start the same way. Somebody has noticed
something moving on a counter, or a fine line of specks along the edge of a
worktop, and they are not certain whether they are looking at insects or at
dirt.

At 1.3 to 1.8 millimeters, thief ants are among the very smallest ants in the
Pacific Northwest. They are small enough that the trail is usually seen before
any individual is, and small enough that they get through gaps nobody thinks of
as gaps.

They are native, they are common, and they are the ant most often confused with
the one species where confusion is expensive.

## Recognizing them

**Size.** 1.3 to 1.8 mm, monomorphic — every worker the same.

**Color.** Yellowish or light brown through to dark brown.

**The antennal club.** This is the diagnostic and it is worth learning. The last
segments of the antenna are enlarged into a club, and in the thief ant that club
is **unusually long and made of two segments**. In the pharaoh ant it is three.

That single character separates the two species that matter most in this size
range, and it is why we would rather have three ants in a bag than a photograph
of a counter.

## The name, and what it tells you

Thief ants earn the name literally. They nest beside other ant colonies, tunnel
into them, and take food and brood.

What makes the strategy work is size. The galleries they use are too small for
the host ants to pass through, so the raiders operate with something close to
impunity — inside the walls of a larger colony, feeding on it, unreachable.

That is not a curiosity. It explains where they nest, which is in the fine
cracks and voids adjacent to other things rather than in an open chamber
somebody could find and treat. Indoors it means masonry joints, gaps in
woodwork, and voids that are effectively invisible.

## What they eat

Highly predaceous, and then omnivorous on top of that.

The extension guidance lists honeydew, nuts, grains and a wide range of
household foods alongside the insect prey. In practice the household complaint
is usually greasy or protein foods — a thief ant trail into a pantry very often
leads to something oily rather than something sweet, which is a useful clue when
the smaller ants are being sorted out.

That breadth is also why they persist. An ant that will take insects, seeds,
grease and sugar is not going to be starved out of a building by tidying one
cupboard.

## Where they nest

Outdoors: exposed soil, under stones, boards, and other objects, and in rotting
wood.

Indoors: **the woodwork and masonry of houses** — which is the phrase in the
regional guide and it is an honest description of a difficult problem. Wall
voids, the gap behind a baseboard, mortar joints, the space around a pipe
sleeve, the cavity behind a tiled splashback.

Colonies run from many hundreds to a few thousand workers, which is modest
compared with a pavement ant or a thatching ant colony and large enough to be a
persistent nuisance.

Swarming happens from mid-summer to early autumn, and unlike pharaoh ants they
do produce genuine reproductive flights.

## Why the identification decides the treatment

This is the practical heart of the page.

**If it is a pharaoh ant**, sprays are actively harmful. The colony buds under
chemical stress, splits, and spreads through the building. The job is bait,
patience and no repellents, and it takes weeks.

**If it is a thief ant**, that constraint does not apply in the same way. The
work is still largely bait-led, because the nests are unreachable and a bait
goes where a technician cannot, but the extreme care around repellency is not
the governing consideration it is with pharaoh ants.

Getting it wrong in one direction makes an infestation worse. Getting it wrong
in the other direction means a customer pays for a long, careful, repeat-visit
program they did not need.

Either way, the answer is a specimen and two minutes with a lens, and we do not
charge for that.

## The chickens question

One genuinely specific note, and it is the reason this species gets a mention in
agricultural contexts rather than only domestic ones.

Thief ants serve as an **intermediate host for a poultry tapeworm**. The ant
picks up the parasite, a bird eats the ant, and the cycle continues.

For a household with a few hens — which is a great many properties across
Ferndale, Lynden, Everson and the rural parts of this county — that is worth
knowing. It does not mean a thief ant problem is a poultry emergency. It means
that persistent thief ant activity in and around a coop and feed store is worth
dealing with for a reason beyond nuisance, and that the feed handling advice on
our farm pages applies here too: feed in closed containers, spillage cleaned up,
and a gap kept around the walls so the place can be inspected.

## Around this county

Thief ants are common and under-reported here, largely because of the size. We
suspect a fair number of "tiny ants in the kitchen" calls that get resolved by
somebody cleaning a cupboard were this species.

They turn up in older housing with masonry and lath, in kitchens and bathrooms,
around chimney breasts, and along the junctions of older woodwork — the same
building types described on our downtown Ferndale and downtown Lynden pages,
where a century of alterations has left a great many small voids.

Outdoors they are ordinary in soil and under objects across the whole county,
which is why exclusion at the obvious points matters less with this ant than
with a rodent. A gap that stops a mouse does not stop something under two
millimeters.

## What we would actually do

**Identify first**, from a specimen. The whole point of this page is that the
answer to "which tiny ant" changes the plan.

**Trace the trails** rather than hunting the nest. With an ant this size in
masonry and woodwork, finding the nest is frequently not realistic, and a
treatment plan that depends on finding it is a plan that will fail.

**Bait on the trails**, matched to what they are actually taking — which for
this species is more often protein and grease than sugar, and worth testing
rather than assuming.

**Deal with the competition.** Bait competes with whatever else is available.
Spillage, an open pet food bag, grease behind a cooker and an unsealed bin all
slow the job down.

**Look at what they are raiding.** Where thief ants are indoors in numbers,
there is sometimes another ant colony nearby they are working, and dealing with
that changes the picture.

## What is worth doing yourself

More than with most ants, because the scale is domestic.

Food in sealed containers rather than boxes and bags. Spillage cleaned promptly,
including under and behind appliances where it accumulates unseen. Pet food not
left down overnight. Bins that close.

None of that removes a colony living in a wall. All of it reduces what is on
offer, which makes bait more attractive and the job shorter.

What is not worth doing is buying an aerosol and treating the trail. It kills
the visible workers, leaves the colony untouched, and destroys the trail the
bait needed to travel along.

## What we will not sell you

We will not name an ant this small without looking at one properly. The
description "tiny yellowish ants in the kitchen" fits at least three species with
different answers, and guessing is not identification.

We will not sell a pharaoh ant program for a thief ant. It is longer, more
careful and more expensive than this species requires, and selling the more
elaborate job on an ambiguous identification is exactly the sort of thing this
site exists to argue against.

We will not put a house on a recurring schedule for an ant that a bait and a
tidy cupboard will finish.

And where the honest answer is that the trail leads to a bag of dog food with a
hole in it, we will say so and there will be nothing to pay.

## Why they are hard to exclude

Most of this site argues for exclusion. Seal the gap, end the route, and the
problem does not repeat. With thief ants that argument has a limit and it is
honest to state it.

At 1.3 millimeters an ant passes through openings that no reasonable sealing
program addresses. The gap under a door sweep that stops a mouse is a highway.
The hairline joint between a countertop and a wall, the space where a pipe
passes through a cabinet base, the seam between two pieces of trim, the mortar
joint that has lost a little pointing — all of them are wide open.

So exclusion moves from being the primary tool to being a supporting one. What
it can still do is worth doing: sealing around pipe penetrations under sinks,
closing gaps at the back of cabinetry, caulking the junction where a splashback
meets a wall. Each of those removes a specific route into a specific room.

What it cannot do is make a building ant-proof at that scale, and anybody
promising that has not thought about the arithmetic.

The consequence is that thief ant work leans harder on bait than most ant work
does, because bait travels to where the treatment cannot.

## What a trail is telling you

With an ant this small the trail is the main evidence available, and it repays
attention before anybody treats.

**Where it enters the room** narrows the nest to a direction. A trail emerging
from the junction of a baseboard and the floor is coming from a wall void; one
appearing at the back of a cabinet is coming through the carcass; one at a
window frame may be coming from outside entirely.

**Where it goes** identifies what is attracting them, and with this species it
is frequently protein or grease rather than sugar. A trail leading to a pantry
is straightforward; a trail leading behind an oven is telling you about
accumulated grease nobody can see.

**Whether it persists** distinguishes a foraging line from an outdoor nest from
an indoor colony. An outdoor trail comes and goes with weather and time of day.
An indoor colony runs regardless.

**How thick it is** gives a rough sense of the population, and a very thin
intermittent trail is often a small satellite worth handling before it becomes
anything.

Ten minutes with a torch after dark, when they are most active, gathers more
useful information than any amount of describing them over the phone.

## Outdoors, and the ones that never come in

Most thief ants in this county never enter a building at all, and it is worth
saying so because the species is common in soil across the whole area.

Under a paving slab, under a board left on the ground, in a raised bed, under a
rock, at the edge of a compost heap, in rotting wood — they are simply part of
the fauna, and they are doing genuinely useful work as predators of other
insects and of the eggs and larvae in the soil.

Nothing about a thief ant colony in a garden requires attention. The species
only becomes our business when a colony has established in the fabric of a
building or a foraging trail has found a food source indoors.

That distinction matters because it changes the ambition of the job. We are not
trying to remove thief ants from a property. We are dealing with a specific
trail and a specific nest, and the ones under the patio can stay where they are.

## The confusion that runs the other way

Most of this page is about not mistaking a thief ant for a pharaoh ant. The
reverse mistake happens too, and it is worth a note because the cost falls on
the customer rather than on the outcome.

A pharaoh ant job is slow, careful, bait-led and revisited. It is priced
accordingly, because it genuinely takes that effort. Selling that program to
somebody whose actual problem is a thief ant trail from a nest under the patio
means charging for weeks of work the situation did not require.

We would rather establish the species in the first ten minutes than run an
elaborate program on a guess, and this is one of the places where a company's
incentive and a customer's interest point in slightly different directions. The
more elaborate diagnosis is the more profitable one.

That is exactly why the identification is done from a specimen and why we do not
charge for it. A free two-minute check that sometimes talks us out of the bigger
job is the only version of this that is honest.

## What we would tell a neighbor

Small yellowish ants in the kitchen, no smell when crushed, trail leading to
something greasy: probably this, probably fixable, and worth trying the simple
things for a fortnight before anybody is called.

Containers rather than packets. Everything wiped, including the parts nobody
wipes — under the toaster, behind the kettle, the tray under the cooker. Pet
food up overnight. A bin that shuts.

If that has not shifted it in two weeks, the food source is somewhere you cannot
see or the colony is in the fabric of the building, and that is the point at
which somebody should look at it properly.

Nobody needs to spend money on this in week one, and we would rather say so on a
web page than take a booking that a clean cupboard would have solved.

## Why this ant is under-reported

We suspect thief ants account for a good many kitchen ant complaints that never
reach a pest control company at all, and the reason is the size.

An ant this small does not read as an infestation. It reads as crumbs that move,
or as dirt, or as something wrong with the eyesight. People wipe the counter,
the trail is gone for a day, and the whole episode gets filed as nothing.

That is often the right outcome, and none of this page argues otherwise. Where
it becomes a problem is when the same non-event repeats every few weeks for a
year and nobody connects the instances, because each one on its own was too
small to think about.

If a counter has been wiped clean of the same faint line of specks half a dozen
times since spring, that is a colony, and it is worth ten minutes with a torch
after dark to find out where the line goes.
