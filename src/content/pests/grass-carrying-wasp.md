## What you are actually looking at

Somebody opens a sliding window in late summer and finds the channel packed
with dry grass, cut to length and stuffed in tight. Sometimes there is a
green cricket in there too, not moving. It looks like something went badly
wrong in the window frame.

Nothing went wrong. A single female wasp built a nursery in the most
convenient tube she could find, and the tube happened to be your window.

## The nest, and why the window

Grass-carrying wasps do not excavate. They take over a cavity that already
exists — a hollow plant stem, an old beetle tunnel, a nail hole, a weep hole,
a gap beneath siding, and very often the track of a sliding window or door.
Extension guidance from Ohio State names window wells, weep holes and gaps
beneath siding in the same list, which is a fair description of the average
house.

The grass is the structural material. She cuts long blades, carries them in
with her mandibles, and packs them into the cavity as bedding and as a plug.
The strands sticking out of the opening are simply the ends she could not get
all the way in.

## She is not defending anything

This is the part worth saying plainly, because the shape of a large
thread-waisted wasp near a window sets off the same alarm a yellowjacket does,
and the two could hardly be less alike in temperament.

A yellowjacket nest is a colony with hundreds of workers whose job includes
defense. A grass-carrying wasp is one female working alone. She has no
daughters to call, nothing to lose by leaving, and a sting whose purpose is
subduing crickets. Extension sources describe the genus as beneficial solitary
wasps that rarely, if ever, sting.

## Why we usually tell you to leave it

Ohio State's guidance says management is not required, and that the contents
of a window well can be cleared if they are unsightly or interfere with
opening a window. That is our answer too, and it is the honest one: this is a
cleaning job and, if you want it not to recur, a sealing job.

Where we are useful is the second half. Clearing a track takes a minute.
Working out which cavity she used and closing it — screening a weep hole,
sealing a gap under siding, capping an open tube — is exclusion, and it is the
only thing that stops next August looking the same.

## Recognizing her

She is a thread-waisted wasp: a long, narrow body with a conspicuously slender
stalk between thorax and abdomen, black or very dark, sometimes with a faint
sheen on the wings, and around three quarters of an inch to an inch long. The
waist is the giveaway. Yellowjackets and paper wasps are comparatively thick
through the middle; this one looks as though it were assembled from two parts
joined by a wire.

Two species turn up around houses in this region and the difference does not
change anything you would do. Both behave identically, both use cavities, both
carry grass.

You will usually see her in flight with a blade held beneath her, trailing well
past her body, flying with the labored determination of an insect carrying
something awkward. Watch that once and you will never mistake it for anything
else.

## What she is not

Four insects get confused with this one, and separating them matters because
the correct response to each is different.

**A yellowjacket** is banded bright yellow and black, thick-bodied, and comes
from a colony. It defends. Where one is nesting in a wall void or in the
ground, that is a genuine hazard and it is work we do.

**A European paper wasp** is also banded and is regularly mistaken for a
yellowjacket in its own right. It builds a small open comb of hexagonal cells
under a rail or a window frame — visible papery cells, no grass, no mud.

**A mud dauber** builds with mud rather than grass and provisions with spiders
rather than crickets. Same solitary temperament, entirely different material.
If what is packed into the gap is a hard earthen tube rather than dry
vegetation, you are looking at that instead.

**A great golden digger wasp** is the large orange-legged relative that tunnels
into bare ground and carries katydids. Same family, same solitary habit, no
interest in your window.

The four-way test is quick. Grass means this wasp. Mud means a mud dauber.
Paper cells mean a social wasp. A hole in bare ground with no structure at all
means a digger.

## What she hunts

Tree crickets and young katydids, which explains where she came from and why
she turned up when she did.

She hunts in vegetation, stings the prey to paralyze rather than kill it, and
carries it back to the cavity. Paralysis rather than death is the point: a dead
cricket would decay before the larva finished with it, while a paralyzed one
stays fresh and immobile for as long as it takes. Each cell gets several, an egg
is laid on one of them, and the chamber is closed with more grass.

The practical consequence is that a house beside long grass, an unmown margin,
a hedgerow or a garden with ornamental grasses is far likelier to get her than
a house on a clipped lot with nothing but lawn. She needs the crickets, and the
crickets need the cover.

Nobody should read that as a reason to cut everything down. It is a reason to
understand that the wasp in the window frame is a sign of decent habitat nearby
rather than a sign of anything wrong.

## The year, in this county

She is a summer insect here and the sequence is consistent.

The overwintering generation emerges in early summer as the weather settles.
Through July and August the females hunt, provision and seal cells — this is
when grass appears in tracks and gaps. By late August and September the
provisioning is largely done.

The larvae feed inside their sealed grass chambers, spin cocoons, and pass the
winter in place. The following summer the adults chew out and the cycle
repeats.

That timing is why the grass is usually discovered in autumn, when somebody
opens a window that has been shut all summer, or closes one for the season and
finds it will not run in the track. The wasp has finished. What is left is the
nursery.

## Where the cavities are on a real house

She does not excavate. Everything she uses was already there, and the full list
is worth knowing because closing them is the entire preventive measure.

**Sliding window and door tracks** are the classic, and the reason is
mechanical: a slider has a channel with a covered end and a slot of about the
right size, open all summer, that nobody ever looks into.

**Weep holes** in vinyl and aluminum windows — the small drainage slots along
the bottom of the frame.

**Gaps beneath siding**, particularly at the bottom course, and behind trim
where two materials meet.

**Nail and screw holes** in exposed timber, old bolt holes, and the open ends
of hollow railings and gate posts.

**Hollow plant stems and bamboo**, which is why insect hotels get used
enthusiastically — if you have put one up, this is one of its intended tenants.

**Unused hose reels, furniture tubing, wind chimes and the open end of any pipe
left uncapped**, which between them account for a fair share of the odder
calls.

## Weep holes deserve their own paragraph

Because the wrong fix here causes an actual building problem.

A weep hole drains water out of the bottom of a window frame. Sealing it with
caulk to keep insects out traps water in the frame instead, and a frame holding
water is a rot problem considerably more expensive than a wasp.

The right fix is a screen or a purpose-made weep hole cover that lets water out
and keeps insects from getting in. They cost very little and they are the one
genuine product recommendation on this page.

Anybody who caulks your weep holes to solve a wasp problem has traded a
nuisance for a repair bill.

## What happens if you do nothing

Nothing dramatic, which is the honest answer.

The sealed cells sit through the winter. In early summer the adults chew out,
leave, and do not return to that cavity — they are not colonial, and there is
no accumulating nest the way there is with a paper wasp under an eave. If the
cavity is still open and still suitable, a female may use it again the following
year, but that is a fresh occupant rather than an expanding population.

The grass will sit in the track meanwhile, and in a slider it will keep the
window from running properly. That is the actual cost: a window that does not
close, not a hazard.

## Timing, if you would rather clear it

If the reason for clearing is a window that will not shut, clear it whenever you
need to. It is a minute with a vacuum or a flat blade.

If there is no urgency, the considerate timing is either before she has finished
provisioning, when the cavity is empty and she will simply choose elsewhere, or
after the following summer's adults have emerged. Clearing a sealed cell in
midwinter kills the developing wasps inside it.

We are not going to lecture anybody about that. She is a beneficial predator of
crickets and a native solitary wasp, and given the choice most people would
rather not destroy the nursery for no reason. If you have the choice, that is
the choice.

## What not to do

**Do not spray the track.** A residual insecticide in a window channel puts
product at hand height on a surface people touch, to no purpose — the occupant
is either sealed in a cell the spray will not reach or already gone.

**Do not fill the cavity with expanding foam.** Foam is not a barrier to an
insect that chews, it blocks drainage where the cavity is a weep hole, and it is
extremely difficult to get out of a window frame afterwards.

**Do not take a weep hole cover off to reach the grass and then leave it off.**

**Do not accept a recurring treatment quote for this.** There is no population
to suppress and nothing recurring to prevent except an open hole.

## What the nest actually tells you about your building

This is worth more than the wasp is.

She used a cavity open to the outside and big enough to fly into. On its own
that is harmless. But the same walk that finds her cavity finds the others, and
some of the others matter a great deal more than she does.

A gap under a bottom course of siding that admits a wasp will admit a mouse if
it is a quarter of an inch. An open hollow rail is a wasp nursery this year and
a route into a soffit the next. A weep hole with no cover is fine; a missing
chunk of trim beside it is not.

So when we come out for this, what we are really doing is a perimeter walk with
a specific and useful prompt: something small found a way in, so what else
could. That is where the value of the visit is, and it is why we would rather
look at the whole elevation than clear one track and leave.

## Solitary and social, and why the distinction runs through everything

Nearly every wasp question we are asked comes down to this one fact, and almost
nobody has been told it.

A **social** wasp — yellowjacket, bald-faced hornet, aerial yellowjacket, paper
wasp — lives in a colony with a queen and workers. Workers are expendable and
defense is part of the job. Approach the nest and you are met, because the
colony has something to protect and individual lives are cheap to it.

A **solitary** wasp — grass-carrying wasp, mud dauber, digger wasp — is one
female doing everything herself. No workers, no colony, nothing to gain from a
fight. If she dies, every cell she has provisioned fails. Her sting is a hunting
tool, and spending it on a human is a losing trade she has no instinct to make.

Once somebody has that distinction, most of the alarm drains out of most wasp
sightings and the fear that remains points at the two or three species that
genuinely warrant it. That is a better outcome than treating everything with
wings.

## What a visit for this looks like

We identify what is actually in the cavity first, because grass, mud and paper
mean three different things and we are not going to guess from a photograph
when we could be standing in front of it.

Then we clear it, if clearing is what you want, and check the rest of the
openings on that elevation — the other tracks, the weep holes, the siding line,
any exposed hollow section.

Then we tell you which are worth closing and which are not. Weep holes get
screened, not sealed. Gaps under siding get closed properly. An open rail end
gets a cap. Most of it is small, permanent, mechanical work, and a good share of
it you could do yourself with what is already in the garage. We will say so
where that is true.

## What we will not sell you

We will not sell a treatment program for a solitary wasp with no colony to
suppress and no habit of defending anything.

We will not tell you this is dangerous. It is not, and the fastest way to lose
somebody's trust is to be caught making an insect sound worse than it is.

And we will not clear the grass, take the money and leave the cavity open,
because that guarantees the same call next August and everybody involved knows
it.

## If you have an insect hotel

A fair number of these calls come from people who deliberately put up a bee
hotel and then found something unexpected using it.

That is the structure working. Drilled blocks and bundles of hollow tubes are
built to offer exactly what she needs, and a grass-carrying wasp taking a tube is
the same success as a mason bee taking one. She is a native solitary wasp and a
predator of crickets, and she belongs there.

The one thing worth doing is what any insect hotel needs anyway: keep it dry,
keep it out of the worst of the weather, and replace or clean the tubes on a
cycle so parasites and mold do not accumulate across years. Beyond that, the
grass sticking out of one tube is a photograph rather than a problem.

One further note for anybody who put the hotel up to attract mason bees
specifically: the two do not compete for the same tubes in any way that harms
the bees. Mason bees work in early spring and seal their cells with mud; the
grass-carrying wasp works in high summer and seals hers with vegetation. They
use the same block at opposite ends of the season, and a tube that held a mason
bee in April is simply an empty tube in July.
