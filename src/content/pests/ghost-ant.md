## The honest answer first

The ghost ant is a real insect and it is a genuine structural pest. It is also
tropical, it is not established outdoors anywhere in the Pacific Northwest, and
the regional extension guide to the key ant pests of Washington, Oregon and
Idaho does not include it.

So this page starts where a page about it should start in this county: if you
have a tiny pale ant in a Whatcom County house, it is far more likely to be
something else, and the useful work is telling which.

That is not a reason to leave the species off this library. Ghost ants do occur
in heated buildings and greenhouses well north of their natural range, they
behave differently enough from our common ants that a wrong identification leads
to a wrong treatment, and somebody who genuinely has them deserves an accurate
page rather than a generic one.

## What a ghost ant actually looks like

The name is a good description, which is not always true of common names.

The head and thorax are a deep dark brown. The **legs and the gaster — the rear
section — are opaque or milky white**, close to translucent. Against most
surfaces the pale parts effectively disappear, and what a person sees is a dark
speck moving with a faint blur behind it.

Workers are **1.3 to 1.5 millimeters**, which is very small indeed, and
**monomorphic** — every worker in the colony is the same size. The antennae have
twelve segments and thicken gradually toward the tip rather than ending in an
abrupt club.

If the ant you are looking at is uniformly dark, or uniformly pale, or has a
distinct two- or three-segmented club on the end of the antenna, it is not this.

## What you probably have instead

Four ants get reported in the same words — small, pale, indoors, in a trail —
and all four are more likely here than a ghost ant.

**Odorous house ant**, *Tapinoma sessile*. Same genus as the ghost ant, common
throughout this region, 2.5 to 3 millimeters, brown to black, all one size. The
diagnostic is the smell: crush one and there is a strong rotten, coconut-like
odor from the anal glands. That test takes two seconds and settles it more often
than anything else on this page.

**Pavement ant**, *Tetramorium*. Light to reddish brown, 2.5 to 3 millimeters,
with the head and thorax sculptured by numerous fine parallel grooves. The other
giveaway is the excavated soil — small piles of sand pushed up along a driveway
edge, a patio joint or a basement slab.

**Thief ant**, *Solenopsis molesta*. One of the smallest ants in the region at
1.3 to 1.8 millimeters, yellowish to brown, with an unusually long two-segmented
antennal club. This is the one genuinely close on size, and the antennal club is
what separates it.

**Pharaoh ant**, *Monomorium pharaonis*. Yellow or light brown, about 2
millimeters, all one size, and an indoor tropical species that does establish in
heated buildings here. This is the one that behaves most like a ghost ant and
requires the same careful approach.

## The test that settles most of these

Crush one and smell it.

That sounds crude and it is the single most useful field test for small dark
ants in this region, because odorous house ants are common, they are the most
frequent answer, and the smell is unmistakable once you have met it. Somewhere
between rotten coconut and blue cheese, strong out of all proportion to the size
of the insect.

No smell, and you are into the smaller pale group — thief, pharaoh, ghost — and
at that point the identification wants a hand lens or a specimen rather than a
guess.

## Why the identification matters more here than usual

For most ants, getting the species slightly wrong costs you some efficiency.
With this group it can cost you the job, because of one behavior.

Ghost ants and pharaoh ants both spread by **budding**. Rather than a mated
queen flying off to found a colony alone, one or more queens simply walk out of
the nest with a retinue of workers and start another one nearby. The colony does
not need a mating flight to multiply and it does not need to leave the building.

The consequence is the thing every pest control company should be able to
explain and many cannot: **spraying a budding ant makes it worse**. A repellent
residual applied to trails and surfaces stresses the colony, and a stressed
colony buds. One nest under a bath panel becomes four spread across a floor,
each one harder to find than the original.

Baits work, because a bait is carried home and shared, and the queens are fed by
the workers. Slow-acting bait reaches the queens; a fast knockdown spray kills
foragers and warns the colony.

So the identification is not academic. It decides whether the treatment
concentrates the problem or removes it.

## If it really is a ghost ant

The situation would look like this.

**Location.** A heated structure, a greenhouse, a plant nursery, a
commercial kitchen, a warehouse handling imported produce, or a building with
year-round warmth and moisture. Not a crawlspace, not a garden, not an
unheated garage.

**Origin.** Introduced, and recently. Ghost ants move with plants, produce,
packaging and freight from warmer regions. A nursery bringing in stock, a store
receiving tropical produce, or a household that recently acquired houseplants
from a long way south are the realistic entry routes.

**Habits.** They nest in small spaces — wall voids, behind skirting, in potted
plant soil, in stacked material, in the gaps around plumbing — and a single
population may occupy many small nests rather than one large one. They are
strongly attracted to sweets and to moisture, so kitchens and bathrooms are
where they are noticed.

**Approach.** Sugar-based bait placed on the trails, no repellent sprays,
attention to moisture and to what is being brought into the building, and a
follow-up to confirm rather than an assumption that one visit did it. Where
plants are involved, the plants themselves are part of the problem.

## Why we would not simply treat and move on

Because a tropical tramp ant established in a heated building in Whatcom County
would be a genuinely unusual finding, and unusual findings are worth confirming
rather than assuming.

If we identified ghost ants on a job here we would want a specimen looked at
properly rather than relying on a field call, because the treatment plan, the
expectation we set with the customer and the follow-up all change. Being right
matters more than being quick.

And if it turned out to be the odorous house ant it almost certainly is, that is
a much easier conversation and a much cheaper job for the person paying.

## What this page is really for

Most people who arrive here will have searched "tiny ants in the house" and
landed on the most exotic-sounding answer, which is a normal thing for a search
engine to do and a bad basis for a treatment.

So the practical value is the sorting. Small dark ant with a strong smell when
crushed: odorous house ant. Small brown ant with grooved head and thorax and
sand piles on the patio: pavement ant. Very small yellowish ant with a long
two-segmented antennal club: thief ant. Small yellow ant in a heated building
with many queens and no swarming: pharaoh ant. Very small ant with a dark front
and a pale, near-invisible back half: ghost ant, and worth confirming.

Each of those has its own entry in this library, and each has a different
answer.

## Sending us something to identify

We will identify a specimen for nothing, and we would rather do that than quote
for a treatment aimed at the wrong insect.

A photograph sometimes works, though tiny ants defeat most phone cameras. The
better option is a few workers in a sealed bag, a piece of clear tape folded
over, or a small container — collected from the trail rather than from a bait,
because a bait full of ants is harder to work with than three clean specimens.

Tell us where they were, what time of year, whether there was a smell when one
was crushed, and whether there is a plant, a leak or a food source nearby. Those
four details narrow it considerably before anybody looks down a lens.

## What we will not sell you

We will not tell you that you have ghost ants because it sounds more serious
than odorous house ants. That would be a straightforward lie for money, and this
species is exactly the kind of exotic-sounding name that could be used that way.

We will not spray a budding ant. If the identification points at ghost or
pharaoh ants, the answer is bait and the reasoning goes in writing, because the
customer is owed an explanation of why the visible fast option is the wrong one.

We will not put a house on a recurring program for an ant we have not
identified.

And if what you have is the common one, we will say so, and the visit that
established it will not appear on an invoice.

## What a tramp ant is, and why it matters

Ghost ants belong to a small group of species that entomologists call **tramp
ants**, and the label explains almost everything about how they behave in
buildings.

A tramp ant is a species that has spread around the world with human trade
rather than by its own dispersal. They share a set of traits that suit that
life: very small workers, many queens per population, colonies that reproduce by
budding rather than by mating flights, little or no aggression between nests of
the same species, and a tolerance for living entirely inside structures.

That combination produces something quite unlike a native ant colony. Instead of
one nest with a defended territory, you get a diffuse population occupying many
small nests that cooperate, exchange workers and expand sideways. There is no
single nest to find and destroy, because there is no single nest.

The same description fits pharaoh ants, which is why the two get discussed
together and why both resist the treatment that works on ordinary ants.

## Why the climate stops them outdoors here

Ghost ants are a tropical species and their limit is cold, not competition.

In Florida and Hawaii they live outdoors year-round and are a genuine landscape
and structural pest. Moving north, the outdoor populations disappear and what
remains are indoor ones — colonies inside heated buildings and greenhouses,
which are effectively small pieces of the tropics maintained at expense through
a northern winter.

Records exist that far north; the species has been reported from heated
structures as far as Manitoba. What does not happen at this latitude is
establishment in soil, in gardens or in the wider environment. A Whatcom County
winter ends an outdoor colony.

The practical consequence for anybody reading this is that a ghost ant problem
here would be a **building** problem with an identifiable point of entry, not a
neighborhood problem creeping in from outside. That is genuinely good news,
because a closed system can be dealt with.

## Greenhouses, nurseries and anybody receiving plants

If ghost ants are going to appear in this county, this is where.

A commercial greenhouse holds warmth and moisture through the winter, contains a
great deal of potting media and plant material to nest in, and receives stock
from suppliers who may be much further south. Every one of those is a condition
the species needs.

The controls are unglamorous and they are mostly about receiving rather than
treating.

**Inspect incoming stock**, particularly anything from a southern supplier, and
look at the root ball and the pot rim rather than the foliage. Potted plants are
the classic transport for several small ant species, and the extension guidance
for another of them — the ponerine ants, which are also established here from
subtropical origins — makes exactly the same point about checking pots.

**Hold new stock separately** where the operation allows it, long enough to see
whether anything is moving in the media.

**Keep an eye on trails rather than on individuals.** A tramp ant population
shows itself as persistent thin trails along edges — bench legs, irrigation
lines, the junction of a wall and a floor — rather than as a swarm.

**Report it rather than treating it quietly.** An unusual ant in a nursery is
worth identifying properly, because the business consequence of shipping one
out to a customer is worse than the cost of a specimen being looked at.

## If we found them, what the work would look like

Bait first and bait only, at least to begin with.

The bait would be sugar-based, because that is what the foraging workers respond
to, placed directly on the trails rather than scattered, in enough placements to
serve a population spread across many small nests. It would be left alone rather
than topped up constantly, since disturbance is the thing to avoid.

No repellent residual anywhere near the active area. Nothing sprayed on trails.
Nothing applied that would cause the population to move, because moving is
exactly what this group does when pressed and every move makes the next
treatment harder.

Moisture would get attention alongside the bait, because these are ants that
follow water as much as food — condensation, a slow leak under a sink, an
over-watered planter.

And there would be a follow-up visit to confirm rather than an assumption that
one round did it, because a budding population that looks gone for three weeks
is a normal stage rather than a result.

## Why a specimen beats a description

Everything on this page turns on identification, so it is worth saying how ants
are actually identified rather than leaving it as something that happens
elsewhere.

The characters that separate these species are structural and small. How many
segments in the waist — one node or two. How many segments in the antenna, and
whether the last few thicken into a club and how abruptly. Whether the thorax
carries spines. Whether the surface is smooth or sculptured with grooves.
Whether there is a sting.

None of that survives a phone photograph of a two-millimeter insect on a
counter. It survives perfectly well in a specimen under a hand lens, which takes
about a minute.

That is why we ask for a sample rather than a picture whenever the ant is small
and pale, and why we do not charge for the identification. A wrong species name
here does not cost a little accuracy. With a budding ant it costs the customer a
worse infestation than the one they called about.
