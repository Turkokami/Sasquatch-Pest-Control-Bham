## The position, stated first

We do not treat bumble bees.

That is not a case-by-case judgment we will walk back once somebody is annoyed
enough, and there is no reason to make you read down the page for it. If what
you have found is a bumble bee colony — under a shed floor, in a compost heap,
in an old rodent burrow in a bank, in the loose insulation of a wall or a
crawlspace, in the bottom of a bird box — the answer is nearly always to leave
it where it is and let the season finish it.

That is not sentiment and it is not us being difficult. It rests on three facts,
all of which are checkable, and all of which point the same way.

The colony is small. The colony is over by autumn, permanently. And several of
the species doing this in Whatcom County are in real trouble.

## The trouble part, because it is the part people do not know

Washington has at least 23 species of bumble bee, which is a larger number than
most people expect and a genuinely good thing for a state whose agriculture
leans on pollination.

Some of them are doing badly.

The clearest case is the western bumble bee, *Bombus occidentalis*, which was
once one of the most common and widespread bumble bees in the West. The
Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife's account of what happened to it is
stark. Populations crashed in the 1990s. Occupancy across its range fell by
about 93 percent over roughly two decades. Surveys have since located only a
handful of populations anywhere in Washington, and those are mostly in remote
subalpine and montane sites rather than in the lowland country where the species
used to be ordinary. It carries state Candidate status, it is listed as a
Species of Greatest Conservation Need under Washington's State Wildlife Action
Plan, and a federal status review has been underway to decide whether it
warrants listing.

WDFW's assessment of the cause is worth reading directly, because it is not the
one most people assume: the speed and breadth of the collapse, including out of
apparently high-quality habitat, supports the prevailing hypothesis that
pathogens escaping from commercial bumble bee rearing facilities into wild
populations are the main factor.

It is not the only species in difficulty. WSU's home garden guide notes that
Franklin's bumble bee — a species with one of the smallest known ranges of any
bumble bee in the world, in southern Oregon and northern California — may now be
extinct.

That is the background against which somebody asks whether we will spray a nest
in a compost heap. The answer is no, and now you know why.

## The colony is annual, and that decides most cases

This single fact resolves the great majority of bumble bee calls, and it is
different in kind from what a honey bee or a yellowjacket does.

A bumble bee colony lasts one season and then ends. There is no perennial hive,
no stored surplus carried through winter, and no returning population.

WSU's account of the cycle: young queens that mated the previous summer
overwinter alone, and emerge very early in the year — nest-seeking usually
happens in February and March here, depending on temperature. A queen who finds
a suitable cavity founds the colony by herself, building a small irregular
cluster of ball-like wax cells and laying a batch of about a dozen eggs, which
she incubates and feeds until the first workers take over the foraging.

The colony builds from there to somewhere between a few dozen and a few hundred
individuals. Late in the cycle it produces males and new queens, those mate, and
then the colony declines and dies. WSU puts the end anywhere from as early as
May or June to as late as October, depending on species. Only the mated young
queens survive, and they scatter to overwinter individually somewhere else
entirely.

And the site is finished. WSU's note is that the deserted wax nest is soon eaten
by wax moths and other scavengers. There is nothing left to reoccupy and nothing
that draws next year's queens back to the same hole. A shed that had a colony
this year is no more likely to have one next year than the shed next door.

So the practical shape of nearly every bumble bee problem is: this is temporary,
it is on a clock you can count, and doing nothing produces the same end state as
doing something, minus the cost and minus the dead bees.

## Recognizing one

The identification matters more than it looks, because the three things people
call bumble bees have three different answers.

**Bumble bee.** WSU Hortsense describes them as robust, stout and very hairy,
from about 0.4 to 1.0 inches long. Dense fur all over, including the abdomen,
banded in black and yellow with orange, red or white on some species. Slow,
loud, and unbothered. They fly in weather other bees will not — cold, damp,
overcast mornings are exactly when you see them working, which is one of the
reasons they matter so much in a climate like this one.

**Honey bee.** Slimmer, smaller, amber and brown, finely haired rather than
furry. Colonies in the tens of thousands, perennial, and living in a cavity
rather than in a small pocket of fluff. We do not treat those either, and the
reasoning is different enough to have [its own
page](/pest-library/honey-bee/).

**Carpenter bee.** Similar size and outline to a bumble bee, and separated by
the back half of the insect. UC IPM's description is that carpenter bee abdomens
are shinier than those of bumble bees, with fringes of hairs on only some
segments. In practice: furry all the way down means bumble bee, glossy and bare
at the rear means carpenter bee. The other useful fact is distributional —
carpenter bees are uncommon in northwest Washington, so a large dark bee here is
overwhelmingly likely to be the bumble bee. If there is also a perfectly round
half-inch hole in bare wood, that changes things and belongs on the
[carpenter bee page](/pest-library/carpenter-bee/).

**Yellowjacket**, which is not a bee at all. Smooth, shiny, narrow-waisted,
fast, and interested in your food. Nothing furry about it. If it is at the
table in September it is a wasp, and that is a different service and a different
answer.

## Buzz pollination, and why the species matter here

Worth a paragraph because it explains why an agricultural county cares.

WSU Hortsense describes bumble bees vibrating their entire bodies at a frequency
close to middle C to shake pollen loose from flowers that will not release it
otherwise — buzz pollination, or sonication. Tomatoes, peppers and blueberries
are on WSU's own list of crops where this matters. Honey bees cannot do it.

Whatcom County grows a great deal of blueberry and raspberry. The bumble bee in
your compost heap is not an abstraction about biodiversity; it is a working
pollinator of the crops in the fields a few miles north of here, and the
colonies in gardens and hedgerows are part of the same regional population as
the ones in the fields.

WSU's management recommendation for the genus, in the field of a fact sheet
where a pest guide would normally tell you what to apply, reads: none
recommended.

## Where the nests are, and what they look like

Bumble bees do not excavate. They find a ready-made cavity with soft material in
it and move in, which is why the sites are so consistently domestic.

WSU's list of what they use: old mouse nests, bird nests, house insulation, and
other places above or below ground where dry fluffy nest material is available.
In Whatcom County that plays out as:

**Abandoned rodent burrows** in banks, lawns and under shrubs. The single most
common nest site, and the reason so many people report "bees in the ground."

**Under sheds, decks and outbuildings**, in the void between the floor and the
soil.

**Compost heaps**, which are warm, fibrous, dry in the middle and undisturbed —
close to ideal.

**Loose insulation** in a wall, a crawlspace, a garage ceiling or an unfinished
attic, where a queen has found a route in and settled into the fiber.

**Stacked material** — a tarp folded on a pallet, a bag of potting soil, an old
mattress in a barn, a coil of hose left over winter.

**Bird boxes** with old nesting material still in them, which is a classic.

What you will find, if you can see it, is not a comb. WSU describes an irregular
cluster of ball-like wax cells, more like a small heap of waxy marbles than the
neat hexagonal sheets of a honey bee. There is no honey worth taking, no
structure worth removing, and nothing that will rot into your framing when the
colony ends. That last point is the difference between this and a honey bee
colony in a wall: when a bumble bee colony finishes, there is essentially
nothing left behind to deal with.

## The insulation case, which is the one that worries people

Of all the nest sites on that list, the one that produces the most anxious phone
calls is a colony in the insulation of a wall, a crawlspace, a garage ceiling or
an unfinished attic. It feels like the honey bee scenario, and it is not.

What makes a honey bee colony in a wall a building problem is the mass of comb
and stored honey left behind when the bees go. That is what ferments, bursts its
cappings, runs down framing and feeds a second wave of insects for years. A
bumble bee colony has none of it. There is no honey surplus, no sheet comb, and
the entire structure is a small loose cluster of wax cells sitting in the
fibrous material the queen found. When the colony ends, scavengers work through
what is left and there is essentially nothing to clean up.

So the correct response to bees in loose insulation is the same as the response
to bees under a shed: identify it, mark the entrance, keep away from it, and
wait for autumn. The one addition is that once the colony has finished, the hole
they were using is worth closing — not because the bees will return, but because
a route into insulation is a route for rodents in November and for overwintering
insects in October, and it will get used by something.

The failure mode to avoid is sealing the entrance while the colony is live. Shut
in, the bees work at the material around them looking for another way out, and
in a building the softest direction is frequently toward the room. A colony that
was outside your living space becomes one inside it, which is a worse problem
than the one somebody was trying to solve.

## Temperament, and stings

Bumble bees have a bad reputation they mostly do not earn, and one genuine
capability worth knowing.

WSU describes Pacific Northwest bumble bees as very mild in temperament, and
notes that they will not attack unless the nest is seriously disturbed or
threatened. A bumble bee working a flowering shrub beside a walkway is not a
hazard and does not become one because you walk past it.

The capability: unlike a honey bee, a bumble bee's sting is not barbed, so it
does not tear free and the bee does not die. It can sting more than once. In
practice this comes up when somebody puts a hand into a nest, mows directly over
one, or lifts the thing the colony is under — which is the entire list of ways
people get stung by this insect.

Which is exactly why the standard advice works. Mark where the nest is. Keep the
mower and the strimmer away from it. Keep children and dogs out of that corner.
Do not put a hand into a compost heap you have not looked at. Then get on with
your summer.

## The honest version of relocation

People ask, and the answer they want is that we will scoop the nest into a box
and put it in a hedgerow.

We would rather tell you what usually happens.

Moving a bumble bee colony has a poor record and there are two reasons for it.
The nest itself is a loose cluster of soft wax cells bedded in fluff, without the
structural frame that lets a beekeeper lift a honey bee comb intact — it comes
apart. And the foragers navigate to a fixed location, so a colony moved even a
short distance leaves its working bees orienting to an empty patch of ground.
Move it far enough that they do not return and you have stranded them; move it
close enough that they find it and you have not really moved it.

There are people who attempt it and occasionally succeed, usually early in the
season with a small colony and a well-prepared site. It is not a service we
offer and it is not something we would sell as reliable. Where somebody wants to
try, a local beekeeping association or a native pollinator group is a better
first call than a pest control company, and the honest expectation to set is
that it may not work.

The alternative — waiting — has a success rate of one hundred percent, costs
nothing, and finishes by autumn.

## When it genuinely cannot stay

There are real cases, and pretending otherwise would make the rest of this page
less credible.

**A nest in the immediate path of daily use.** Directly beside a back door, in
the frame of a gate that has to be opened, under a step, or in the section of
lawn a small child plays in. Proximity that cannot be worked around is different
from proximity that is merely uncomfortable.

**A diagnosed sting allergy in the household**, combined with a nest that cannot
be avoided. That is a medical consideration rather than a preference, and it
changes how quickly the conversation needs to happen.

**A commercial or institutional site** — a school entrance, a care facility
doorway, a working area on a farm — where the ground cannot simply be roped off
for two months.

**A nest inside living space** rather than in the structure around it, where
bees are coming through a light fitting or a vent into a room.

In every one of those, the first question we ask is still whether the area can
be worked around for the remainder of the season, because in a surprising number
of cases it can — a doorway can be swapped for another doorway, a play area can
move ten yards, a mowing pass can be skipped until October. Where it genuinely
cannot, we will talk it through with you, and what we will not do is treat it as
the default.

The one thing we would ask is that nobody pours something into the hole first
and calls afterward. That is how a mild colony becomes a defensive one, and it
takes the good outcomes off the table.

## What we will actually do on a bumble bee call

**Identify it, at no charge.** A photograph settles it in under a minute, and a
large share of these calls end there. The single most common outcome is that it
turns out to be a western yellowjacket in the ground, which is a wasp, is a
different animal in every respect, and is something we do treat.

**Tell you the timeline.** Which stage of the season the colony is in, and
roughly when it will finish, which is usually the only piece of information
somebody actually needs.

**Suggest the workaround.** Where to rope off, what not to mow, which door to
use, and how long for.

**Deal with the building afterward, if it is in the building.** Once a colony in
insulation or a wall void has ended naturally, closing the route in is ordinary
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work, and it stops the
same opening being used by rodents and overwintering insects. That is a job
worth doing in October.

**Handle the wasps.** Which is most of these calls.

## Around this county

**The agricultural corridor** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Berry and tree
fruit production here depends on pollination, wild bumble bees are part of that
workforce, and a nest in a field margin or a hedgerow is doing paid work nobody
is paying for.

**Lake Whatcom and the forest edge** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor.
Ground nests in banks, under decks and in old rodent burrows, in numbers, and
almost always somewhere that can simply be left alone.

**Older city neighborhoods** — the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York.
Crawlspace insulation, garage ceilings and the voids under back steps, where a
queen has found a small route into soft material.

**Newer subdivisions** around Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain and Ferndale,
where the nests are more often in landscape features — a bark bed, a retaining
wall, a compost bin — than in the buildings.

**Rural acreage anywhere**, where the answer is nearly always that nobody needs
to do anything and frequently that nobody had noticed until they mowed.

## Pesticide practice, since it is relevant here

One thing worth stating because it applies well beyond this page.

Our routine work is targeted — perimeters, entry points, harborage and structure
— rather than broadcast across vegetation. Broad application over anything in
bloom is one of the main ways general pest work damages pollinators, so we avoid
treating flowering plants, work around bloom where an application has to happen
near it, and prefer timing when foraging is not underway.

On properties adjacent to managed hives or commercial berry ground, that
coordination matters more, and it is worth telling us when you book. None of
that is a certification or a badge. It is a set of ordinary choices a company
either makes or does not, and we would rather say which.

## The short version

A bumble bee colony is small, gentle, temporary, and made up of an animal that
several state and university programs are actively trying to keep from
disappearing. It will be over by autumn. The nest will never be used again.
There is no honey to rot in your wall and no population to come back.

Rope it off, leave it be, and it resolves itself. That is the whole
recommendation, and it is the same one WSU gives: avoid the area near the nest
for a couple of months until the bees finish their yearly life cycle.
