## What a house mouse is

*Mus musculus* is small, gray-brown above and only slightly paler beneath, with
small eyes, a pointed muzzle and a tail about as long as its head and body
together that is nearly hairless and the same color all the way round. An adult
weighs less than an ounce. Cornell's rodent identification guidance gives the
body at roughly two and a half to just under four inches, with the tail adding
another two and three quarters to four.

It is not a native animal and it does not do well in competition with our native
mice outdoors. What it is very good at is living inside human buildings, and it
has been doing that alongside us for long enough that a modern house is
effectively its native habitat.

The practical consequence, and the reason this profile is organized the way it
is: a house mouse does not commute. It settles into a small territory inside a
structure, feeds within it, nests within it, and breeds within it. Almost
everything that goes wrong in dealing with one comes from treating it like an
animal that ranges.

## House mouse or deer mouse

This is the one identification on the page that changes the health advice, so it
goes near the top.

A **house mouse** is one color from the shoulder to the belly — gray to
gray-brown, blending rather than switching. Its eyes and ears are modest for its
head. The tail is scaly, sparsely haired and uniform in color.

A **deer mouse** has a sharply white belly with a clean line where the brown
stops. Its tail is well furred and distinctly two-toned, dark above and white
below, and the line between the two runs straight. Its eyes and ears are
noticeably larger. UC IPM uses those same features to separate the two, and once
you have seen the tail difference you will never need the rest.

Habitat is a strong prior. Deer mice are animals of woodland edge, field margin
and outbuilding, so a Sudden Valley cabin, a Lake Whatcom shoreline property, a
seasonal home at the edge of forest or a barn out toward Everson is deer mouse
country. A downtown apartment, a Sunnyland kitchen or a student rental near
campus is house mouse country. Neither rule is absolute and both are useful.

You cannot tell the two apart from droppings. That matters, and it is why the
cleanup method further down this page applies to all rodent waste regardless of
what you believe left it.

## A quarter of an inch, and a coin that is not

The single number worth carrying around is a quarter of an inch.

Washington's Department of Health tells residents that mice can squeeze through
openings as small as a quarter of an inch. Whatcom County's own rodent guidance
instructs sealing holes and gaps bigger than a quarter inch. The eXtension
wildlife damage guidance gives the same threshold and specifies twenty-four-gauge
quarter-inch mesh as the material for it. UC IPM is more precise again: a house
mouse gets under gaps a quarter inch tall and through openings three-eighths of
an inch wide.

For a physical reference, use a pencil. A standard hexagonal pencil is a quarter
inch across the flats, it is in every drawer in the county, and it can be pushed
into a suspect gap in a way a mental image cannot.

What not to use is the comparison that dominates online pest writing. A mouse
hole is endlessly described as "the size of a dime," and the dime is the wrong
coin by a wide margin. The United States Mint publishes it at 0.705 inches
across — very nearly three times a quarter inch, and larger than the half-inch
figure the same literature gives for rats. Somebody sealing a house to the dime
standard closes almost nothing and reasonably believes the job is done.

We have carried that phrasing ourselves in older writing, which is a fair part of
why it appears here in this much detail. The number was always right. The
comparison never was.

## Thirty feet, and why it changes the whole job

UC IPM records that house mice seldom venture more than thirty feet from their
nest site and food supply. That one behavioral fact explains most of what is
peculiar about mouse work.

It explains why a mouse problem is so often confined to one part of a building
while the rest of it is untouched. A kitchen infestation and a garage
infestation in the same house can be two separate populations that have never
met.

It explains why traps have to go close to the evidence. Ten feet apart along the
active area, tight against a wall, trigger end toward the wall. A trap in the
middle of a room is in a place a mouse has no reason to visit, and a trap in a
different room may as well be in a different building.

It explains why a mouse can live in a wall cavity above a kitchen for months
without anybody in the house seeing one.

And it explains why the exclusion work is different in emphasis from rat work.
With a rat you are closing a building against an animal ranging in from outside.
With mice you are frequently closing the interior route between a void and a
living space at the same time as the exterior shell — the hole cut for a supply
pipe under a sink, the gap behind a dishwasher, the open back of a cabinet run,
the toe-kick void that connects to the floor structure.

## The arithmetic nobody expects

Two sets of figures explain why "there was only one" is not the reassurance it
sounds like.

**Breeding.** UC IPM gives five to ten litters a year of about five or six pups,
with the young reaching sexual maturity in five to six weeks. That compounds
rather than adds. A pair arriving in the second week of October has, by the
standards of that arithmetic, a household by February.

**Feeding.** A mouse eats roughly twenty percent of its body weight daily, taken
in something like two hundred separate nibbles over a night. It is not eating
your food so much as sampling all of it, which is why contamination outruns
consumption and why a single animal fouls far more than it removes.

**Droppings.** Cornell puts house mouse droppings at an eighth to a quarter inch,
pointed at both ends. A single mouse leaves dozens of them a day. So a light
scatter of fresh droppings in one cupboard usually means a recent arrival rather
than a small tolerable population, and recent arrival is the cheap version of
this problem.

## Where they nest here

Mice nest in something soft, dark and within a few steps of food. In Whatcom
County housing that is a short and predictable list.

The void beneath a kitchen toe-kick, and the dead space at the back and
underneath of a cabinet run. The insulated cavity behind a dishwasher or beside a
range. The base of a hot water tank enclosure. The back corners of an attached
garage, especially where stored boxes meet the wall. Inside stored soft goods —
camping gear, upholstered furniture, a bag of cloth in a loft. Under a bath, and
inside the boxed-in section of ceiling that carries the plumbing.

And, more than any of those, in insulation.

## Signs, in the order they usually appear

Mice are quiet, small and mostly nocturnal, so the evidence generally arrives
before the animal does.

**Droppings, somewhere enclosed.** A drawer, the back of a low cupboard, the
shelf above a toe-kick, the top of a stored box. Fresh ones are dark, soft and
slightly glossy; within a couple of days they dull and harden, and old ones
crumble under pressure from something disposable. A mixture of both is the
common finding and it means the activity has been running for a while and is
still running.

**Gnawed packaging.** A corner opened on a cereal box, a bag of rice with a
pinhole and a trail of grains, a chewed edge on a paper sack. The damage is often
small enough to miss until you are looking for it.

**A faint, persistent smell** in a confined space — a cupboard under a sink, a
utility cabinet — that does not correspond to anything stored there.

**Sound.** Light, quick scurrying rather than the heavier movement a rat makes,
usually within an hour of dusk and again before dawn, and usually low in a wall
where the cavity meets the floor structure.

**Shredded material.** Small heaps of finely worked paper, insulation fiber or
fabric, tucked rather than scattered.

**A pet that will not leave one spot alone.** Cats and dogs are frequently the
first detector in the house, and they are frequently right.

If you find droppings, it is worth leaving a representative patch undisturbed for
a day rather than cleaning immediately. Where they are, how many and how fresh
are the three questions that set the trap plan, and once the evidence is wiped
away all three become guesswork.

## Shared buildings, where one unit is never the whole story

Mice are the dominant rodent in apartments, duplexes and the student rental belt
around campus, and shared construction changes what a solution has to cover.

A stud bay that runs behind two units is one space. A utility chase carrying
supply pipe up through four floors is one route. A shared attic over a row of
townhouses has no interior walls at the level the animal is using. So a mouse
population in a building of that kind is a building-level population, and
treating a single unit moves it sideways rather than reducing it.

That has an awkward practical consequence for whoever is calling. A tenant can
seal the gap behind their own kitchen cabinet and gain something real, because
that closes the route into the living space. What a tenant cannot do is close the
foundation vent or the roofline that admitted the animals to the structure, and
that is where the durable work is. If you are renting, the useful move is to
document what you are finding and where, and to raise it as a building issue
rather than a unit issue.

For an owner or a manager, the cheapest moment to do this properly is at
turnover, when a unit is empty, the access is unobstructed and nobody's schedule
has to be worked around.

## What mice do to insulation

Mouse damage in an attic or a crawlspace looks different from rat damage and is
frequently underestimated because of it.

There is no dramatic swathe of material pulled down across several joist bays.
What there is instead is a set of discrete, roughly fist-sized nests tucked
against a top plate or into a corner, a network of shallow tunnels running
through loose fill, and a general urine load along the routes between them,
because mice are incontinent as they travel rather than depositing in one place.

Each of those reduces the still air the material holds, and trapped air is the
whole mechanism by which insulation performs. So the loss is real even where the
material looks broadly intact from the hatch, and in a climate with a long mild
heating season it is a cost that runs quietly for years rather than announcing
itself.

Where it goes far enough — nesting distributed through the material, saturation
you can smell, tunneling that has taken the structure out of loose fill — the
honest answer is removal and replacement rather than cleaning, which is
[attic insulation](/services/attic-insulation/) work, or in a crawlspace,
[cleanout and restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/). Where it has not,
localized removal of the affected area and cleaning of the rest is the
proportionate response, and that is more often the case with mice than with rats.
Anybody quoting a full replacement for a mouse problem should be asked to show
you why.

The sequencing is not negotiable either way: animals out, building closed,
insulation last. New material laid into a building that is still open is bought
for the benefit of next year's mice.

## What house mice carry, and what they do not

**They do not carry the hantavirus Washington reports.** This is the correction
worth making loudly, because the internet is careless about it. The Washington
State Department of Health states that hantavirus-infected deer mice excrete the
virus in urine, saliva and droppings, that infected deer mice live throughout the
state, that people are at risk anywhere in Washington, and that one to five cases
are reported per year with roughly a one-in-three fatality rate. The species
named in that guidance is *Peromyscus maniculatus*. Whatcom County's health
department repeats the attribution. Neither names *Mus musculus*, and neither
names rats.

The department's surveillance guideline adds the proportions, and they are worth
carrying alongside the annual figure rather than instead of it: 58 Washington
cases in total through 2022, 20 of which were fatal, and roughly seven in ten
reporting their exposure east of the Cascades. Both halves of that matter to
somebody reading this in Bellingham. The state genuinely does not carve the west
side out of its risk statement, and the great majority of the exposure history
this state has recorded sits on the other side of the mountains. Fewer than sixty
cases in decades, most of them east, in a state of eight million people, is a
different proposition from the impression a search result usually leaves.

That is not a reason to relax about house mice. It is a reason to be accurate
about which animal is doing what, because a health warning attached to the wrong
species eventually stops being believed for the right one.

**What house mice are actually associated with** is a shorter and less dramatic
list. UC IPM names leptospirosis, salmonella and lymphocytic choriomeningitis.
CDC's LCM fact sheet is direct on the last of those: the primary host is the
common house mouse, roughly five percent of house mice across the United States
carry the virus, they shed it for life without appearing ill, and exposure comes
from fresh urine, droppings, saliva or nesting material. Illness is usually mild
and fatality is under one percent, but infection during pregnancy can affect a
fetus, which is the one circumstance in which this is more than a nuisance.

**Allergen load is the underrated one.** Mouse urine is a significant indoor
allergen, and in a building with a long-running population it accumulates in
exactly the dust that gets disturbed by cleaning. In a household with an
asthmatic occupant this is frequently the most consequential thing a mouse
population is doing.

**The cleanup rule follows and applies to everything.** Air an enclosed space for
at least thirty minutes before you go in. Do not sweep and do not vacuum dry
droppings or nesting material. Wet it down with a ten percent bleach solution and
give it ten minutes. Pick up with disposable towels, wearing gloves, and bag it
for an outside bin. That is Washington's own guidance and it is written for the
worst case, which is the correct way to write it when the species cannot be told
apart by eye.

## Trapping, and where it goes wrong

Mice are, helpfully, the opposite of rats on the one behavior that matters most.
Rats avoid new objects for days. Mice investigate them, sometimes within hours of
placement. So a mouse trap line that is set correctly usually produces results the
first night, and a line that produces nothing for a week is telling you something
about the setting rather than about the population.

**The usual error is quantity.** One trap under a sink is a gesture. Six to a
dozen across the active area is a trap line, and the cost of the extra traps is
trivial against a second visit.

**The second is distance.** Thirty feet is the animal's world; traps outside it
are ornaments.

**The third is orientation.** Set tight against a wall with the trigger end
toward the wall, so the animal running its usual route along the baseboard passes
over the trigger rather than around the back of the trap. Pairs set an inch or
two apart increase the odds. Peanut butter works, and so does a wisp of nesting
material, which is sometimes better in a house with abundant food.

**The fourth is the one people do not expect.** Where mice are living in
insulation, floor traps catch the animals traveling and miss the ones that never
come down. Traps have to go into the material's plane — against top plates, along
the joist runs, at the edges of the affected bays — and that means somebody
willing to go up or under rather than leaving a box by the hatch.

**Glue boards we do not use.** They are slow, indiscriminate, and they leave you
with a live animal to deal with.

## Bait, and the two reasons for caution

Rodenticide has a legitimate role in exterior and commercial pressure management.
Inside a house with mice it is usually the wrong tool, for two separate reasons
worth stating rather than implying.

**The first is where the animal dies.** A mouse that has taken bait goes back to
the place it feels safest, which is inside a wall cavity, a floor structure or a
cabinet base. Washington's health department makes the same point in its own
guidance. The odor runs for weeks, it draws flies on top of itself, and sometimes
the only remedy is opening the wall.

**The second is everything downstream.** Anticoagulants kill slowly, poisoned
animals keep moving, and predators take the dose with the prey. EPA removed the
second-generation anticoagulants — brodifacoum, bromadiolone, difenacoum and
difethialone — from consumer products specifically because they pose greater
risks to non-target species that eat bait once or eat animals that have, and left
them available to professionals. That places the responsibility on product choice
and placement rather than on the label. Owls, hawks, coyotes, cats and dogs are
the animals on the receiving end of getting it wrong, and in the rural parts of
this county the owls are doing more rodent work than any of us.

So: trapping inside the envelope, bait outside it and in tamper-resistant
stations where property pressure genuinely warrants it, and products selected to
limit secondary exposure rather than by cost per block.

## The season, and the honest verdict

Mouse pressure in this county climbs from late September, runs hard through
October and November and stays elevated all winter. What changes is not the number
of mice outdoors but the relative appeal of a heated building once cover has died
back and outdoor food has declined. A house that was untroubled all summer and
has mice in the second week of October did not get worse; the weather did, and the
quarter-inch gap was always there.

Which sets up the qualification this page owes you. The verdict above says we
treat this, and we do. But trapping is a recurring cost and sealing is a
one-time one, and a building that gets trapped every autumn is a building whose
shell was never closed. Quarter-inch hardware cloth over vents, sheet metal at
chewable edges, closed penetrations under the sink and behind the range, a door
sweep with a gap under a quarter inch, and a crawlspace hatch that actually
latches. That is [exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/), and on
a mouse problem it is what makes the trapping the last time rather than the first
of many.

The window for doing it comfortably is the second half of August, on an empty
shell, before anything has moved in.

## When it is not a house mouse

If the animal has a white belly with a hard edge to it and a two-tone furry tail,
it is a deer mouse, and the cleanup guidance above matters more rather than less.

If the droppings are noticeably bigger than a grain of rice and blunt at the ends,
the trap line and the gap sizes both change — that is a
[Norway rat](/pest-library/norway-rat/) or a
[roof rat](/pest-library/roof-rat/), and being under-trapped for a rat is a
common and expensive way to lose a month.

If what you are finding is closer to coarse ground pepper than to a pellet, it is
not a rodent at all.

And if the noise is overhead in daylight and heavier than a mouse could produce,
it is very likely something we do not handle, in which case we will say so on the
phone rather than after driving out.
