## The dog flea that is not

Somebody in Whatcom County finds fleas on a dog and reasonably calls them dog
fleas. The species is almost certainly *Ctenocephalides felis*, the cat flea,
and the name is a historical accident rather than a description of its habits.

UC IPM calls it the most common external parasite of domestic cats and dogs
alike, and states plainly that a flea found on either animal is most likely this
one. Purdue's public health entomology guidance goes further on the host range:
alongside cats and dogs it lists chickens, calves, pigs and other livestock,
and among wild animals coyotes, raccoons and opossums. A true dog flea,
*Ctenocephalides canis*, exists and turns up occasionally. The human flea,
*Pulex irritans*, is on the Pacific Northwest list and is genuinely uncommon in
housing here.

The practical value of knowing this is small but real. If somebody tells you that
what is in your carpet is a dog flea and that it therefore behaves differently,
the identification is worth a second look, because the treatment does not change
and the biology below is what you are actually dealing with.

## The fact that explains almost everything else

Here is the number to carry: about ninety-five percent of the fleas in an
infested house are not fleas you can see.

University of Arizona extension states it as a straight split — roughly
ninety-five percent of the population is eggs, larvae and pupae distributed
through the house, and the other five percent is adults living on the animal.
Everything frustrating about fleas follows from that ratio.

It explains why a household treats the pet, watches the visible fleas disappear,
and finds the house full again a fortnight later. It explains why a thorough
vacuum makes a real dent where a spray on a sofa does not. It explains why the
follow-up visit is part of the plan rather than an admission that something went
wrong. And it explains why a flea job has a household half and a professional
half, and fails when either one is skipped.

## The life cycle, stage by stage

**Eggs.** A female cat flea produces about twenty to fifty of them a day, per UC
IPM — Missouri extension puts a lifetime figure at up to eight hundred. They are
pearly white, oval and about a thirty-second of an inch, and crucially they are
not sticky. They fall off the animal within hours of being laid, wherever it
happens to be standing. That single detail redirects the whole treatment: the
nursery is not the pet, it is the pet's route through the house and the places it
lies down.

Eggs hatch in two to five days.

**Larvae.** Blind, legless, wormlike and up to three-sixteenths of an inch, moving
by bristles rather than feet. They avoid light and work downward — into carpet
fiber, floor cracks, the joints of hardwood, the underside of upholstery cushions
and the fabric of a pet bed. They do not bite anything. They feed on organic
debris and, more importantly, on the dried blood in adult flea droppings, which is
why the flea dirt shaken out of an animal's coat matters as food supply rather
than just as evidence.

Development takes eight to fifteen days and needs at least seventy-five percent
relative humidity at seventy to ninety degrees Fahrenheit. UC IPM is specific that
larvae are not usually found in heavily walked areas or in places that get direct
sun. Anybody treating the middle of a bright living room floor is applying
product where the insects are not.

**Pupae.** The larva spins a silk cocoon and works local debris into it as it
goes, which camouflages the thing and makes it awkward to reach with anything
applied to a surface. Inside, development finishes and then stops. UC IPM records
fully formed adults remaining in their cocoons for up to twelve months at cool
temperatures. Missouri gives up to a year in unfavorable conditions, and up to
five months waiting for a host.

**Adults.** Emergence is triggered rather than scheduled. UC IPM names warmth and
mechanical pressure — somebody walking on a carpet, a vacuum running. Missouri
adds body heat, odor and air movement. Purdue describes pupae emerging quickly
when they detect the vibration of an approaching animal or person.

Once out, the adult finds a host and stays there. UC IPM notes that the female
cat flea remains on the animal, unlike most other fleas, and lives roughly thirty
to forty days there. Purdue records adults taking as many as ten to fifteen blood
meals a day and jumping at least twelve inches. At room temperature the whole
cycle runs in about eighteen days.

## Why fleas appear in an empty house

This is the single most misread situation in the whole subject, and once the
pupal stage is understood it stops being mysterious.

A property stands vacant. A sale closes, or a rental turns over, or a summer
place at Birch Bay is shut up from October. The previous occupants had an animal,
so the carpet holds a cohort of cocoons. With nobody in the building there is no
warmth, no footfall and no carbon dioxide, so nothing emerges. The population
simply holds.

Then people move in. Within a day or two of the first evening walking around,
fleas appear in numbers, and the new occupants conclude the house was infested
and the fact was hidden from them.

In a sense it was infested, and in the sense that matters nobody could have seen
it. There was nothing to find. The cocoons are camouflaged, they are down in the
carpet fiber, and the insects inside them were doing nothing at all until the
building told them a host had arrived.

The same mechanism produces the more modest version: a family returns from two
weeks away and walks into fleas that were not there when they left.

None of this says anything about the house, the previous tenants or the cleaning.
It says the property had an animal in it at some point in the preceding months.

## Confirming it is a flea at all

Two other things get reported as fleas here often enough that it is worth being
able to rule them out from an armchair.

**Springtails** are tiny, soft-bodied, and they spring — which reads as jumping.
They turn up around damp: a bathroom floor, the soil surface of a houseplant, a
window well, the edge of a shower base. They do not bite anything, they are not
parasites, and they are telling you about moisture rather than about an animal.
A springtail collapses when you press it. A flea does not, because it is
laterally compressed and armored, and a person who has tried to crush one between
two fingers usually remembers the failure.

**Carpet beetle larvae** are the other, and they matter because their shed
bristles irritate some people's skin and produce welts that get read as bites.
The larva is a small bristly elongated thing in a carpet edge or a closet floor,
not a jumper.

The test that settles it is **flea dirt**, and it costs nothing. Comb the animal
over a sheet of white paper or a light towel, or run a damp paper towel across
the spot where it sleeps. Flea droppings are dried blood, so dark specks that
smear rusty red when moistened are diagnostic. Grit that stays gray is grit.

A flea itself, seen properly, is dark reddish-brown, about an eighth of an inch,
flattened side to side rather than top to bottom, with the hind legs built for
the jump Purdue measures at a foot or more.

## When this does not need us

The library is meant to say so where that is the truth, and with fleas there is a
real case for it.

A dog comes back from a weekend with somebody else's animals, and two or three
fleas are combed out over the following days. The pet is on a veterinary
preventive, nothing is being found on the floor, no eggs have had time to
complete anything, and nobody in the house is being bitten. That is an
introduction that has not established, and the useful response is the comb, the
hot wash of the bedding, a vacuum, and a fortnight of paying attention rather
than a treatment.

What moves it into a job is duration and evidence off the animal. Flea dirt in
the carpet where the dog lies. Bites appearing on people. Adults still turning up
three weeks later after the pet has been treated. Any of those means the house
has a developing population rather than a few passengers, and at that point the
ninety-five percent is what you are dealing with and the household half alone
will not finish it.

## Where the source is when there is no pet

A flea problem in a house with no animal in it is not really a flea problem. It is
an animal problem with fleas attached, and treating the carpet without resolving
the source buys a few good weeks.

The hosts Purdue lists for this species include raccoons, opossums and coyotes,
and in this county the pattern is consistent. Something dens in a crawlspace,
under a deck, in the void behind a porch skirt, or in a wall cavity or attic. It
raises young there through spring and summer, and the fleas breed in the nest
material. Then the animal leaves, or is removed, or dies — and a cohort of adults
emerges into a nest with no host in it and moves toward the nearest warm-blooded
thing, which is upward and inward.

The tell is timing and geography. Biting that starts abruptly, concentrates in one
part of the house, and follows a period of noise overhead or under the floor.
Sometimes it follows a rodent job that was otherwise successful.

Where the animal is a rodent, that is [rodent control](/services/rodent-control/)
and then closing the way in, which is
[exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/). Where it is a raccoon,
an opossum or anything else in the nuisance wildlife category, it is a
specialist's work rather than ours, and we will say so on the phone instead of
after driving out. Either way the sequence is the same: deal with the animal,
close the opening, then treat.

If you have no pet, say that first when you call. It is the most useful sentence
available to you and it changes what the visit is looking for.

## What goes on the animal is a veterinary decision

We do not treat animals and we will not tell you what to put on one.

This is not a liability posture. Flea products for pets run on active
ingredients dosed by body weight with hard species restrictions, and the
consequences of getting that wrong are serious — several compounds that are
routine on a dog cause severe harm to a cat. There are also interactions with
other medication, age and weight limits, and conditions that rule particular
products out. The person qualified to weigh all of that is a veterinarian who has
the animal in front of them, and the answer legitimately differs between two dogs
in the same household.

The Pacific Northwest handbook, which is written for exactly this region, states
that success depends on simultaneous treatment of the pets and the premises.
Purdue splits the same job the same way: the veterinarian for the animal, the
pest control operator for the structure.

What that means in practice is worth being blunt about, because both halves
failing separately is the commonest outcome we see.

An animal treated while the house is left alone gets re-infested continuously
from the carpet, and the owner concludes the veterinary product does not work.

A house treated while the animal is left alone gets re-seeded daily by a female
laying twenty to fifty eggs on a moving delivery vehicle, and the owner concludes
the pest company does not work.

So the useful sequence is to book both and get them within a few days of each
other. Call your vet, then call us, and tell each of us what the other is doing.

## What the household half looks like

The part homeowners control genuinely changes the result, which is not something
we say about most pests.

**Vacuum, daily, for at least a fortnight after treatment.** It physically removes
eggs, larvae and the dried blood the larvae are eating. More usefully, the
vibration triggers pupae to come out of their cocoons at a moment when that is a
bad decision for them. An adult emerging into a treated carpet is dealt with; one
that waits three weeks in an armored cocoon is not. Empty the canister or pull
the bag after each session and put it straight outside.

**Vacuum where the insects are, not where the dirt is.** Along baseboards, under
furniture, into upholstery seams, and above all wherever the animal sleeps and
lies. Open floor in a bright room is close to sterile ground for larvae.

**Launder pet bedding hot and dry it hot.** The dryer does the work. Anything soft
the animal habitually lies on counts as pet bedding, including a particular
armchair.

**Clear the floor before treatment.** Toys, shoes, clothing and the contents of
the space under beds, so what is applied reaches the level the larvae are living
at.

**Pick up food and water bowls, and cover fish tanks and switch off their pumps.**

Then keep going after the visit. The vacuuming schedule is not housekeeping
advice bolted onto a treatment; it is a component of the treatment with a
specific job to do.

## How the property gets treated

**Confirm the insect first.** Springtails and carpet beetle larvae are the two
things most often reported as fleas in this county, and neither responds to any of
this. A flea is laterally flattened, dark, hard to crush, and jumps.

**Find the host.** Pet or wildlife. If wildlife, that comes first and everything
else waits.

**Work the focus areas.** Where the animal rests, along the wall line, under
furniture, in upholstery, and in the fabric of pet bedding — the places larvae
actually develop rather than the open middle of a room.

**Use a growth regulator alongside the adulticide.** This is the part that matters
across the length of the job, because it stops eggs and larvae from maturing into
the next generation of breeding adults. Killing the visible five percent without
touching the other ninety-five is how a treatment produces two clean weeks and
then a repeat.

**Treat the specific outdoor places, not the lawn.** Shaded, humid, protected
spots: under a deck, around a crawlspace hatch, beneath shrubs where a dog lies
out of the sun, the north-facing strip against a foundation, and anywhere an
animal has denned. Open sunny grass does not support larvae, and treating it is
money spent on the wrong ground.

**Schedule the follow-up against the pupal emergence**, not against whether
anybody has complained.

Expect activity between the visits. It is the cohort that was in cocoons on the
first day arriving on time, and it is the reason the second visit exists.

## What fleas do to people and animals

For most people, flea bites are itching and small red marks concentrated on the
ankles and lower legs. Purdue notes that several bites more or less in a row are
characteristic of one flea feeding repeatedly, which is the pattern that gets
mistaken for something worse.

For animals it is often more consequential. Flea allergy dermatitis is a reaction
to flea saliva rather than to the number of fleas, and an allergic animal can be
genuinely miserable on a burden a household never notices. That is a veterinary
matter and it is one of the reasons the veterinary half is not optional.

On disease, the honest position is that the risk here is low and specific.

CDC associates the cat flea with murine typhus, with most United States cases
reported from California, Texas and Hawaii, and with cat scratch disease, which
spreads to people through the scratch of a cat carrying these fleas rather than
through the flea bite itself. Both matter more as a reason to control fleas on
animals than as a local hazard.

Fleas are also the intermediate host for the tapeworm *Dipylidium caninum*, which
a person or an animal picks up by swallowing an infected flea. CDC notes small
children are at higher risk than adults, and that most infected people show no
symptoms.

Plague deserves a sentence because it comes up. CDC attributes United States
plague cases to the ground squirrel flea and the oriental rat flea, in rural
areas of the western states. It is not a cat flea story and it is not a Whatcom
County one.

## The year here

Our climate stretches the season at both ends rather than producing a sharp peak.

Activity climbs through late spring and summer as temperatures and humidity rise
together, and the heaviest call volume runs from August into October. That window
overlaps with wildlife moving toward structures ahead of the wet season, which is
why the autumn cases are disproportionately the ones with an animal under the
floor rather than a pet in the house.

Winter does not reset anything indoors. A heated house holds the temperature and
humidity larvae need, so an indoor population continues on its own schedule
regardless of the weather, and cocoons formed in October can hold until something
in the house wakes them.

Spring is the vacant-property season, as sales and tenancies turn over and
dormant cohorts meet new occupants.

Around the county the pattern tracks cover and wildlife. Properties against the
treeline — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, the Chuckanut and Lake Padden margins —
produce the highest share of wildlife-source cases. Older carpeted housing in the
city, with open crawlspaces under raised porches, produces the vacant-unit cases.
Rural property around Lynden, Everson and Sumas produces the property-scale ones,
with outbuildings, working dogs and barn cats in the mix.

## What does not work

**Flea bombs and total-release foggers.** They deposit on the tops of things. The
larvae and pupae are down in the fiber and in the cracks, which the product does
not reach, and the pupal stage is untouched either way.

**Treating the lawn.** Covered above, and worth repeating because it is sold
often. Open sunny grass is too hot and too dry at the surface for larval
development.

**Salt and diatomaceous earth spread on carpet.** Slower than the population
reproduces, and difficult to get back out of a carpet afterward.

**Ultrasonic devices.** They do nothing.

**A single treatment with nothing else.** The most common failure of all, and the
reason is the ninety-five percent that were never adults on the day somebody
came.
