## The rat that is actually here

Washington has exactly two established rat species, and both arrived on ships.
WSU Extension puts it plainly: the Norway rat and the black rat, neither of them
native, both of them here long enough that nobody thinks of them as introduced
any more. Of the two, the Norway rat is what turns up in most Whatcom County
buildings.

Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife describes an animal averaging sixteen
inches nose to tail tip, grayish-brown top and bottom, heavy through the
shoulders, with a scaly tail slightly shorter than the head and body combined.
That last detail is the one worth memorizing, because it is the single
measurement that separates this species from the one it gets mistaken for.

What makes the Norway rat matter in this county is not its size. It is where it
chooses to live. This is a ground animal. WDFW records it nesting at or below
ground level and in the lower floors of buildings, digging burrows typically less
than eighteen inches deep and three feet long with entrances two to four inches
across. Everything about the way it uses a property follows from that
preference, and so does everything about where you should be looking.

## Norway rat or roof rat

The two get written about as though they were interchangeable, and they are not.
Sending somebody into an attic to look for a Norway rat wastes an afternoon;
sending them into a crawlspace to look for a [roof
rat](/pest-library/roof-rat/) wastes the same afternoon in the other direction.

UC IPM sets out the differences cleanly. The Norway rat is the larger and more
robust of the two at seven to eighteen ounces, with a blunt muzzle and ears that
do not reach its eyes when folded forward. Its tail is shorter than head and body
together. The roof rat is sleeker at five to ten ounces, pointed in the face,
with ears long enough to cover the eyes when folded, and a tail longer than head
and body together.

The droppings separate them too. Cornell's rodent identification guidance gives
Norway rat droppings as three-quarters of an inch to an inch, blunt at the ends
and capsule-shaped. Roof rat droppings run to about half an inch and are more
slender. If what you are looking at on a crawlspace vapor barrier is nearer an
inch than a half, you are dealing with the ground species.

Behavior is the tiebreaker when the evidence is ambiguous. Both climb — WDFW is
clear that both are good climbers, and a Norway rat will happily run a
three-inch pipe. But a roof rat prefers to be off the ground and a Norway rat
prefers not to be, and given a choice each takes the option that matches its
build.

## Where it lives on a Whatcom County property

Start outside and low. Blackberry is the single best rat habitat this county
produces, and there is a great deal of it — thickets along creek corridors, the
back of a greenbelt, the fence line nobody has cut for six years. Woodpiles
stacked on soil. Sheds set on skids. The dead space beneath a deck. Compost that
is a heap rather than a bin. Stacked pallets, tarped equipment, an old boat on a
trailer that has not moved since 2019.

Then look at the building's waterline. WDFW notes that rats travel fifty to three
hundred feet from a nest for food and water, and that they cannot go long without
drinking. In a wet county that constraint almost never binds, which is part of
why our populations are steady rather than boom-and-bust.

Inside a building, the Norway rat's territory is the crawlspace, the underside of
the ground floor, the garage, the utility room and the base of the wall cavities
that connect them. It reaches upper floors through vertical chases rather than
over the roof. When people describe a thump rather than a scratch beneath a
living room floor, this is the animal they are describing.

## Reading a burrow

Burrows are the best evidence a property gives you and almost nobody looks for
them, because they sit at ankle height against a foundation and under vegetation.

An active burrow entrance is smooth-edged and clear of leaf litter and spider
webbing, with a fan of loose soil pushed out below it and a beaten path running
away from it toward food or water. WDFW's two-to-four-inch figure is a useful
filter — smaller than that and you are looking at something else entirely.

Two or three burrows along the sunny side of a foundation, a run worn through the
grass toward a bird feeder, and droppings near the deck skirting will tell you
more about the size of the problem than a week of listening at the floor. It is
also the part of a property a homeowner can survey without going anywhere
unpleasant.

## The year, which runs on rain rather than cold

The received explanation for autumn rats is cold. In Whatcom County that is the
wrong mechanism, and believing it puts the prevention work in the wrong month.

We do not get a sustained freeze. What we get is a long saturating wet season,
and a burrow eighteen inches into soil that has taken on thirty-odd inches of
annual rainfall stops being habitable. The animal that dug it is not fleeing
temperature; it is fleeing groundwater. The dry, heated underside of a house is
the nearest available replacement, and it is available because of a vent screen
that rusted through some years ago.

That gives a real and unusually specific piece of timing advice. The work that
prevents a winter of rats is done in the second half of August, on a dry
crawlspace, with nothing living in it. Nobody books it then, because in August
nothing is wrong.

Breeding runs against the same calendar. WDFW records year-round reproduction
concentrated in the warmer months, litters of six to ten, three weeks in
gestation, weaning at about twenty days and breeding maturity at three to four
months. An August population is the one that arrives in November.

## Half an inch, a quarter inch, and the dime that is wrong

Numbers matter here more than adjectives, so here are the ones that hold up.

University of Nebraska Extension's rodent-proof construction guidance states that
rats need slightly more than a half-inch gap to enter and mice slightly more than
a quarter inch. WSU Extension gives the same half-inch figure for rats. Whatcom
County's own health department tells residents to seal holes and gaps bigger than
a quarter of an inch.

Then there is the figure that actually decides whether a building holds. WDFW's
framing is that a rat requires a hole a quarter-inch across **to chew larger and
squeeze through**. That is a different claim from the one most people take away,
and it is the more useful one. The threshold is not the size the animal fits
through. It is the size at which the animal can get its incisors around an edge
and start work — and with teeth growing five inches a year, the work gets done.

Which brings us to a comparison that circulates constantly and is simply
incorrect. A mouse-sized hole is routinely described as "the size of a dime." The
United States Mint publishes the dimensions: a dime is 0.705 inches across. That
is nearly three times a quarter inch, and it is larger than the half-inch rat
figure. Anybody sealing to a dime is leaving both species a doorway and believing
the building is closed.

Use objects that are the right size instead. A quarter inch is the width of a
standard pencil. Three-quarters of an inch — comfortably enough for an adult
Norway rat, and the figure worth working to on a foundation — is the width of a
penny, which the Mint gives as 0.750 inches. Those two comparisons are
dimensionally honest. The dime is not, and it appears on a great deal of pest
control content including, historically, some of ours.

## What the damage actually is

**Wiring.** Cable sheathing is a chewable material sitting in exactly the spaces
this animal occupies. It is the damage with the worst tail risk and the one
nobody can see from inside the house.

**Plastic supply lines.** PEX under a floor is soft enough to breach, and a
pinhole leak in a crawlspace can run unnoticed for months, which produces a wet
crawlspace, which produces a second and larger set of problems.

**Ducting.** Flexible duct tears easily. A breached supply run heats the
crawlspace instead of the house and moves air out of a contaminated space into
the rooms above it.

**The vapor barrier.** Norway rats burrow beneath sheeting rather than walking
over it. The barrier ends up torn, displaced and useless as a ground-moisture
control, which is a building-performance loss on top of a pest problem.

**Stored goods.** Cardboard is a resource rather than a container. Anything soft
in a garage or under a house is nesting material.

## Insulation, and the honest version of that conversation

Fiberglass under a floor is the best nesting material most buildings contain, and
an established crawlspace population does three things to it. It pulls batts down
out of the joist bays to build with. It compresses what remains into flattened
runs. And it saturates the material with urine, after which the fibers settle and
stay settled.

The consequence people underrate is thermal. Insulation works by holding still
air in a low-density mat, so anything that reduces the loft reduces the
performance close to proportionally. A crawlspace that has been worked over for
two winters costs measurably more to heat above, and in a climate with a long
mild heating season that cost runs quietly for years.

The decision that follows is a real one and it goes both ways. Widespread
contamination — material pulled down across many bays, nesting distributed
through the space, saturation you can smell, a barrier that has been tunneled
under and shredded — is a case for taking it out and starting again, which is
[crawlspace cleanout and restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/) rather
than pest work. Scattered droppings on dry, lofted, intact batts is not, and we
would rather say so standing in the crawlspace than sell the larger job.

Where the activity ran overhead as well, [attic insulation
replacement](/services/attic-insulation/) is the same decision applied to a
different space. In both cases the sequence is fixed: animals out, building
closed, material replaced last. New insulation installed into an open building is
a donation to next autumn.

## What a Norway rat carries, and what it does not

This is worth getting exactly right, because the risk that gets attached to rats
in popular writing is frequently the wrong one.

**Hantavirus is not a rat story in Washington.** The Washington State Department
of Health states that hantavirus-infected deer mice excrete the virus in urine,
saliva and droppings, that infected deer mice live throughout the state, and that
one to five cases are reported in a typical year. Whatcom County's health
department says the same thing in the same terms. The animal named in that
guidance is *Peromyscus maniculatus*, and it is not the animal in your
crawlspace.

The department's surveillance guideline is where the scale of it lives, and the
scale is easy to lose in a one-line warning. It records 58 cases in Washington
altogether through 2022, 20 of them fatal, with about 70 percent of exposures
reported on the east side of the state. That last figure is the one a reader in
Whatcom County should hold onto. The health department does not exclude western
Washington from its risk statement and neither do we, but the recorded exposure
history is heavily weighted to the dry country, and a page that quotes the
fatality rate without quoting the distribution is selling alarm rather than
reporting it.

**There is a rat hantavirus, and it is a different and rare thing.** CDC
identifies Seoul virus as a hantavirus carried by Norway rats, found in both pet
and wild rat populations including in the United States. It causes a hemorrhagic
rather than a pulmonary illness and it is uncommon. We name it because omitting
it would be tidier than it is honest, not because it should change what you do.

**Leptospirosis is the one with local weight.** It spreads through water
contaminated with infected animal urine, which makes standing water plus rat
activity the combination that matters — and this is a county with a great deal of
standing water. It is also a genuine risk to dogs.

**Then the ordinary ones.** WDFW lists salmonella and tularemia alongside
leptospirosis for Old World rats. Food contamination is the everyday mechanism
rather than anything exotic.

**Handling follows from all of it identically.** Do not dry-sweep and do not
vacuum droppings or nesting material. Washington's health department directs
airing an enclosed space for at least thirty minutes, wetting the material with a
ten percent bleach solution and leaving it ten minutes, then wiping up with paper
towels while wearing gloves and bagging everything. Because you cannot tell a
deer mouse dropping from a house mouse dropping by eye, that method is the right
one for all rodent waste regardless of what you think left it.

## Trapping, including where it fails

Trapping is how anything inside a building envelope gets removed, and it is a
skill rather than a purchase. It also fails in specific, repeatable ways, which
are worth naming.

**Neophobia is the first one.** UC IPM describes rats as quick to detect and
avoid new objects and new foods, and notes that they often ignore traps and baits
for the first several days. A trap line that produces nothing on night one has
not failed; a trap line moved on night two has. Set them, leave them, let the
animals get bored of them.

**Under-trapping is the second and the most common.** A crawlspace run carrying
an established colony needs traps in double figures. Two traps on a run like that
remove two animals and educate the rest.

**Placement against the wall is the third.** Rats travel with a flank in contact
with a vertical surface — WSU Extension describes them using the same routes
repeatedly along a wall, fence or other boundary. A trap set out in the open,
with the trigger facing the wrong way, sits in a place the animal never goes.

**And here is the local one that catches everybody.** Where a crawlspace has a
vapor barrier, Norway rats very often travel *underneath* the sheeting rather
than across it. Every trap on top of the plastic is then sitting above a runway
it will never intersect, and the job looks unresponsive when it is simply set on
the wrong plane. The tell is a raised, meandering ridge in the sheeting, and
sometimes a smell of disturbed soil. The answer is to cut in, set below, and
patch afterward.

**Glue boards are not part of this.** They are slow, they catch non-target
animals, and they leave a live animal in a state most people find genuinely
distressing.

## Rodenticide, stated with the risk attached

Bait has a place on the exterior of a property under real pressure, and the
reason to discuss it openly is that the risk it carries is not to the rat.

Anticoagulant baits kill slowly. An animal that has taken a lethal dose keeps
moving and feeding for days, and a share of those animals die in the open where
something else finds them. WDFW states the mechanism plainly: dead rats and mice
may contain several lethal doses, and secondary poisoning of pets and wildlife is
possible where carcasses are consumed over a few days — with the further note
that for the strongest compounds a single carcass may be enough.

The animals downstream of that are the ones already doing rodent control for free.
Barn owls, red-tailed hawks, coyotes, and the neighborhood's cats and dogs. On a
Lynden or Everson property with outbuildings and a resident owl pair, poisoning
the predator to kill the prey is a straightforwardly bad trade.

EPA has moved on part of this. The second-generation anticoagulants —
brodifacoum, bromadiolone, difenacoum and difethialone — are no longer registered
for consumer products, precisely because they pose greater risks to non-target
species that feed on bait once or that feed on animals that have. They remain
available to professional and agricultural users, which puts the choice on the
company rather than on the label.

So the position here is: bait selected for reduced secondary exposure rather than
by price, always in a tamper-resistant station, always mapped, exterior only —
and trapping inside the envelope, because an animal that dies inside a floor
structure is a decomposition and blowfly problem in a place nobody can reach.

## Exclusion is the half that lasts

The verdict at the top of this page says we treat this, and that is true. It
would be dishonest to leave it there.

Trapping is a recurring cost. It removes the animals present and does nothing
about the opening that admitted them, so a building that is trapped and not
sealed produces a new population on roughly an annual cycle. The work that
changes the outcome is physical: quarter-inch hardware cloth over vents in the
gauge that survives this climate, sheet metal where an edge will be gnawed,
mortar into masonry, a crawlspace hatch that closes and latches, and every
utility penetration closed with something that has no give in it.

Nebraska Extension specifies nineteen-gauge half-inch mesh to exclude rats and
twenty-four-gauge quarter-inch mesh to exclude mice; the quarter-inch cloth does
both jobs and is what we default to. What does not work, in any gauge, is
expanding foam on its own, steel wool packed into a hole, vinyl weather stops, or
window screen asked to do a rodent's job.

That is [exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work, and on a
rat problem it is almost always the better purchase. We say so knowing it argues
against the recurring half of our own business.

## When it is not a Norway rat at all

Two corrections worth making before anyone treats.

If the noise is overhead, in daylight, and sounds heavier than a rat, it is
probably not one of ours. Squirrels, raccoons and bats all end up in Whatcom
County roof spaces, none of them is rodent work as this company defines it, and
several carry legal timing constraints. We will say what it sounds like from your
description and point you somewhere useful rather than selling a rodent program.

And if the animal is small, sharply bicolored with a white belly, and you are on
a wooded or field-edge property, you are looking at a deer mouse rather than a
young rat. That distinction is the one that actually changes the health guidance,
which is why it appears twice on this page.

## What the work looks like

Someone goes the length of the crawlspace with a light rather than reporting from
the hatch, walks the exterior at ground level, and reads the burrows, runs and
droppings for size, age and direction. That produces a mapped list of openings
and a realistic account of how long this has been running.

Then trapping on the mapped runs in the numbers the activity justifies, revisited
and adjusted rather than laid out and left. Then sealing in metal. Then, only if
the contamination genuinely warrants it, removal and replacement of the
insulation — with the smaller version of that job offered where the smaller
version is the truth.
