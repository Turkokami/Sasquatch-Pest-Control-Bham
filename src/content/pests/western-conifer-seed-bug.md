## The thing that flew at the lamp

It is a mild October evening, a reading light is on, and something roughly the
size of a thumb joint comes across the room with a buzz you can hear from the next
chair. It hits the shade, drops, rights itself, and sits there looking prehistoric.

That is a western conifer seed bug, *Leptoglossus occidentalis*, and it generates
more genuinely frightened phone calls in this county than anything else on this
site that is completely harmless.

It has no sting. It does not bite in any way you need to plan around. It will not
eat your house, your food or your clothes, and it cannot produce a single offspring
indoors. It is a seed-feeding insect from the conifers around your property that
came in to spend the winter, and by April it will be gone.

## Recognizing one

**Size.** Large by household standards. Colorado State puts the adult at about
three quarters of an inch, and Montana State gives a range of roughly half to three
quarters — nine to eighteen millimeters. That is big enough to see clearly from
across a room, which is half the reason for the alarm.

**The hind legs are the whole identification.** This is a leaf-footed bug, and the
name is literal: each hind leg carries a flattened, leaf-like expansion partway
down, like a small paddle. Nothing else that comes into a Whatcom County house in
autumn has that. If you can see the paddles, you are done.

**Color and markings.** Brown to reddish-brown, long and narrow rather than
shield-shaped, with a faint pale zigzag running across the back. Utah State
describes the abdomen beneath as orange to light orange with black stripes, which
is visible in flight when the wings open and is the reason some people report a
wasp.

**The nymphs** are orange, becoming reddish then brownish through five stages, and
you are unlikely to meet one indoors because they are still on the trees.

**The head** ends in a small point, and the mouthparts are a long straw held under
the body — a piercing tube for draining seeds, not a jaw. Once you have seen that,
the idea that it might chew something becomes obviously wrong.

## What people call it instead

Four misidentifications account for nearly every call, and it is worth taking them
in order of how frightening they are.

**A hornet.** This is the one that matters here, and Whatcom County residents have
a better reason for the reflex than most. Northern giant hornets were detected in
this county from 2019, with nests found in 2020 and 2021, and the Washington
Invasive Species Council records that eradication was declared in December 2024
after three consecutive years without a finding. That episode taught an entire
county to look hard at large flying insects, which was the correct response at the
time. A conifer seed bug is not one: it has no sting, no yellow-and-black banding,
and those unmistakable paddle legs.

**An assassin bug.** Related in the broad sense — both are true bugs with a
piercing beak — and unrelated in every way that matters. Assassin bugs hunt other
insects and can deliver a genuinely painful defensive jab. This one drinks from
pine cones.

**A longhorned beetle.** The confusion comes from size and antennae. Beetles have
hard wing covers meeting in a straight line down the back; a seed bug's wings
overlap and end in a membranous section. It is a two-second check.

**A brown marmorated stink bug.** The most common mix-up and the most consequential
one, because those two calls should end differently. WSU's identification
publication FS079E lists the western conifer seed bug among the insects people
mistake for the stink bug, and separates them on the obvious grounds: the stink bug
is a broad shield with banded antennae, and this is a narrow brown insect with
paddle legs.

## The conifers, and why that puts it on your wall

The trees are why this animal is here in the numbers it is.

Utah State lists its hosts as Douglas-fir, pines, spruces, hemlocks and other
conifers, and it feeds on the developing seeds within the cones. Colorado State
records the same, adding that the females lay small groups of eggs on the needles.

Look at what Whatcom County is made of. Douglas-fir and western hemlock across the
foothills and the lake shore, ornamental pines and spruces in every subdivision
planted since the seventies, cedar and fir hedgerows on the rural properties,
second-growth stands running up to the back fences of half the county. This is
about as good a landscape for a conifer seed bug as the Pacific Northwest offers.

So the association is direct: the more conifer within a few hundred yards of a
building, the more of these will be on it in October. That is why the calls
concentrate around Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor and the wooded edges
rather than in the middle of a treeless subdivision.

What it does to the trees is close to nothing. Montana State's assessment is that
the insect is not known to significantly affect the health of pines or Douglas-fir.
Where it is taken seriously is in commercial conifer seed orchards, because
draining developing seeds reduces a seed crop — and that is a forestry industry
concern rather than anything a homeowner with a Douglas-fir in the yard needs to
act on.

## The year it has

One generation, run on a simple schedule.

Adults that survived the winter come out of shelter and are active by May and June,
when females lay eggs in small groups on conifer needles. The eggs hatch in around
ten days. Five nymphal stages follow through the summer, feeding on cones and
seeds, and the new adults are mature by late summer.

Then the weather turns and the whole cohort needs somewhere to be. Colorado State
puts the search for shelter between September and November, when they enter homes,
garages and sheds. In Whatcom County the peak is a warm week in early October,
almost always following the first genuinely cold night.

They find shelter by warmth, which is why they end up on the sunlit elevations,
and Utah State's exclusion advice specifically calls out sealing on the south and
west-facing sides for exactly that reason.

Once inside they go quiet in a wall cavity, an attic or the space behind a soffit.
A warm spell rouses some of them, they move toward light, and they arrive in your
living room in January with no visible route and no explanation — which is the
sequence that convinces people something is breeding in the structure.

Nothing is. The insect on your lampshade in February is one of last October's,
and there will be fewer next month than there are now.

## What it does indoors

The honest list is short, and most of it is a list of things that do not happen.

It does not feed. There is nothing in a house resembling a conifer seed, and its
mouthparts are built for one job.

It does not reproduce. Egg-laying happens on needles, in early summer, outdoors.

It does not damage the structure. Utah State states it in as few words as
possible: the western conifer seed bug does not damage homes. There is no chewing,
no boring, no nesting and no tunneling, because the animal is not equipped for any
of it.

It does not bite you, and it has no sting.

There is one genuine oddity worth including for completeness, because it is the
only recorded exception we found and leaving it out would be tidier than it is
honest. Montana State's fact sheet notes a rare incident of one of these insects
puncturing polyethylene tubing — the beak that opens a seed coat can, very
occasionally, get through soft plastic pipe. It is a curiosity rather than a
household risk, and it has never once come up on a job here.

## The smell

They do produce an odor, and it is the mildest of the three insects on the wall.

Colorado State describes an unpleasant pine-like smell when the insect is
disturbed. Maine Extension records the same on handling, pressing or crushing.
Compared with a brown marmorated stink bug it is a minor complaint, and it fades
from a ventilated room quickly.

Vacuum rather than crush anyway, for the ordinary reason that a crushed insect on a
painted wall leaves a mark and a squashed one on a carpet is worse than one in a
canister. Empty the canister outside and seal it, because they are not necessarily
dead.

The one myth worth clearing while we are here: the National Pesticide Information
Center states that killing an overwintering bug of this kind does not attract more,
and that the released odor will not attract other bugs. That claim circulates
widely and it is not supported.

## Why we say treating is rarely warranted

The verdict at the top of this page is that treatment is rarely worth buying for
this species, and the reasoning is arithmetic rather than principle.

Consider what a treatment can act on. By the time anyone is upset enough to call —
which is October, or more often January — the population is inside a wall cavity.
Very little reaches an insect wedged dormant in a void. If a product did reach
them, the result would be a quantity of dead insects in an inaccessible space,
which becomes food for carpet beetles that then move into the living space and eat
wool. You would have traded a harmless nuisance for a destructive one, and paid for
the privilege.

Interior application is worse than useless. These insects are not feeding and not
moving across surfaces in any pattern a residual can exploit, and every source we
work from says the same about indoor treatment for overwintering bugs.

So what is left, and what is genuinely worth buying, is two things.

**Sealing.** Utah State names physical exclusion as the preferred approach for this
species, and it is right. The insects are slow, small enough to use an existing
gap and incapable of making a new one, so caulk and intact insect screen are
sufficient — no hardware cloth, no sheet metal. This is
[exclusion work](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/), it is bought once, and it
closes the same openings that admit cluster flies and overwintering stink bugs.

**An exterior application timed to late August or early September**, where the
numbers justify it. Applied before the aggregation begins, to the elevations they
gather on, it reduces how many get in. Applied in late October it is treating a
wall the insects have already walked through. And it is a residual sitting on
siding through a wet autumn, so its useful life here is shorter than a label
written for a drier climate implies.

If you call us in January, we will tell you to vacuum, and book the sealing for
summer. That is the whole of the honest answer, and it is a smaller sale than the
one we could make.

## Where to seal

The openings that matter are mostly above head height, which is why this keeps
happening on buildings where somebody has carefully sealed everything they could
reach from the ground.

Soffit and fascia runs, and the returns where a soffit dies into a wall. Gable,
roof and attic vents with coarse, torn or missing screen. The joint where a window
frame meets siding on the sunny elevations. The open ends of vinyl siding panels
and the J-channel. The junction where an addition's roof meets a wall. Service
penetrations at height — vent boots, brackets, heat pump line sets. Any disused
duct ending in open air with nothing over it.

Then the interior side, which is a separate job and often the one people care about
most: around recessed light housings, bathroom fan surrounds, the attic hatch, and
the top of an interior wall. Closing those does not keep the insects out of the
building, but it keeps them out of the room.

Do the work in August. It overlaps with the window for sealing against rodents
before the rain, so one trip up a ladder addresses several problems.

## Around this county

**The forest edge.** Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor, Geneva, the whole Lake
Whatcom shore, and any property backing onto second growth. Conifers on three sides
and a warm south wall in the middle is the classic setup, and this species dominates
the autumn calls there.

**The Chuckanut corridor and the Skagit foothill towns south of it**, for the same
reason and with the same species mix.

**Older subdivisions with mature ornamental conifers**, which is most of what was
planted around Bellingham between the sixties and the eighties. A pair of mature
pines beside a driveway is a supply line.

**Rural properties with shelter belts** around Lynden, Everson and Deming, where
Douglas-fir and cedar hedgerows put the host within yards of the house.

**Waterfront and seasonal homes** at Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo, where nobody
is present in October to see the wall, and the accumulation is met all at once in
spring.

The one place they are scarce is the treeless middle of a new development. Give it
fifteen years for the landscaping to mature and that changes.

## Children, pets and the panic response

Because this is the insect that makes people scream, it is worth being explicit
about how safe it is.

**A child can hold one.** It is slow, clumsy and unarmed. The worst outcome is a
faint pine smell on the fingers.

**A cat or a dog that eats one** has eaten an insect with a bad taste. There is no
venom and no toxicity of consequence.

**It cannot sting**, and this is not a hedge. There is no stinging apparatus on the
animal. The buzz that makes people duck is wing noise and nothing else.

**It carries no disease.** Utah State's phrasing covers both halves — it generally
does not bite or transmit disease.

If there is somebody in the household who finds insects frightening, this is a good
one to learn, because it is large enough to identify from a safe distance and
harmless enough to be worth the effort. A glass and a piece of card puts it back
outside in about ten seconds, which is a better outcome for everybody than a can of
aerosol emptied at a curtain rail.

## How many is a lot

People ask us whether their numbers are normal, and it is a fair question because
nobody has a baseline for this.

A handful over the course of an autumn is unremarkable on any property with
conifers nearby, and describes most of the county. A dozen or two indoors across a
winter, appearing in ones and twos on warm days, is common on a wooded lot and
tells you only that the building has the usual number of gaps.

The point where the arithmetic changes is when a household is collecting several a
day for weeks, or finding them in a room that has no obvious connection to the
outside. That is not more insects on the property. It is a building with an
unusually good route from the wall cavity into the living space, and it is a
sealing problem with a specific location rather than a pest problem in general.

The other signal worth noticing is a seasonal home. A house shut up from October
until spring collects a whole winter's arrivals with nobody to remove any of them,
so an owner arriving in April meets four months of accumulation at once and
reasonably concludes the place is overrun. It is the same rate of entry as a
lived-in house; only the collection method differs.

Year-to-year swings are ordinary too. A warm dry late summer produces a heavy
autumn, and a cool wet one can pass almost without notice, so a quiet season is
weather rather than evidence that something worked.

## What actually happens when we come out

Since the honest answer for this species is usually that no treatment is warranted,
it is worth saying what a visit consists of instead.

Somebody walks the outside of the building slowly, paying particular attention to
the sunlit elevations and to everything above head height, because that is where
the openings are. Then the attic, because a roof space is where an overwintering
population usually ends up and where the routes down into the rooms are visible
from the inside rather than the outside.

Out of that comes a list of openings, their sizes, and what sits behind each one —
plus the items that belong to another trade, marked as such. If the fix for your
building is a length of failed soffit or a vent screen that rusted through, we
would rather say so than sell a service that works around it.

Then the conversation about timing, which for this insect is most of the value. If
it is February, the useful work is scheduled for August and the interim measure is
a vacuum. If it is July, we can do the whole thing before the insects arrive and the
difference shows up in the same year.

And if the answer is that your numbers are ordinary and the building is reasonably
tight, that is a legitimate outcome and we will say it. A customer who learns to
recognize this insect and stops worrying about it has got the full value of the
call, whether or not anything follows.

## What is not worth buying

**Foggers and total-release products.** They do not reach a wall void, which is
where the insects are.

**Ultrasonic devices.** Nothing.

**A winter interior treatment.** They are dormant. There is nothing for it to act
on.

**Sealing in November.** It shuts in what is already there and stops the warm-day
escapees leaving, which makes the indoor sightings worse rather than better.
Sealing is an August job.

**Removing a conifer.** Even setting aside what a mature Douglas-fir is worth, they
fly, the county is full of host trees, and the arithmetic does not work.

**An emergency visit for a single insect.** One in a room is one insect. A glass
and a card handles it, and we would rather tell you that than drive out for it.

## The short version

Large, brown, narrow, with paddles on its back legs and a wingbeat you can hear.
It came out of the conifers, it wants to be somewhere warm until March, and it can
do you and your building no harm whatsoever.

Vacuum the ones you meet, or carry them outside. Seal the gaps in late summer if
the numbers bother you. And if what you actually have is a broad mottled shield
with white-banded antennae, read the [brown marmorated stink
bug](/pest-library/brown-marmorated-stink-bug/) page instead, because that one has
somewhere to be reported.
