## What the tiny red bugs on your wall actually are

Clover mites. Not spiders, not baby ticks, not bedbugs, and not anything that
has any interest in you.

A clover mite is about a hundredth of an inch long — WSU Pestsense gives that
figure, and it is worth pausing on, because it means the thing you are looking
at is smaller than the full stop at the end of this sentence. They are oval and
reddish-brown, with one feature that gives them away under any magnification:
the front pair of legs is much longer than the other three pairs and is carried
out in front, so people consistently mistake those legs for antennae.

They feed on clover and grasses. That is the entire diet. WSU describes them as
occasional household pests that do not bite humans or animals and cause no
structural damage, and every other extension service in the country says a
version of the same thing.

What makes them memorable is not any of that. It is the numbers. Clover mites do
not arrive one at a time; they arrive in hundreds or thousands, on a warm day
in spring or autumn, all over one wall, and the effect of that on somebody who
has just opened the curtains is not proportionate to a harmless lawn feeder a
hundredth of an inch long.

## Clover mites, spider mites, and the names people use

Three different sets of words for roughly the same experience, and it is worth
sorting them out because they send people to different answers.

**Tiny red spiders.** This is the phrase most people reach for, and it is one of
the highest-volume spider searches in the United States while being almost
entirely not about spiders. Mites are arachnids, so the instinct is not
ridiculous. But they are not spiders, they do not spin, and the spider pages on
this site will not help you.

**Spider mites.** A different group, and a plant problem rather than a building
one. Spider mites live on the leaves of ornamentals and vegetables, fine webbing
and stippled foliage are the giveaway, and they are a gardening question. If
your complaint is about a wall and not about a plant, you have clover mites.

**Red spider mite.** A name used loosely for several things and reliably for
none. It turns up in garden centers attached to products that will not help with
a wall.

**Chiggers.** Worth ruling out because they do bite. If something tiny and red
is associated with intense itching after being in long grass, that is a
different animal and not this one. Clover mites leave nothing on skin at all.

## Why they are on the south wall and not the north one

Because they are following the sun, and once you know that the whole pattern
makes sense.

Clover mites move up warm surfaces on mild days. WSU describes them swarming over
sunny outer walls and entering through cracks and crevices around windows, doors
and foundations. In practice, in a Whatcom County neighborhood, that means:

- the south and west faces of a house get them and the north face does not
- light-colored siding collects more than dark, because it reflects warmth up
- concrete patios, paths and foundation stem walls are prime surfaces
- the sunniest window in the house is the one with mites on the sill inside
- a mild, bright day after a cold spell is when you see the worst of it

It also means the problem is geographically tiny. People describe it as an
infestation of the house. It is almost always a problem with one elevation,
sometimes one window, and that is a far smaller thing to solve.

## Where they come from, which is the part nobody wants to hear

The lawn, growing right up against the building.

Clover mites feed on turf and clover. A well-watered, well-fertilized lawn that
runs straight to the foundation is a feeding ground with a vertical surface
attached, and the mites that live in the top of that turf have no particular
reason not to walk up the wall when the sun hits it. Heavily fertilized lawns
produce the lushest growth and, predictably, the worst years.

The sequence looks like this. The turf against the wall supports a large
population. A mild day in April or October warms the siding. The mites move up.
Some find a gap — a failed window seal, a crack at the base of the siding, a
weep hole, a door sweep that no longer touches. They come inside, where there is
nothing to eat and no moisture, and they die within a few days.

Which is why the indoor part of this problem is a symptom and not the problem.
You can vacuum the windowsill every morning for a fortnight and the wall outside
will keep sending more, because nothing about the wall has changed.

## The life cycle, and why there are two waves a year

Two generations, with a gap in the middle, and the gap is the reason people
think they have fixed it when they have not.

Penn State Extension sets out the cycle. The mites overwinter mostly as eggs,
tucked into dry protected places — the cracks in concrete paths, under tree
bark, in the gaps between building walls, behind siding. Those eggs hatch early
in spring, around the start of April, or whenever temperatures get reliably
above about 45°F. In a mild winter they can become active far earlier, during a
warm spell.

That spring generation runs through until early summer and then stops. The mites
estivate — a warm-weather dormancy, the summer equivalent of hibernating —
which is why a wall that was covered in May is clean in July and everybody
assumes the problem went away. A second generation develops in autumn, the wall
does it again in September or October, and then the overwintering eggs are laid
as nights start dropping below freezing.

Two consequences follow, and both are practical:

1. **The autumn appearance is not a new problem.** It is the same population on
   its second run, and treating in October without changing anything outside
   simply books an appointment for April.
2. **The useful work happens between the waves.** Late spring and late summer,
   when the wall is quiet, is when a foundation band or a sealing job gets done
   without working through a swarm.

Penn State also notes the part that reframes everything: they cannot reproduce
indoors, and ones that get in die within a few days of dehydration. There is no
such thing as a clover mite population living in a house.

## Do clover mites bite, and do they damage anything?

No, and no, and it is worth being specific about both.

**They do not bite.** WSU Pestsense states it directly: they do not bite humans
or animals. There is no stinging, no skin reaction, nothing transmitted. If
something is biting you and you are also seeing tiny red specks, those two facts
are probably unrelated and the biting one is worth running down properly — fleas
and [rodent mites](/pest-library/rodent-mite/) both produce that complaint and
both are real.

**They do not damage the building.** No structural damage, nothing chewed,
nothing bored, no wood or fabric interest. They are not in the same category as
anything on the [termite and wood-destroying pest](/services/termite-control/)
side of this trade.

**They do not infest food or fabric.** They have no use for either.

**They do stain.** This is the only real damage they do, and it is not nothing.
A wall or sill wiped with a cloth ends up with a long red smear, and on grout,
matte paint, unsealed timber, curtain fabric or sill caulk that mark can be
permanent.

So the honest summary is that clover mites are a nuisance with one consequence,
and the consequence is caused by the response rather than by the animal.

## The red stain, and what to do instead of wiping

Vacuum. That is most of the advice.

WSU's recommendation is to clean them off surfaces carefully — a soapy sponge
or cloth used gently enough not to crush them, or a vacuum with the crevice
tool. In a real house with a real windowsill and several hundred mites on it,
the vacuum is the one that works, because wiping gently enough to avoid crushing
a hundredth-of-an-inch animal is not a thing most people can do.

What actually works indoors:

- **Vacuum with the crevice tool**, slowly, along the sill and the frame. Empty
  or bag the contents afterwards so nothing walks back out.
- **Do not wipe first.** Once they are crushed, you are no longer removing mites,
  you are spreading pigment.
- **If something is already stained**, treat it as a dye stain rather than a
  grease one. Blot, do not rub, and test anything stronger somewhere hidden.
- **Do not spray insecticide on a windowsill or a kitchen counter.** WSU is
  explicit that no pesticide goes on counters or kitchen surfaces, and against an
  animal that is dying in there anyway it buys nothing.

On the outside of the building, WSU notes mites can be sprayed directly with
soapy water, which knocks down what is on the wall at that moment. It is a
useful way to deal with a bad afternoon. It is not a fix.

## The bare band, which is the only thing that really works

A strip of ground against the foundation with no grass in it.

Penn State's recommendation is a vegetation-free band roughly six to
twenty-four inches wide along the foundation. Gravel, bare soil, river rock,
paving — the material matters less than the absence of turf. The logic is
direct: the mites live in the grass, they walk up the wall, and if the grass
does not reach the wall there is nothing at the bottom of it to walk up from.

Doing it properly, in the order that works:

1. **Strip the turf back.** Six inches is the minimum worth doing, and most of
   the benefit shows up by twelve to eighteen. Take it off, do not just mow it
   short, because the crowns are where the mites are.
2. **Put something down that will stay bare.** Gravel over landscape fabric is
   the usual answer here and survives our winters. Bark mulch is a poor choice —
   it holds moisture against the siding and brings its own problems.
3. **Keep ornamentals off the wall too.** Clover mites will use dense ground
   cover, ivy and low shrubs the same way they use turf.
4. **Fix the gaps while the ground is open.** The band stops the supply; sealing
   stops the route. Doing both at once is one afternoon instead of two.
5. **Ease off the fertilizer on that strip of lawn.** The lushest turf carries
   the heaviest populations, and the edge against the house is the part that
   least needs feeding.

If you do one thing, do the band. Everything else on this page is housekeeping
by comparison.

## Sealing, and why it is worth doing anyway

The band removes the supply. Sealing closes the route, and it is the same list
of gaps that matters for everything else that gets in here.

Clover mites get in through cracks and crevices around windows, doors and the
foundation. The entry points worth checking on the sunny elevation are the
window frame perimeter and the gap behind the stop, the junction where siding
meets the foundation stem wall, weep holes and vent screens, the sweep under an
exterior door, and the frame around a sliding door track.

None of this is clover mite work specifically, which is the point. It is the
same set of gaps that lets in
[overwintering bugs in October](/services/stink-bug-control/bugs-on-the-south-wall-every-october/),
and the full version of the job is in
[sealing a building before fall](/guides/sealing-a-building-before-fall/) and
under [exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/). A house sealed
for one is sealed for the others, which is the argument for doing it once and
properly rather than reacting twice a year.

## Tiny red bugs that are not clover mites

Worth a quick check before committing to the answer, because a few other things
present as small and red here.

**Something that bites.** Not clover mites. Fleas are the common one in this
county — see [flea control](/services/flea-control/) — and rodent mites turn up
after a rodent problem has been dealt with and the mites have lost their host.
Both of those are real bites and both have a different fix.

**Larger, faster, hunting on a wall.** Possibly a small spider. The
[spiders in Washington state](/guides/spiders-in-washington-state/) guide covers
what lives here.

**Red on plants, with fine webbing.** Spider mites, which is a garden question
and not a building one.

**Tiny, reddish, in stored food.** Grain and pantry pests — a different group
entirely, covered under
[beetle and pantry pest control](/services/beetle-control/).

**On a concrete patio in huge numbers in spring.** Clover mites again, almost
certainly. Concrete is a classic surface for them.

## When it is worth calling somebody

Not usually, and we would rather say that on the phone than after a visit.

The cases where a call is reasonable:

- **The numbers are genuinely unmanageable** and have been for successive
  seasons, on a building where taking the turf back is not straightforward — a
  commercial elevation, a tight property line, a landscaping scheme somebody
  else controls.
- **You are getting them in quantity indoors**, which points at a sealing
  problem worth finding rather than at the mites themselves.
- **You are not sure what they are.** That is a photograph and a phone call, not
  a service visit.
- **You want the band and the sealing done properly** and would rather not spend
  a weekend on it.

Where we do treat, the work is outside — a targeted exterior application to the
band of foundation and the vegetation against it, timed to the spring or autumn
wave. WSU notes insecticidal soaps applied to foliage around outer walls and the
foundation, with thorough coverage, as an option. It knocks the population down
for that wave. It does not change the lawn, which is why we will keep talking
about the lawn.

## What we will not do

Treat the inside of your house for clover mites. They are dying in there
already; the only thing an interior application achieves is putting product
where WSU specifically says not to put it.

Tell you that you have an infestation. You have a lawn against a wall and two
warm weeks.

Sell a recurring program on the strength of a harmless lawn mite. If a
foundation band and an afternoon of sealing fixes it, that is what we will tell
you, and if you would like us to do that work we will quote it as what it is.

Or guess at the identification from a description. Photograph the wall and then
photograph a few of them close up against something white, send it in, and we
will tell you what you have — including, most of the time, that it is this and
it does not matter.
