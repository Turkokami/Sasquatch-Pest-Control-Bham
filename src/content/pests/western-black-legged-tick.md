## We do not treat for ticks

That is the verdict at the top of this page and it is worth putting in plain
words before anything else.

Sasquatch does not sell tick control. There is no tick program here, no yard
spray package, and no perimeter service aimed at this animal. If a company has
told you that a treatment around your house is how you deal with ticks in
Whatcom County, we would want you to hear a second opinion before you spend
anything on it.

The reason is not squeamishness about the work. It is that the exposure this
species actually produces happens away from the treated ground. You meet a
western black-legged tick on a brushy edge, in tall grass along a fence line, or
brushing past vegetation on the side of a trail. A product applied to a lawn does
not follow you to Chuckanut or up Galbraith, and a household that believes it is
covered because the yard was sprayed is worse off than one that checks itself
after a walk.

So this profile does what the library is for. It tells you what the animal is,
where you genuinely encounter it here, what the risk realistically looks like in
this corner of the state, and how to take one off correctly. Where you need
authority beyond that, the Washington State Department of Health is the source
we cite throughout, and it is where we would send you.

## What the animal is

*Ixodes pacificus* is a hard tick and an arachnid rather than an insect —
eight-legged as a nymph and adult, related to spiders and mites, as Washington
State University's veterinary hospital puts it.

Washington's Department of Health describes the western black-legged tick as
mainly found in western Washington, with populations also present along the
eastern slopes of the Cascades, inhabiting forested, oak savannah or brushy
areas. That places us squarely inside its range: this is the *Ixodes* of the
wet side of the mountains, and the tick most likely to be found on somebody who
walks in Whatcom County.

Size is the thing people get wrong. An unfed adult female is roughly the size of
a sesame seed, dark, with a reddish-brown body behind a darker shield. Adult
males are smaller and darker overall. A nymph is smaller again — around the size
of a poppy seed — and it is the nymph that matters most for disease transmission
in this genus, precisely because it is small enough to go unnoticed for the days
required.

A fed female is a completely different object. Engorged, she swells to something
like a small pale grape or a bean, gray-green rather than red-brown, and people
frequently do not recognize the two as the same animal. A great many "what is
this" photographs are engorged females.

## Which tick you are looking at, and why it matters

Washington's Department of Health illustrates four species that commonly bite
people here, and the distinctions are useful because the geography and the
diseases differ.

The **western black-legged tick** is the western Washington one, in forest, oak
savannah and brush, and it is the species associated with Lyme disease and
anaplasmosis in this state.

The **western dog tick** is described as primarily an eastern and parts of
southwest Washington animal, in woodland, medium grasses, shrubs and sunny
woodland edges, with adults on dogs and immatures on rodents and rabbits.

The **Rocky Mountain wood tick**, *Dermacentor andersoni*, is primarily an
eastern Washington species in woodland habitat, with a life cycle the state
describes as taking up to two to three years.

The **soft tick**, *Ornithodoros hermsi*, is a different creature entirely and is
worth knowing about because the exposure route is peculiar. The state ties it to
tick-borne relapsing fever and to sleeping in rustic, rodent-infested cabins in
mountainous areas. It feeds briefly at night rather than attaching for days, so
people are often bitten without ever finding a tick.

For somebody in Bellingham or Ferndale, the practical implication is that the
Rocky Mountain spotted fever material dominating national tick content is largely
about ticks that live on the other side of the Cascades. Washington's own data
records zero to three Rocky Mountain spotted fever cases identified in the state,
with most of those infected elsewhere.

## Where you actually meet one here

Habitat is the most useful thing on this page after the removal instructions,
because it tells you when to do a tick check and when not to bother.

CDC's surveillance guidance describes *Ixodes pacificus* as primarily a
woodland-associated tick, found in forested settings including their edges and
particularly along trails. Within that, the stages separate: nymphs sit in leaf
litter and on rocks, logs, tree trunks and fallen branches, while adults are found
on the tips of grasses and shrubs. Washington's health department gives the same
picture from the other direction — grassy, brushy or wooded areas, tall grass and
leaf litter, with some species questing from the edge of low-lying vegetation.

Questing is the behavior worth picturing. A tick climbs a stem to a height that
suits the host it is waiting for, holds its front legs out, and waits for
something to brush past. It does not drop from trees, it does not jump, and it
does not fly. It has to be touched.

That is why the encounter is so specific to where the vegetation touches you. The
narrow part of a trail where salmonberry and thimbleberry lean in. The deer trail
through a greenbelt. The fence line on a rural property where nobody has cut for
several seasons. The unmown margin where a pasture meets timber. Sitting on a log
at a lunch stop.

And it is why a mown lawn in open sun is a poor place to look for one. It is hot
and dry at the surface, it has no leaf litter, and it offers nothing to quest
from. Ticks are not absent from residential property in this county — a
brush-choked back corner against a greenbelt is habitat. But the pattern is an
edge, not a lawn, and a household that understands that will spend its attention
in the right place.

## The season runs backwards from what you expect

Most people carry a mental model of ticks as a hot-weather problem. For this
species that model is wrong in a way that matters locally.

CDC's surveillance summary gives adult *Ixodes pacificus* as active mainly during
the cool months of the year, from late fall to early spring. Nymphs are the
warm-season stage, host-seeking typically in May and June depending on location,
and larvae that hatch do not seek hosts actively until the following spring.

Put that against a Whatcom County winter and the implication is direct: our
climate does not produce a hard freeze that shuts anything down. A February hike
in the Chuckanuts on one of the mild bright days that follow a wet week is
squarely within adult activity. Somebody who does tick checks in July and never
in January is checking during the smaller of the two exposures.

Washington's health department describes ticks as most active in spring and
summer when they feed, and both statements are true — they describe different
stages. The state's spotted fever reporting, which runs April to October with a
June and July peak, tracks the eastern Washington *Dermacentor* season rather
than this one.

## The life cycle, which takes years

*Ixodes pacificus* runs through egg, larva, nymph and adult, and takes a blood
meal from a different host at each of the three feeding stages. The whole cycle
occupies several years rather than a season, and each stage feeds once.

CDC's guidance names the hosts. Larvae and nymphs feed on lizards, squirrels and
ground-dwelling birds, and on other mammals and deer. Adults come most commonly
from deer, and from other medium-sized and large mammals.

Two consequences follow that are worth holding.

The first is that a tick can only acquire a pathogen from a host that carries
one, and the small hosts that feed the immature stages are not equally competent
at carrying anything. That is a large part of why western infection rates in this
genus run lower than the figures published from the northeastern states, and why
importing a Connecticut risk estimate into a Washington conversation produces a
misleading answer.

The second is that a long, slow, multi-year cycle across wild hosts is not
something a property treatment interrupts. The animal is produced by the
landscape, and it arrives on you rather than emerging from your ground.

## The honest version of the Lyme risk here

This is the part of the page most worth reading carefully, because the public
conversation about Lyme runs to both extremes and neither is accurate for
Washington.

Washington's Department of Health states that the western black-legged tick is the
species that spreads Lyme disease in this state, and that those ticks are
primarily found in western Washington with a presence on the eastern slopes of
the Cascades. So the vector is here. That much is not in question.

The state also publishes the case numbers, and they are the figures that put it
in proportion. Each year ten to forty Lyme disease cases are reported among
Washington residents, and most result from exposures outside the state. Only zero
to seven confirmed cases per year are reported as acquired within Washington.

Both halves of that deserve saying plainly.

It is not zero. Locally acquired Lyme disease does occur in Washington, in single
digits, and somebody who spends a great deal of time in brush on this side of the
mountains is the person it happens to. Telling that person there is no Lyme here
would be false and would cost them a diagnosis.

And it is not the northeastern picture. A tick bite acquired on a Whatcom County
trail is not the same probabilistic event as a tick bite acquired in Connecticut,
and the difference is large enough that it should change how worried somebody is
while still leaving them careful.

On what to watch for, the state describes early illness as fever, chills,
headache, fatigue, muscle and joint aches and swollen lymph nodes, with the
bull's-eye rash occurring in seventy to eighty percent of infected people and
typically appearing three to thirty days after the bite. The rash is common but
not universal, so its absence does not settle anything. That conversation belongs
with a physician, and it goes better if you can say when you were bitten and
where you had been.

## The other things carried here

Washington's health department lists the tick-borne diseases known to be acquired
in this state: anaplasmosis, babesiosis, Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted
fever, tick-borne relapsing fever, tick paralysis and tularemia.

**Anaplasmosis** is the other one attached to this species in Washington,
typically transmitted by an infected black-legged tick.

**Rocky Mountain spotted fever** comes from American dog ticks and Rocky Mountain
wood ticks, which the state describes as common in eastern Washington, and most
Washington cases involve infection elsewhere.

**Tick-borne relapsing fever** is the soft tick and the rustic cabin.

**Tick paralysis** is the unusual one and it is worth knowing because it is
mechanical rather than infectious. A toxin in tick saliva produces ascending
weakness, and the state notes paralysis usually begins after the tick has fed for
four to seven days, with prompt removal usually leading to complete recovery. A
child who becomes unsteady on their feet after a camping trip is a situation in
which somebody should be looking through hair for a tick.

**Tularemia** is confirmed in both Washington people and Washington ticks, and it
is rare.

## Removing a tick, correctly

Washington's Department of Health gives the method, and it is short enough that
there is no excuse for improvising.

**Use fine-tipped tweezers.** Not fingers — the state advises against removing a
tick with bare hands. Not a tick key you do not know how to use, if fine-tipped
tweezers are within reach.

**Grasp the tick as close to the skin surface as possible.** You are aiming at
the mouthparts and the head, not at the body. Squeezing the swollen body is what
you are trying to avoid.

**Pull upward with steady, even pressure.** Straight out, slowly, and keep
pulling. The skin will tent up before it releases. That is normal and it takes
longer than people expect.

**Do not twist and do not jerk.** The state is explicit that this is what causes
the mouthparts to break off.

**Then clean the bite area and your hands** with rubbing alcohol or soap and
water.

If mouthparts do break off, the sensible course is to leave the skin alone rather
than digging at it, and to keep an eye on the site. Digging causes more tissue
damage than the fragment does.

Sooner is better than later at every point in this. A tick attached for a short
time has had less opportunity to transmit anything than one attached for days,
which is the whole argument for doing a check the same evening rather than
discovering something on the third morning.

## What not to do, and why

The folk remedies are not merely useless. Washington's health department warns
that hot matches, and coating the tick's body with petroleum jelly, soap or nail
polish, may cause it to release additional saliva, and that increases the chance
of transmitting disease.

That mechanism is worth understanding, because it reframes the whole category.
The idea behind smothering or burning a tick is to make it back out on its own.
What it actually does is stress an animal whose saliva is the delivery route for
anything it is carrying, while leaving it attached and salivating for longer than
a pair of tweezers would have taken.

So: no petroleum jelly. No nail polish. No dish soap on a cotton ball. No lit
match, no cigarette, no hot needle. No twisting it counterclockwise, which is a
persistent piece of internet advice with nothing behind it. And no waiting to see
whether it drops off.

Keeping the tick is reasonable. Washington State University's veterinary hospital
suggests preserving a tick in alcohol so it can help with diagnosis if illness
follows, and the same logic applies to a person: a tick in a sealed bag with the
date written on it is more useful to a physician than a recollection.

## What people bring us that is not a tick

A fair share of the photographs we get sent under this heading are something
else, and the misidentifications run in a consistent set.

**Engorged ticks** are the ones people fail to recognize as ticks at all, because
a fed female looks nothing like the flat dark thing in the reference photographs.
Smooth, pale, bean-shaped, with legs that look far too small for the body.

**Scale insects and plant galls** picked up off a coat or a dog after a walk
through brush. They sit still, they are the right size, and they are firmly
attached to whatever they were on.

**Ordinary spiders and mites** get sent in when they are small, dark and
eight-legged, which is a description that fits a great deal.

**Skin tags, moles and blood blisters** account for more calls than anybody
expects. The giveaway is legs, and the absence of them settles it.

**Fleas and bed bugs.** Neither has eight legs and neither embeds, but a person
who has found something on their skin does not always start from taxonomy.

A tick that is attached is embedded head-first and does not come off with a
brush of the hand. A tick that is not attached walks, slowly and deliberately,
and has all eight legs forward of the middle of its body. Those two observations
sort most of the confusion.

## Recording a bite, and what to tell a doctor

Because the diagnosis is clinical and the window matters, a small amount of
record keeping is worth more here than it is with most things in this library.

Write down the date the tick was found and, as best you can, where you had been
in the preceding days — not the town, but the terrain. Brushy trail, unmown
pasture edge, camping, a rural fence line. Photograph the tick before you remove
it if that is practical, and keep it afterward in a sealed bag or a little
alcohol with the date on it.

Photograph the bite site as it changes over the following weeks. The state's
description of the rash as appearing three to thirty days after the bite means
that a mark showing up a fortnight later is not too late to be relevant, and a
dated sequence is far better evidence than a memory.

Then, if illness follows, say the word tick early in the conversation. A
physician in western Washington sees very few locally acquired cases, which is a
consequence of the numbers above rather than a criticism, and the history you
supply is the thing that puts the possibility on the table.

## Prevention that is worth the effort

The measures that reduce exposure are behavioral, and they are cheap.

Walk in the middle of a trail rather than along the vegetated edge, because the
edge is where the questing happens.

Wear light-colored clothing, which makes a dark tick visible against it, and tuck
trousers into socks on a brushy walk. It looks ridiculous and it works.

Repellents belong on the label rather than in our advice, so read the product and
follow it. Clothing treatments and skin repellents are different products with
different instructions, and the difference matters.

Do a tick check the same day, and do it properly: hairline and scalp, behind and
in the ears, the back of the neck, armpits, waistband, groin, behind the knees.
Nymphs are poppy-seed sized and they go where clothing is tight and skin is thin.
A shower within a couple of hours helps, partly because unattached ticks wash off
and partly because it is when people actually look.

Put the clothes from a brushy walk through a hot dryer cycle. Dry heat is
effective on ticks and it takes no thought.

## Property, pets and what actually helps

If your land includes a brushy margin, the honest advice is habitat rather than
chemistry. Keeping grass cut where people and animals walk, clearing leaf litter
away from the areas that get used, cutting back the vegetation that leans over a
path, and maintaining a clear separation between the mown area and the wooded edge
all reduce contact with the places ticks quest from. None of that is a treatment
and none of it is something you buy from us.

For dogs, Washington State University's veterinary hospital gives the sensible
version: talk to a veterinarian about preventatives, and check the animal after
time in cover — around the neck, in and behind the ears, in the leg folds and
between the toes. Cats accumulate them on the face and neck. What goes on an
animal is a veterinary decision and we do not make it.

## Why this page exists at all

A pest control company that will sell you a treatment for anything you are worried
about is not a company doing you a service. Ticks are the clearest example we
have.

The demand is real, the anxiety is real, and there are products that could be
applied to a yard and invoiced. What there is not is a good argument that doing so
addresses the exposure, because the exposure is on the trail, at the fence line
and in the brush, and it comes home attached to a person or a dog.
