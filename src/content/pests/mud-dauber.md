## What you are looking at

A hard tube of dried mud, roughly the length and thickness of a finger, stuck
to a wall in a place that stays dry. Sometimes one. Sometimes a dozen laid side
by side and plastered over into a lump the size of a fist.

That is a mud dauber nest, and it is one of the most common things we are asked
about that needs the least done to it.

There is no swarm. There is no colony. There was one female, working alone, and
she is very probably finished.

## The three kinds, and the two you will actually meet

**The black-and-yellow mud dauber**, *Sceliphron caementarium*, is the one most
people find. Long thread waist, black with yellow markings and yellow legs. She
builds cells one at a time out of mud, sets them side by side, and then plasters
the whole group over so the finished nest is a rounded lump rather than a set of
visible tubes.

**The blue mud dauber**, *Chalybion californicum*, is metallic blue-black and
worth knowing about because she breaks the pattern. She largely does not build.
She finds an old black-and-yellow nest, carries water to it, softens the mud,
and refurbishes the cell for her own use. She is also the one with a reputation
for hunting widow spiders.

**The organ-pipe mud dauber**, *Trypoxylon politum*, builds the long parallel
tubes that look like pipes on a wall, and is primarily an eastern species. If
you have seen photographs of neat organ-pipe nests and are wondering why yours
looks like a blob instead, that is why.

## Where she builds

The requirement is a vertical or overhead surface that stays dry and sheltered,
and that narrows it down to a predictable set of places on any property.

Garage and carport walls. Porch and patio ceilings. The underside of eaves and
soffits. Exposed rafters and joists in an open outbuilding. Behind shutters. In
sheds, barns and pump houses. Under decks. Inside anything left open — and this
is the one that causes actual trouble, because "anything" includes stored
equipment, vent louvres, unused chimneys and the inside of a garden hose reel.

The pattern is always the same: out of the rain, out of the direct sun, on
something she can stick mud to.

Mud is the other half of it. She needs a source of wet soil to build with, so
properties near a ditch, a pond margin, a downspout outfall, an irrigated bed or
any patch that stays damp will see more of her than a dry lot will.

## Spiders, which is the interesting part

Every cell is a larder, and what is in the larder is spiders.

She hunts them, stings them into paralysis rather than killing them, and carries
them back. Paralysis is the point — a dead spider decays, a paralyzed one stays
fresh and cannot escape. Several go into each cell, an egg is laid on one, and
the chamber is sealed with mud before she starts the next.

The larva hatches, eats the provision, spins a cocoon and overwinters inside the
sealed cell. The following season it chews its way out, which is what leaves the
neat round holes in an old nest that people ask about. **A hole in a mud nest
means the occupant has already left.**

The blue mud dauber is the one worth mentioning specifically here. She is a
documented predator of widow spiders, which means she is dealing with the one
genuinely medically significant spider in this region and doing it for nothing.

## Why we usually tell you to leave it alone

Because there is nothing to treat, and the extension guidance says so about as
plainly as extension guidance ever says anything.

Purdue's advice for mud dauber nests found on a home is to ignore them or remove
them with a putty knife, on the stated grounds that these wasps do not defend
their nests and there is therefore little to no risk of stings. That is not a
hedge. That is a land-grant entomology department telling homeowners the answer
is a hand tool.

Our answer is the same, with one addition: if the nests are somewhere that
genuinely matters — inside a vent, blocking a weep hole, in a piece of equipment,
or on a wall somebody has to keep clean — remove them, and remove them
mechanically.

## How to take them off

Wet the nest first if you want less dust, then scrape.

A putty knife, a paint scraper or a stiff brush takes them off in seconds. On a
painted surface the mud usually leaves a faint outline that washes off with soap
and water. On unpainted timber it may leave a mark; on masonry it comes away
clean.

Wear a dust mask if you are clearing a lot of them in an enclosed space, not
because of the wasps but because you are making a cloud of dried mud and old
spider fragments.

The only judgment call is timing. A sealed cell with a developing wasp inside it
is a nursery; a cell with a neat exit hole is empty. If you would rather not
destroy the developing ones, clear the nests with holes now and leave the sealed
ones until after they emerge.

## Preventing them, honestly

There is not much, and we would rather say so than sell a plan.

You cannot exclude a wasp from an open carport ceiling. What you can do is
reduce the two things that make a spot attractive.

**Reduce the spiders.** She is there because the hunting is good. A carport with
heavy webbing in every corner is advertising. Knocking webs down regularly, and
adjusting exterior lighting so it stops concentrating the insects the spiders
are eating, does more than anything applied to the wall.

**Reduce standing mud.** Fixing a downspout that discharges against the wall, or
a bed that stays saturated, removes the building material from the immediate
area. This is a marginal gain and it is honest to say so.

Repainting a surface, hanging things over it, or fitting screening where the
nests are going inside equipment all work in specific cases. A residual spray on
an open ceiling does not, and it is a genuinely poor use of pesticide — an
exposed exterior surface, weathering, applied for an insect that is not a
hazard.

## What the old nests attract afterwards

This is the one part of the story that occasionally does matter, and it is why
clearing nests off an outbuilding is not purely cosmetic.

An abandoned mud cell is a ready-made cavity. Other insects move in — the blue
mud dauber being the obvious one, but also small solitary bees and, in some
cases, the carpet beetles and other scavengers that feed on the remains inside.
Where nests have accumulated on a wall in an enclosed space over several years,
clearing them removes a standing supply of cavities and debris.

That is a reason to clear an old accumulation. It is not a reason to treat
anything.

## Telling a mud dauber apart from what actually matters

The whole practical value of this page is helping somebody sort a harmless
solitary wasp from the two or three that are not, and the material each builds
with does most of the work.

**Mud tubes or a mud lump** — mud dauber. Solitary. No defense. Scrape it off.

**Dry grass stuffed into a gap or a window track** — grass-carrying wasp.
Solitary, harmless, and covered on its own page.

**Gray papery cells, open, hexagonal, on a short stalk** — a paper wasp. Social,
mildly defensive, and worth a conversation if it is above a door.

**A gray papery ball or a football-shaped enclosed nest** — bald-faced hornet or
aerial yellowjacket. Social, genuinely defensive, and this is the one to leave
alone and call about.

**A hole in the ground with steady two-way traffic** — most likely a
yellowjacket colony, which is the ground nest that puts people in hospital every
August in this county.

Five materials, five answers. If you can photograph what the nest is made of,
you have told us most of what we need to know before we arrive.

## Around this county

Mud daubers are ordinary here without being the thing anybody calls first. We
see them most in the places the rest of this page predicts: carports and open
garages, barns and equipment sheds on rural property, the underside of decks,
and pump houses.

Rural Whatcom County properties see more than town lots do, and the reason is
the mud rather than the buildings. Ditches, irrigated ground, stock water and
unpaved yards all supply the material, and the outbuildings supply the shelter.
A dairy or a berry operation with open structures and damp ground has both in
quantity.

They also turn up in exactly the awkward interior spaces that outbuildings
provide — inside a disused vent, inside stored equipment, inside a chimney
somebody stopped using. That is where clearing them is genuinely worth doing,
and it is usually part of a broader tidy rather than a job of its own.

## What this tells you about the building

Very little, and that is unusual for this site.

Most of what we write about here — a wasp in a window track, a rodent at a vent,
a beetle in soft framing — is a symptom of an opening or a moisture problem. A
mud dauber nest is not. She stuck mud to the outside of a sound wall because the
wall was sheltered. It says nothing about the condition of the building.

The one thing it does tell you is that there are enough spiders in that space to
be worth hunting, and if the spiders themselves are the thing bothering you,
that is the problem to look at rather than the wasp that came to eat them.

## What we will not sell you

We will not treat mud dauber nests. If we are on your property for something
else and there are nests on the carport ceiling, we will knock them down for you
and not put it on the invoice.

We will not describe them as aggressive or as an infestation. One female per
nest, no colony, no defense — the words would be false and this trade already
has a reputation for using them anyway.

We will not sell a preventive program against them, because there is no honest
one to sell. What reduces mud daubers is fewer spiders and less standing mud, and
neither of those is something to be billed monthly.

And if you tell us there is a wasp nest on the house and it turns out to be this,
we will say so and stand the visit down rather than finding something to do.

## The one place a mud dauber nest is genuinely a hazard

Everything above says this insect is harmless, and it is. The nest is a
different question in exactly one situation, and it is worth its own heading
because almost nobody knows about it.

**Gas barbecue burners.** The burner tubes on a gas grill are open horizontal
cavities in a sheltered spot that sits unused for months at a time. That is a
close match for what she is looking for, and a mud dauber will build inside one.
A blocked burner tube does not let gas reach the ports properly, so the gas
finds another way out and burns where it should not — behind the control panel,
under the firebox, or back along the tube.

If a grill has stood unused through a summer, look into the burner tubes before
lighting it. Most manufacturers put exactly this warning in the manual and
nobody reads it. A blocked tube usually shows as flames burning yellow instead of
blue, uneven heating, or fire visible somewhere other than the burner ports.

The same logic applies to anything else with an open tube that sits still:
generator and pump exhausts, vent louvres, unused chimney flues, irrigation
components, and equipment stored in an open shed over winter.

That is not a reason to treat the property. It is a reason to look inside the
tube before you use the thing.

## The year, and when the nests appear

She is active through the warm months, building and provisioning from roughly
early summer to the end of August in this county.

The nests people notice in autumn and winter are finished. The mud has dried
hard, the cells are sealed, and the occupants are cocoons waiting for spring.
Nothing is flying in or out, which is why a nest discovered in November feels
abandoned. It is.

That makes winter the easy time to clear an accumulation off an outbuilding, and
it is also the time when clearing destroys the developing generation. If that
matters to you, wait for the exit holes to appear.

## Finding one indoors

It happens, and it is not a sign that anything is nesting in the house.

A mud dauber that gets into a garage, a shop or a room will usually be at a
window trying to leave. She is not searching for anywhere to build indoors and
she is not going to sting somebody who does not grab her. A glass and a piece of
card puts her outside in about fifteen seconds.

The exception worth knowing is a nest built inside a structure that later opens
into living space — inside a wall vent, say, or in a disused flue. In that case
the emerging adults come out on the wrong side and turn up indoors in ones and
twos over a week or two in spring. That is a sealing job on the vent rather than
a treatment, and it fixes itself permanently.

## Clearing an outbuilding, practically

Where nests have built up over several seasons in a barn, a carport or a shop,
the job is worth doing properly once rather than picking at it.

Do it on a dry day, wet the nests down first with a garden sprayer so the mud
comes off in pieces rather than dust, and work from the top of the wall
downward. A stiff putty knife and a floor scraper on a pole between them reach
most of what a ladder would otherwise be needed for.

Sweep up rather than leaving the debris on the floor. Old cells contain dried
spider remains and cocoons, and leaving a pile of that in the corner of a shed
simply feeds the scavenging beetles mentioned above.

If the building has open rafters that get used every year, that is the moment to
decide whether it is worth it at all. Some outbuildings are cleaner for the
clearing. Some will look identical in two summers and the honest answer is to
leave them to it.

## A last word on the nests you cannot reach

Some mud dauber nests are simply out of reach — high in a barn, on a rafter over
stored equipment, on the underside of a bridge deck or a dock structure. If they
are not blocking anything and nobody has to walk under them, the honest answer is
to leave them there rather than hire access equipment to remove something
harmless. We would rather say that than quote for a lift.
