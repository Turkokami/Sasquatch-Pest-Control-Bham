## The wasp that ruins the end of summer

Ask people in this county when wasps are bad and you will get the same six-week
window every time: the back half of August through the middle of September. That
is not an impression. It is arithmetic, and the western yellowjacket is the
species doing most of it.

*Vespula pensylvanica* is a ground nester. It is the one that makes a lawn or a
bank dangerous without anything being visible, the one that arrives at an
outdoor table in numbers as the season turns, and the one behind most of the
sting incidents we hear about on residential property here. It is not the large
black and white insect people photograph in trees, and it is not the slim
long-legged wasp under the eaves. It is a stocky yellow and black insect, around
half an inch, that most people never notice until something goes wrong.

Everything that makes it a problem in late summer follows from a single-season
colony cycle, so that is where this page starts.

## The year, in one arc

A colony is annual. It exists for about seven months and then it is gone, and
each stage explains something people notice.

**Spring.** One mated queen who spent the winter under bark, in leaf litter or
tucked into a structure emerges and starts a nest by herself. She builds a small
paper envelope in a cavity, lays, and raises the first brood without help. At
this point the entire colony is one insect, and it is trivially dealt with by
anybody who happens to find it.

**Early summer.** The first workers emerge after about three weeks of larval
development and take over foraging and building. The queen stops leaving and
does nothing but lay. The colony is still small — dozens — and almost nobody
notices it.

**Midsummer.** Growth compounds. Workers hunt insects and spiders and carry
protein back to the larvae, which is genuinely useful work in a garden and one
reason we do not treat every nest we find. The nest enlarges downward as the
workers excavate the cavity around it.

**Late summer.** Peak. WSU Extension records western yellowjacket nests running
to as many as five thousand workers and fifteen thousand cells, and puts the
maximum in August and September. New queens and males are produced and leave to
mate.

**Autumn.** The colony collapses. Workers, males and the founding queen all die.
Only the newly mated queens survive, scattering to overwinter somewhere else,
and the abandoned nest decomposes underground over the winter.

## Why late-season aggression is a food story

This is the part worth understanding properly, because it explains the behavior
people find inexplicable — a wasp that ignored them in June landing on their
plate in September, and the same insect defending a hole in the lawn far harder
than it did two months earlier.

Through the growth phase the colony runs on an exchange. Workers bring protein
to the larvae, and the larvae secrete a sugary fluid that the adult workers feed
on. It is a closed loop, and while it is running the adults have a reliable
carbohydrate supply inside the nest and no particular reason to be anywhere near
your kitchen.

Late in the season the queen slows and then stops laying. Brood rearing winds
down, and with it the larval supply that had been feeding the adults. What is
left is the largest worker population the colony will ever have, with its easiest
food source gone, at exactly the moment natural insect prey is thinning out.

UC IPM puts the same point in one line: defensive behavior increases as colony
populations become larger while food becomes scarcer. That is the whole
explanation for late-summer yellowjackets. It is not that they turn nasty. It is
several thousand hungry insects with nothing left to eat at home.

Two consequences follow. They go looking for sugar — fallen fruit, bins, soda
cans, cider, anything sweet at an outdoor table. And they defend the nest
entrance with more of the colony and less tolerance than earlier in the year,
which is why the same mower passing the same spot is fine in July and a hospital
trip in September.

## What it looks like, and what it is not

Getting the species right changes the answer, and three insects account for
nearly all the confusion here.

**Western yellowjacket.** Three-eighths to five-eighths of an inch, bright yellow
and black, effectively hairless and shiny, short and stocky in build with a
blunt-ended abdomen. Flies fast and directly. Traffic in and out of a single
small hole in the ground, in numbers, is the signature nobody else produces.

**Aerial yellowjacket.** A close relative that builds an enclosed gray paper nest
hanging in a shrub, a tree or under an eave rather than nesting underground.
Similar coloring and similar size, so the insect alone will not separate them —
the nest does. If you can see a papery structure, it is not this page's species.

**European paper wasp.** Slimmer, with noticeably longer legs that trail behind
it in flight, and marked in a yellow and black pattern that fools people
constantly. It builds a small open comb of visible hexagonal cells with no outer
envelope, tucked under an eave or inside a pipe end, a mailbox, a grill or the
tubing of patio furniture. Considerably less defensive, and worth leaving alone
far more often than a yellowjacket is.

**Honey bees and bumble bees** get blamed for yellowjacket behavior every
September. Both are hairy and rounded; a yellowjacket is smooth and hard-looking.
More usefully, neither has any interest in your food. A wasp working its way
around a plate of pulled pork is not a bee, and it never has been.

Photograph it from a distance if you can. Size, hairiness and leg carriage
usually settle it, and a description over the phone gets there most of the time.

## Where the nests are

Underground, overwhelmingly, and the site selection is consistent enough to
predict.

**Abandoned rodent burrows** are the first choice, and every source we work from
says so. The cavity is already excavated, it is the right volume, it drains, and
its mouth is small enough to defend. A property with vole, mouse or mole history
is a property with candidate sites throughout.

**Banks, slopes and cut edges.** Sloped ground drains and offers a horizontal
entrance that stays dry through a wet spring. Ditch sides, driveway embankments,
the cut behind a retaining wall, the drop at the edge of a lawn.

**Voids beneath things sitting on the ground.** Sheds and outbuildings on skids
or blocks, deck framing near grade, porch steps, and anything that has been
parked on grass since April.

**Root plates and old stumps**, using the space left as a root system rots out.

**Rockeries and dry-stacked retaining walls**, in the gaps between stones.

**Structural voids.** Less common for this species than for the German
yellowjacket, but it happens — a colony that finds a gap in siding, a soffit
return or an unscreened vent will build in the wall cavity behind it, and that
changes the work substantially.

## Nest location decides the job

This is the practical heart of the page, and it is why the verdict at the top of
this profile is that it depends.

**A nest in open lawn or a bank** is the most straightforward version. The
entrance is reachable, the tunnel geometry is usually short, and material
delivered into the mouth is carried inward by the colony's own traffic. Depth is
the main variable — a shallow nest under turf is quick, and a colony a foot down
behind stone is not.

**A nest under a shed, a deck or a step** adds an access problem. The entrance
may be several feet from where the nest actually sits, and there may be more than
one way in.

**A nest in a wall void or a soffit** is a genuinely different job. Nothing about
it can be seen, the cavity may run the length of a building or connect to an
attic, and treatment has to distribute through a space nobody can inspect from
outside. It also carries the failure mode that makes wall nests worth taking
seriously: a colony shut in at its own doorway chews outward through whatever is
softest, and from inside a stud bay that is frequently half an inch of drywall
rather than siding and sheathing. People seal a hole in the cladding and then
have wasps in a bedroom. We cover that case in detail on
[wasp nest in a wall void](/services/wasp-control/wasp-nest-in-a-wall-void/).

**A nest nobody can get near** — up a bank in blackberry, inside a rockery that
would have to be dismantled — sometimes has no good treatment, and the honest
answer is to fence the area off and wait for the frosts.

So when we quote this work, what we are pricing is access and position rather
than the insect.

## Finding the entrance without getting hurt

If somebody has been stung on a lawn and nobody knows where it came from, the
entrance can be located safely from a distance.

Stand well back — twenty feet is plenty — on a warm afternoon with the light
across the area rather than in your eyes. Watch for the flight line. Foraging
workers do not wander home; they approach along a repeated path and drop into
one point. A couple of minutes of watching usually resolves it precisely.

Mark it from where you are standing. A cane pushed in several feet away, or a
bucket set down as a reference, is enough. Nothing goes at the hole itself.

Then keep everybody off it, tell whoever mows, and leave it entirely alone. Do
not probe, dig, uncover or shine a light into a suspected entrance to confirm
what you already know. That single action is how a manageable situation becomes
an ambulance.

## When leaving it alone is the right call

Not every ground nest is worth treating, and saying so is more useful than
selling every one.

A colony well away from mowing lines, paths, play areas, dog runs and anywhere
somebody works with power equipment is doing no harm to anyone. It spends the
summer removing flies, caterpillars and other soft-bodied insects from the
property, it is finished by the frosts, and the hole will not be a colony again.
In a far field corner or a woodland margin, leaving it is a legitimate choice
and often the better one.

Late in the season the same reasoning applies with more force, because the
waiting period is short. A marginal nest found in the second week of October is
weeks from ending on its own.

What flips the answer is proximity and exposure. A nest within reach of a mower,
a footpath, a gate, a shed people use, a vegetable bed or a patio needs dealing
with. So does any nest on a property where somebody carries an epinephrine
auto-injector for stings, where the calculation is not a judgment call at all.

## What treatment involves

Done properly, a ground nest is treated rather than dug out.

Protective equipment first, and real equipment — a suit, veil and gloves rather
than a hooded sweatshirt and sunglasses. Yellowjackets are efficient at finding
the gap in improvised clothing.

Timing is deliberate. Early morning or dusk puts the largest share of the colony
at home rather than out foraging, which matters both for the result and for what
happens to the workers that would otherwise return to an untreated nest.

The material goes into the entrance and the tunnel rather than being sprayed at
whatever is flying. A dust distributes through the cavity and onto insects moving
through it, and returning foragers carry it inward, which is how the part of the
colony nobody can reach is affected.

Then the site is left alone for a day or two rather than immediately disturbed.
Foragers keep arriving after the nest is dealt with, and digging it up on the
same visit is unnecessary and provocative.

Afterwards, where the cavity was a rodent burrow or a persistent void under a
structure, filling or closing it lowers the odds of the same spot being chosen
the following spring.

## The things people try that go wrong

There is a settled folk repertoire for ground nests and every item in it puts a
person directly over an angry colony at close range.

**Fuel poured down the hole** is dangerous, contaminates soil, and does not
reliably reach the nest chamber. It also requires standing at the entrance for
several seconds while it glugs out of the can.

**Boiling water** mostly cools before it gets anywhere useful, and it commits you
to being at the hole holding a full kettle.

**Hardware store aerosols** are built for a visible nest at a distance. Fired
into a tunnel they coat the top few inches while the colony below is untouched
and now thoroughly alerted.

**Blocking the entrance** does not seal a colony in. Yellowjackets excavate, and
where the nest sits under a structure the new exit they cut is sometimes on the
wrong side of a wall.

**Working at night with a flashlight in your hand.** The logic is sound and the
execution is not, because a light held at the entrance is a target, and you are
moving backwards over uneven ground in the dark.

## Stings, and when it stops being a pest control question

Yellowjackets do not lose the sting the way a honey bee does, so one insect can
sting repeatedly, and a disturbed ground colony recruits.

A normal reaction is sharp pain, redness and local swelling that builds over a
few hours and settles across a day or two. Cold, an antihistamine and a
painkiller cover it.

A large local reaction — swelling spreading well beyond the site, sometimes
across a whole limb — looks alarming and is usually not dangerous, though it is
worth mentioning to a doctor if it recurs.

What needs emergency care immediately is any systemic sign: difficulty
breathing, tightness in the throat or chest, swelling of the face or tongue,
hives spreading away from the sting, dizziness or faintness. That is a call to
emergency services rather than to a pest control company. A large number of
stings from a disturbed colony also warrants medical advice regardless of
allergy history, simply because of the total dose.

If a nest has just been hit, the response is to keep moving away rather than
stand and swat, get children and animals indoors first, and leave the mower where
it is until the following day. A disturbed colony stays agitated for hours.

## The one indoors in November, and the one in February

Two off-season sightings puzzle people every year, and both have the same
explanation.

In late autumn, after the colonies have broken up, single large yellowjackets
turn up indoors — at an upstairs window, in a roof space, behind a curtain, in
the folds of something stored in a garage. These are almost always newly mated
queens looking for somewhere to spend the winter. They got in through the same
gaps everything else uses: a soffit return, an unscreened gable vent, a gap at a
window frame, an attic hatch that never sealed. They are not the start of a nest
and there is no colony behind them.

Then, in a mild stretch in February or March, one appears again — sluggish,
blundering at a window on the warm side of the house. That is the same insect,
woken early by a few days of sun on a wall, and in most cases it will not
survive to found anything.

Neither situation calls for a treatment. A single overwintering queen in a
bedroom gets a glass and a piece of card, and the useful response to several of
them is exclusion work rather than a spray, because whatever route they used is
also open to the things that matter more.

Where it does become worth acting on is when the numbers are not one or two. A
steady trickle of wasps into the same room through the autumn suggests a void
that connects to living space, and that is worth looking at before spring —
partly because it will keep happening, and partly because a cavity comfortable
enough to overwinter in is a cavity a queen may simply build in rather than leave.

## Lowering the count next year

A few habits genuinely reduce how many nests a property carries, and none of
them involves treating anything.

**Walk the ground in late spring.** In April and May a founding queen is working
alone in a nest the size of a walnut. Dealing with one then is safe, quick and
prevents the September version entirely. Almost nobody does this, and it is the
single highest-value thing on the list.

**Deal with the rodents.** Burrows are the preferred cavity, so a property with a
managed rodent situation offers fewer ready-made sites. That work is
[rodent control](/services/rodent-control/) and it pays off twice.

**Keep the margins from going feral.** The untended strip between mown grass and
a fence or treeline is where nests survive undetected longest, because nothing
disturbs it until somebody finally decides to tidy it in August.

**Lift stored material off the ground** and move anything that has been sitting
in one place since spring.

**Take the food away from the living areas.** Lidded bins rinsed occasionally,
fallen fruit picked up, pet bowls not left out, drinks covered at outdoor tables
from mid-August. None of that touches the colony, and all of it reduces how many
foragers are around people.

**Close the structural gaps** in siding, soffits, vents and pipe ends, ideally in
late autumn or winter when nothing is nesting. That is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work and it closes the
same openings that let in rodents and cluster flies.

## Around the county

**Sudden Valley, Silver Beach and the lake margins.** Banks, root plates and
forest edge, on properties where people are outdoors in precisely the ground the
nests are in. The highest concentration of ground-nest calls we take.

**Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas.** Working land, and the exposure is
different — ditch banks, field margins, fence lines and equipment yards, with
somebody on a tractor or a brush cutter rather than a push mower. Livestock and
working dogs cannot be told where a nest is, which raises the stakes again.

**Older Bellingham neighborhoods.** Small lots, a lot of structure close to
grade, and enough gaps in century-old siding that the wall-void version turns up
here more than anywhere else.

**Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo.** Seasonal properties where a colony builds
all summer with nobody present to notice, and gets discovered by whoever opens
the place up in September.

**Barkley, Cordata and the newer hillside stock.** Fewer ground cavities in
young landscaping, more nests under decks and steps.
