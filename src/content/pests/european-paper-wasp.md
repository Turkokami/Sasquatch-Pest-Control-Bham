## A wasp that was not here when most of this county was built

There is a specific conversation we have with people who have lived in Whatcom
County for forty years, and it goes like this: there did not used to be this
many wasps under the eaves.

They are right, and the reason is not a change in the weather or in what
anybody's neighbor is doing with their bins. It is a species that was not here.

*Polistes dominula* is European. It reached North America in the 1970s, first
recorded near Boston, and moved west across the northern states over the
following two decades. WSU PestSense dates its introduction into Washington to
the late 1990s, and by the time WSU's own extension bulletin on yellowjackets
and paper wasps was written it was already being described as widespread and
abundant throughout the state. Colorado, for comparison, got its first record in
2001. This is a recent arrival everywhere west of the Rockies, and it has moved
fast.

What that means for a household here is that a genuinely common wasp on your
property may be one your parents never dealt with, and that most of the folk
knowledge about wasps in this county — where they nest, how they behave, what
provokes them — was formed before it arrived and does not describe it.

It also means the identification is worth getting right more often than usual,
because this insect looks like the species that causes the real trouble here and
behaves nothing like it.

## Why everybody calls it a yellowjacket

Because at a glance it is a yellow and black wasp about half an inch long, and
in this county that description belongs to something else.

WSU's bulletin puts it directly: this species is quite similar in color pattern
to yellowjackets, which is precisely why it gets reported as one. PestSense
describes it as black and yellow striped and roughly half an inch. On a phone
photograph taken from ten feet away, it is a yellowjacket.

Three things separate them, and none of them requires being close.

**The legs in flight.** This is the reliable one. Colorado State describes the
long hind legs of paper wasps trailing below the body in flight, and Penn State
puts the same observation as legs hanging in an extended fashion. Nothing in the
yellowjacket group does that. A wasp that appears to be dangling its back legs
as it cruises along a fence line is a *Polistes*, full stop.

**The build.** Penn State describes the waist as very thin. WSU's contrast is
between paper wasps, which are more slender with longer legs, and yellowjackets,
which appear short and stocky. Held side by side the difference is obvious;
described over the phone, "does it look narrow-waisted or does it look solid"
gets there most of the time.

**The antennae.** WSU Hortsense records this as the only paper wasp here with
bright orange antennae, which is a genuinely useful field mark on a clear
photograph and one almost nobody thinks to look for.

There is a fourth tell that is not about the insect at all, and in practice it
settles more cases than the other three combined: look at what it built.

## The open comb, which nothing else here makes

A yellowjacket nest is a sealed object. Whether it is underground, inside a wall
or hanging in a shrub, the comb is wrapped in a paper envelope and all you ever
see from outside is a structure and an entrance hole.

This species builds nothing of the kind. PestSense describes nests containing
only one layer of cells and not enclosed with paper as yellowjacket nests are.
The result hangs from a short stalk, cells downward, like a small upside-down
umbrella, and you can stand underneath it and count the cells and see the larvae
and see every adult sitting on the comb.

That is not a subtle difference. If you can see individual hexagonal cells, you
are not looking at a yellowjacket nest, and most of the advice you have read
about wasps does not apply to what you have.

Size is modest. WSU's bulletin describes these nests as often less than a
hundred cells and possibly four hundred cells and more at the top of the range,
while UC IPM puts paper wasp colonies generally at fifteen to two hundred
individuals. Colorado State's account of several dozen adults by the end of
summer, on a comb that has grown to a hundred cells or more, is a good
description of the typical version on a Bellingham porch.

Set that against the several thousand workers a mature ground-nesting colony
carries by September and the practical difference stops being academic.

## Where it puts the comb, and why that is the whole problem

If this wasp only built under eaves it would barely register as a pest. Its
actual site selection is what generates calls, because it favors small enclosed
cavities, and small enclosed cavities on a residential property are mostly
things people put their hands into.

WSU's bulletin lists roof shakes, meter boxes, bird houses, outdoor grills, mail
boxes, eaves, chairs, benches and shrubbery. Penn State adds attics and wall
voids, exterior light fixtures, parking meters and vehicles. Colorado State
notes dark cavities such as grills and pipes alongside the undersides of porch
decks and overhangs. PestSense's list is under lawn furniture, in light fixtures
and mailboxes, and under soffits and deck railings.

Read together, the local pattern is consistent, and here it is as somebody in
this county actually encounters it:

**The barbecue.** Open the lid in June to a comb the size of a coaster on the
underside. This is the single most common version we get called about.

**The open end of anything tubular.** Patio furniture legs, fence rail ends,
scaffold tube, a length of downpipe standing in a corner, the cut end of a
gate post.

**The underside of a deck or porch railing**, particularly a wide flat cap rail
that somebody's hand goes on as they come up the steps.

**Wall vents, dryer vents, gable louvers and unscreened crawl vents**, which
matter more than the others because they connect to the building.

**Meter boxes, junction boxes and outdoor light fittings.**

**Mailboxes, bird boxes and anything with a hinged lid** that stays shut most of
the week.

**A shed or garage door track**, a rolled-up awning, an upturned wheelbarrow, a
stack of empty plant pots, a boat cover.

The common factor is a small dark space with an opening, sheltered from the
rain, that nobody looks into between one season and the next. What makes it a
nuisance is not the wasp. It is that the wasp has excellent taste in exactly the
places people reach into without looking.

## Telling it from the paper wasp that was already here

Washington had paper wasps before this one arrived, and the native is still
around, so the comb under your eave is not automatically the introduced species.

The one you are most likely to meet is the golden paper wasp, *Polistes
aurifer*. WSU's bulletin separates it from yellowjackets on coloring — yellow
and dark brown rather than yellow and black — and puts workers at up to three
quarters of an inch and noticeably more slender. Its combs run to around two
hundred cells, with the largest probably under four hundred, and it builds under
eaves as well as in logs, under rocks, within shrubs and grass clumps, and
inside pipe. WSU also notes that it frequently overwinters in the attics of
homes, which is the source of a certain number of confused midwinter phone
calls about a large wasp in an upstairs room.

Three things separate the two in practice. Color: brown-toned bands read as
warmer and duller than the sharp black and yellow of the introduced species.
Antennae: bright orange on *dominula*, dark on the native. And size, since the
native runs slightly larger.

Does the distinction change what you should do? Almost never. Both are
open-comb wasps with small colonies and low defensiveness, and the honest
recommendation for both is usually the same. It matters for a different reason:
the introduced species has been displacing natives across its new range, Penn
State records evidence of an adverse impact on native *Polistes*, and part of
the mechanism is the head start described further down this page. Knowing which
comb is which is how anybody would ever notice that happening on a particular
property.

If it matters to you, photograph the antennae. That is the whole test.

## How defensive it actually is

Considerably less than the insect it resembles, and being straight about this is
most of the value of the page.

Colorado State describes the European paper wasp as relatively non-aggressive,
and adds the observation that matters most: stings from this species occur
almost exclusively when nests are accidentally disturbed. Penn State's version
is that they detect a threat at twelve to twenty feet but do not typically
attack unless somebody is very close. UC IPM's general statement about paper
wasps is that most species are relatively unaggressive and rarely sting people.

So the profile of a sting from this wasp is not somebody being pursued across a
lawn. It is somebody lifting a grill lid, gripping a rail, opening a meter box
or pulling a tarp off a stack of furniture, and finding out that way.

There is a caveat and PestSense states it plainly: more docile than yellowjackets
unless startled, at which point they will react fiercely. That is a fair
description. This is not a stingless insect and a comb held in one hand is a bad
place for a face. But the behavior is reactive rather than territorial, the
defended radius is small, and the colony is a few dozen insects rather than a
few thousand.

The practical consequence is that a comb ten feet up under a soffit, or on the
underside of a shed roof, or in a shrub away from a path, is very unlikely to
produce a sting in an entire season. The same comb inside the handle recess of a
sliding door is a matter of time.

## The head start, and the one place the annual rule bends

Every wasp colony in this county is annual. A queen that mated the previous
autumn starts alone in spring, the colony builds through summer, new queens and
males leave to mate, and the rest of it — workers, males, the founding queen —
does not survive the cold. Only mated queens carry over, sheltering somewhere
else entirely.

That holds for this species too. But there is a wrinkle, and it is the one place
in this whole subject where the received rule needs a footnote, so it is worth
stating carefully.

The rule everywhere else is that the nest is single-use. WSU's bulletin says so
of yellowjackets without qualification: the abandoned nests decompose over the
winter and are not used again. For this species the picture is different in one
respect. Penn State records queens of *Polistes dominula* establishing colonies
earlier than native species partly by taking over the previous year's structure,
and WSU notes that paper wasps may build new combs adjacent to old ones. WSU
PestSense's phrasing is that queens occasionally reuse old structures although
they typically construct new nests.

So: the colony still dies. Nothing overwinters in the comb. But the paper is
capable of outlasting the season in a sheltered cavity, and a founding queen the
following April may adopt it or build alongside it rather than starting from
nothing.

That is not a technicality. It is one of the mechanisms by which this species
got established so quickly, because Penn State connects the early founding to a
head start — workers on the wing before the native paper wasps have their
colonies going, and a broader prey range than the natives, with development from
egg to adult in as little as forty days.

It also gives you something to act on. A comb removed in winter, from a cavity
that is then closed, is a site that does not get a running start in spring. That
is the single most effective thing anybody can do about this wasp, and it
involves no treatment of any kind.

## Why the identification changes the answer

Here is the reason this profile exists.

Somebody sees a yellow and black wasp going in and out of a hole in the side of
their house and calls it a yellowjacket. Under that name, the answer is serious:
a colony in a cavity that cannot be seen, potentially several thousand insects
by September, a real hazard if the entrance is sealed while it is alive, and a
job for somebody with the right equipment.

Under the correct name, the same situation is usually a few dozen wasps on an
open comb four inches inside a vent, ending on its own in a couple of months,
that has never bothered anybody and probably will not.

Those two answers cost very different amounts of money. The identification is
what stands between them, and it is free.

The reverse error happens too and is worth guarding against. A genuine
yellowjacket colony in a wall gets waved off as "just paper wasps," the entrance
gets caulked in August by somebody being helpful, and the colony chews inward
through drywall. When there is any doubt about which one you have, the safe
assumption is the yellowjacket, and the safe action is to do nothing to the
opening until somebody has looked.

## What it does for you

Not nothing, and it belongs on the ledger.

Colorado State describes these wasps preying on caterpillars and sawfly larvae
and acting as a natural control on many kinds of yard and garden insects. Penn
State's account is broader still — a wide prey range across several insect
orders, which is precisely the trait that gave the species its advantage here.
That breadth is also the source of the ecological concern about it, since
somebody's cabbage worms and somebody's butterfly caterpillars are the same
resource, and the same source records evidence of an adverse impact on native
*Polistes*.

For a household deciding what to do about a comb under a shed eave, though, the
practical fact is that it is removing insects from your garden all summer at no
charge, and a great many of these nests are worth exactly the amount of
attention required to walk around them.

## When it is genuinely worth dealing with

The verdict at the top of this page is that treatment is rarely warranted, and
the exceptions are specific rather than vague.

**Anywhere hands go.** A door handle recess, a gate latch, a stair rail, a
mailbox, a grill, a garden tool store, the tubing of the chairs somebody is
about to set out.

**Vents and openings that connect to living space.** Not because the wasp is
dangerous but because a cavity a queen finds attractive is worth closing anyway,
and because adults working their way inward through an unsealed penetration turn
up at an upstairs window.

**Doorways, and any comb over a threshold** where the flight path crosses
somebody's route several times a day.

**Play areas, and low nests in shrubs** at the height a child moves through.

**A household where somebody carries an epinephrine auto-injector for stings.**
That removes the calculation, the same as it does for every other species in
this group.

**Numbers.** One comb under a soffit is unremarkable. Eleven of them around one
elevation, which happens on properties that suit the species, is a different
conversation and usually a sealing conversation rather than a spraying one.

Everywhere else — a shed roof, a fence corner, the underside of a deck nobody
goes under, a hedge away from the path — leaving it is the right answer and it
costs nothing.

## What we would actually do

For an accessible comb in a bad position, the work is small. The right hour is
early morning or after dusk, when the adults are on the nest and slow rather
than out foraging, and the comb comes off its stalk. Where there is no cavity
involved, that is frequently the whole job.

For a comb inside a cavity — a vent, a pipe end, a meter box — treating the
comb is the easy half and the useful half is closing the cavity afterwards so
the site is not chosen again. Screen the vent in metal, cap the pipe, fit the
box properly. That is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work, it holds for
years, and it is the reason this species carries a *rarely-warranted* verdict
rather than a treatment one.

The best time to do all of it is the cold half of the year. Nothing is on the
comb, nothing is defending anything, old paper comes out of the cavities that
hold it, and the openings get closed before a queen is looking. WSU Hortsense's
own suggestion for discouraging this species is removing the nests during
winter, and it is the cheapest advice on this page.

Where a property has a genuine concentration of nests we will walk the
elevations and deal with the sites rather than the insects, because on this
species the sites are the problem.

## What we will not sell you

**A perimeter spray for paper wasps.** They do not walk your siding; they fly to
a cavity. Wetting the wall does very little and it does it to a great many
insects that were not the point.

**A recurring plan for a seasonal comb.** A nest under an eave is a discrete
thing that ends in autumn.

**Removal of every comb on an acre.** On rural property here that is a full day
of work against an insect that is mostly earning its keep, and we would rather
do the four that matter.

**Treatment of a comb in November.** It is finished. Take it down if you like
the look of the eave better without it.

**A yellowjacket price for a paper wasp job.** They are not the same work and
should not be quoted as though they were.

## Around the county

**Older Bellingham housing.** Porch rails, shakes, meter boxes and a century of
odd penetrations in the siding. The highest density of nests per property that
we see, and the highest proportion of them that can safely be ignored.

**Newer subdivisions in Cordata, Barkley and King Mountain.** Vinyl trim, vented
soffits, gas grills on small patios and a lot of tubular outdoor furniture.
Fewer nests, more of them in places people touch.

**Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and the working properties.** Equipment yards,
implement sheds, gate posts, stacked pipe and fence rails. Nests by the dozen,
almost all of them harmless, and the ones that matter are on machinery controls
and around stock handling.

**Blaine, Birch Bay and the seasonal homes.** Everything shut for months is
everything a founding queen wants, and the discovery happens when the covers
come off.

**Sudden Valley and the wooded lots.** More natural nest sites in the
surrounding cover, which paradoxically means fewer of them on the buildings.
