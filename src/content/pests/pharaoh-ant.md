## The one where the obvious response backfires

Most ant problems tolerate a bit of improvisation. Somebody sprays the trail, it
helps or it does not, and the situation is no worse for the attempt.

Pharaoh ants are the exception, and it is the single most important thing on
this page. **Insecticide applied to a pharaoh ant colony causes it to bud.** The
colony splits, queens leave with workers, and new nests establish elsewhere in
the same building. University of Minnesota Extension states it plainly:
insecticides can cause pharaoh ants to bud, creating new colonies.

So the householder who buys an aerosol and does what seems obvious turns one
nest into several, distributed through voids where they are harder to reach.
The problem gets worse in a way that is not immediately visible, and the visible
result — no ants for a few days — reads as success.

That is why this page leads with treatment rather than with identification.

## Recognizing them

Small, yellow, and all one size.

**Size and color.** About two millimeters, yellow or light brown, sometimes
with a darker abdomen. Small enough that a single ant on a counter reads as a
speck.

**Monomorphic**, meaning every worker in the colony is the same size. A trail
containing obviously large and obviously small workers is not this species — it
is more likely a carpenter or thatching ant.

**Many queens.** Colonies possess many queens rather than one, which is the
structural fact behind everything else. There is no single queen to kill and no
single point of failure.

**No swarming.** They produce winged forms within the colony, but they do not
fly and mate on the wing the way most ants do. Mating happens inside the nest.

They are an introduced species, originally from Africa, and they are now found
worldwide inside heated buildings.

## Budding, and why it changes everything

Nearly every ant most people meet reproduces the same way: winged reproductives
leave on a mating flight, mated queens land, shed their wings, and try to found
a colony alone. Most fail. That is why ordinary ant colonies are separate,
territorial, and beatable one at a time.

Pharaoh ants do not do that. **Additional colonies are produced by the splitting
of large mature colonies** — a group of queens and workers simply walks away and
sets up somewhere else in the same structure.

Four consequences follow, and they define the job.

**There is no single nest.** A pharaoh ant population in a building is typically
many small nests connected by trails, in wall voids, behind cabinetry, in
insulation, around pipe runs, inside appliances, behind baseboards.

**Nests are in inaccessible places by preference.** Warmth and moisture, close to
food, out of sight.

**Stress accelerates spread.** Anything that makes a nest site unpleasant —
insecticide, heat, disturbance — prompts the exact behavior you are trying to
prevent.

**They do not need to leave the building.** A colony can persist and multiply in
one structure indefinitely without any outdoor stage at all.

## What actually works

Bait, patiently, with nothing repellent used anywhere near it.

The principle is simple. Foraging workers take bait back and share it by
trophallaxis — mouth-to-mouth transfer — which is how the queens, who never
leave the nest, receive it. A bait that kills the forager too quickly never gets
home. A slow-acting bait moves through the colony and reaches the queens.

That is why bait choice and placement are more skilled work here than on most
ant jobs, and why extension guidance notes that retail baits frequently
disappoint while professionals have access to products and experience that
work.

The rest of the method is discipline:

**No repellent sprays**, anywhere the ants are active or might travel. This
includes the perimeter spray a customer may have bought separately, and it
includes cleaning products applied enthusiastically to trails.

**Bait on the trails**, in enough placements to serve a dispersed population,
replenished as it is taken.

**Leave it alone.** Wiping trails away destroys the recruitment that is
delivering the bait.

**Expect weeks.** The population declines as queens die and brood fails. It does
not stop on a Tuesday.

**Follow up.** A pharaoh ant job that is not revisited is a pharaoh ant job that
has not been finished.

## What they eat, and why that matters

The extension list is worth quoting because its range is the point: bread,
cookies, cakes, sugar, syrup, liver, bacon and dead insects.

Sweet, greasy and protein, all of it, and preferences shift with the colony's
needs. A population that ignored a sugar bait last month may take it now, and
the reverse.

Practically that means a job may run several bait types, and that a bait being
ignored is information rather than failure. It also means the sanitation side
matters more than usual: crumbs, spills, pet food left down, a bin that does not
close, and grease behind an appliance are all competing with whatever we have
placed.

Where a customer can reduce the competition, the bait works faster. That is a
genuine and unglamorous part of the job and we will say so.

## Where they turn up around here

Pharaoh ants are an indoor species at this latitude — heated, occupied buildings
with year-round warmth. They do not overwinter outdoors in Whatcom County.

That focuses the risk on particular buildings rather than particular
neighborhoods:

**Apartment blocks and attached housing**, where a population moves between
units through shared voids and service penetrations. This is the same
shared-structure problem described on our multifamily pages, applied to an
insect that specializes in exploiting it.

**Healthcare and care settings**, where they are a long-standing recognized
problem — they forage into supplies, dressings and equipment, and the
sensitivity of the environment limits what can be applied.

**Food premises and commercial kitchens**, for warmth, moisture and continuous
food.

**Any building that has received goods, equipment or furniture from an infested
one.** That is the usual route in.

## The multi-unit problem

If pharaoh ants are in an attached building, unit-level treatment is close to
pointless and it is worth being blunt about that before anybody spends money.

The population is not respecting the walls. It occupies shared voids and moves
between units, and a single treated apartment is recolonised from the ones
either side. Worse, treating one unit with anything repellent pushes the
population toward the neighbors.

The version that works is coordinated: the affected units and those adjoining,
baited on the same schedule, with the building's shared spaces included and the
service penetrations between units addressed. That needs the owner, the
association or the property manager to agree it.

We will say so on the first visit and put it in writing so it can be taken to
whoever has authority, because the alternative is a resident paying repeatedly
for a result nobody can deliver at that scope.

## Telling them from the others

Four small ants get reported the same way and the differences matter.

**Odorous house ant** — 2.5 to 3 mm, brown to black, and a strong rotten
coconut smell when crushed. Much the commonest answer in this county. The smell
test settles it.

**Thief ant** — 1.3 to 1.8 mm, yellowish to brown, with an unusually long
two-segmented antennal club. The closest to a pharaoh ant on size and color,
and the antennal club is the separator.

**Ghost ant** — dark head and thorax with a pale, nearly translucent gaster and
legs. Very rare here.

**Pavement ant** — 2.5 to 3 mm, with fine parallel grooves on the head and
thorax, and piles of excavated sand on paving.

Two of those — pharaoh and ghost — bud, and must not be sprayed. The others do
not. That is the practical reason we ask for a specimen rather than working from
a description on the phone.

## Why we would rather see a sample

The characters that separate these species are structural and small: antennal
segment counts, the shape and length of the antennal club, the number of nodes
in the waist, whether the surface is sculptured.

None of that survives a phone photograph of a two-millimeter insect. A few
workers in a sealed bag or under a strip of clear tape settles it in about a
minute under a lens.

We do not charge to identify an ant. On this group in particular, being right
before anything is applied is worth considerably more than being quick.

## What a job looks like from your side

**A first visit that mostly involves looking.** Trails traced, nest locations
inferred from where the trails go, food sources noted, and the building's
construction taken into account — especially in attached housing.

**A conversation about what not to do.** No sprays, no bleach on the trails, no
retail ant products, and a request to leave placements alone.

**Bait placed and left.**

**A wait.** This is the part people find hardest, because activity may briefly
appear to increase as recruitment builds around the bait. That is the treatment
working, not failing.

**Return visits** to replenish, reassess and confirm.

**An honest end point.** Pharaoh ants are gone when they are gone, and we would
rather keep coming back until that is true than declare victory on a schedule.

## What we will not sell you

We will not spray a building for pharaoh ants. If somebody quotes you a
perimeter treatment or a knockdown application for this species, they have
either misidentified it or they are about to make it worse, and either way that
is worth knowing before they arrive.

We will not treat one apartment in an infested block and describe the job as
done.

We will not promise a single-visit result. The biology does not allow it and the
promise is the thing that gets sold most often.

And we will not identify an ant as a pharaoh ant without seeing one, because the
treatment plan that follows is materially different from the one for the ant you
almost certainly have instead.

## Why hospitals care about this ant specifically

Pharaoh ants have a place in the medical and institutional pest literature that
almost no other ant has, and the reasons are worth setting out because they also
explain why the species is taken seriously in ordinary buildings.

They forage very widely relative to their size and they follow moisture. In a
clinical setting that has meant ants found in sterile supplies, in dressings, in
drip lines and in equipment — places nothing is supposed to reach. Because they
move between waste areas and clean areas along the same trails, they are treated
as a contamination route rather than merely a nuisance.

The other institutional problem is that the obvious response is unavailable.
Occupied wards, food preparation areas and sensitive equipment sharply limit what
can be applied, and the one thing that would be tempting — a fast knockdown
spray — is the thing that makes this species multiply.

For a Whatcom County reader the point is not that a house is a hospital. It is
that the species has been studied hardest in the setting where getting it wrong
costs the most, and everything learned there is what informs how it should be
handled anywhere.

## The apartment problem, in detail

Attached housing is where this ant does its worst work in an ordinary community,
and the mechanics are worth understanding by anybody in a building with shared
walls.

The colony is not respecting unit boundaries. It occupies the shared volume —
party wall cavities, service chases, ceiling voids, the runs around plumbing —
and treats each apartment as a foraging opportunity rather than a territory.

That produces a pattern residents find baffling. One unit reports ants; that
unit is treated; the ants disappear for a fortnight; they reappear in the unit
next door, then come back to the first. Nobody has done anything wrong and
everybody is frustrated.

The reason is that unit-level treatment against a budding species in a shared
structure is close to the worst possible combination. It stresses part of the
population, the population moves, and the movement is into the neighbors.

What resolves it is a building-level program agreed by whoever has authority:
the affected units and their neighbors baited on the same schedule, common
areas included, and the penetrations between units addressed so the shared
volume becomes less connected than it was.

That is a harder thing to organize than a single service call. It is also the
only version that ends, and we would rather say so at the start than take
payment from one resident for a job the building will undo.

## What to do before we arrive

Small things, and they genuinely shorten the work.

**Stop cleaning the trails.** This feels wrong and it is important. The trail is
the delivery route for the bait, and wiping it away every evening resets the
recruitment we are relying on. Leave the ants their line.

**Stop spraying anything.** Including household cleaners applied enthusiastically
where the ants run, and including any retail ant product already bought. Every
one of those is a repellent as far as the colony is concerned.

**Take the competition away.** Food in sealed containers, spills dealt with
including behind and under appliances, pet food not left down overnight, bins
that close, and grease cleaned off the back of a cooker. Bait competes with
everything else on offer, and a clean kitchen makes it the best thing available.

**Note where you see them and when.** A rough map of trails and times is
genuinely useful and takes a week of paying attention rather than any effort.

**Tell us what has already been applied.** Not to apportion blame — it changes
where bait will and will not be accepted, and we would rather know than discover
it three weeks in.

## Around this county

We do not see pharaoh ants often here, and that is worth saying plainly rather
than implying a threat that is not there. The common indoor ant in Whatcom
County is the odorous house ant by a wide margin.

Where the risk sits is in particular building types rather than particular
towns. Multifamily housing across Bellingham, Ferndale and Lynden. Care and
healthcare settings. Commercial kitchens and food premises. Any heated,
occupied building with continuous warmth, moisture and food, and any building
that has taken delivery of goods, equipment or second-hand furniture from
somewhere already infested.

That last route is the one nobody watches. Pharaoh ants move with things, not
across gardens. A donated sofa, a batch of returned equipment, a pallet of
stock, a box of files from another site — these are how a building acquires
them, and it is why a first infestation frequently appears in a store room or a
receiving area rather than a kitchen.

For a property manager or a facilities lead, that suggests a cheap habit: when
something bulky and soft arrives from another building, look at it before it
goes into a wall-lined room.

## The one thing to take away

If you remember nothing else from this page, remember the sequence.

Tiny yellow ants indoors, all the same size, no swarming, colonies that never
seem to end — do not spray them. Do not let anybody else spray them. Get one
identified, and if it is this species, accept that the job is bait and weeks
rather than a can and an afternoon.

Everything expensive about a pharaoh ant infestation follows from somebody
taking the fast option first.

## A note on what "gone" means

Because this species declines rather than stops, it is worth agreeing in advance
what the end of the job looks like.

Activity falling away is the first sign, and it is not the finish. A budding
population under bait pressure can go quiet for two or three weeks and then show
a small trail somewhere new, which is a remnant nest rather than a failure of
the treatment.

So the standard we work to is not "no ants on the day of the last visit". It is
a period with no activity across the whole area that was affected, confirmed on
a return rather than assumed, with monitoring left in place where the building
warrants it.

That takes longer to say than "we got them", and it is the difference between a
job that finished and a job that stopped being visited.
