## The one in your house is probably not a wolf spider

Start here, because it saves most people the rest of the page.

"Wolf spider" has become the name people reach for whenever a spider is big,
hairy, fast, and on the floor rather than in a web. The Burke Museum, which
holds one of the larger arachnology collections in the country, takes the
unusual step of saying so outright: people associate the word wolf with big and
hairy, but the family is defined by the arrangement of its eyes, not by the
impression it makes. And then the part that matters — no North American wolf
spider is part of the house spider fauna, because active hunting works outdoors
and does not work on a hallway floor.

In this county the large, fast, hairy spider indoors is a
[giant house spider](/pest-library/giant-house-spider/) nine times in ten, and
the tenth is usually one of its relatives. WSU reports that in the Puget Sound
region giant house spiders outnumber hobo spiders and are far more frequently
encountered, and they are described as ideally adapted to life indoors — which
is precisely what a wolf spider is not.

None of which means wolf spiders are absent. They are here, they are native,
and they are common. They are simply outside.

## What a wolf spider actually is

A hunter that gave up on webs.

Most spiders invest in silk and wait. The family Lycosidae went the other
direction: they hunt on foot, mostly at night, running prey down on the ground
rather than trapping it. Everything about the animal follows from that. The legs
are long and strong rather than fine. The body is drab and mottled so the
outline breaks up against soil and leaf litter. The eyes are enormous by spider
standards, arranged in three rows — four small ones in a line low on the face,
two large ones above them, two more set back on top of the head. That eye
arrangement is the actual definition of the family, and it is the only thing
that reliably settles the question.

It is also the easiest field character there is, if you are willing to go out at
night. Hold a flashlight at the level of your own eyes and sweep a lawn. The
large forward-facing eyes throw the light back, and a mown lawn that looked
empty turns out to be covered in small green points. Most people who try this
once never again ask whether they have wolf spiders in the garden.

OSU Extension puts the size range across the family at roughly half an inch to
two inches. That upper figure is doing a lot of work in people's imaginations,
and it belongs to species that are not ours. The Burke Museum's correction is
worth having: most wolf spider species are relatively small, with bodies around
a centimeter or less. A one-centimeter body is a spider most people would walk
past without comment.

## Wolf spiders in Washington, and where they really live

Ground level, outdoors, in the parts of a property nobody looks at.

Washington has a good range of native wolf spiders. The ones encountered around
houses here turn up in lawns, in bark mulch and rockery, in long grass at a field
edge, under boards and flower pots, in leaf litter along a fence line, and on
the open ground of a gravel driveway on a warm evening. They are animals of the
surface — they do not climb much, they do not build, and they do not aggregate.

That last point is the useful one. Wolf spiders do not occur in numbers inside a
structure, because the thing that supports them is open hunting ground. If you
are finding several large spiders indoors, you are not finding wolf spiders. You
are finding funnel weavers, and that is a different conversation with a
different answer, covered on
[spiders appearing every fall](/services/spider-control/spiders-appearing-every-fall/).

A wolf spider that comes inside is a single animal that walked through a single
gap, usually under an exterior door with a worn sweep. It will not thrive, it
will not breed there, and it will usually be dead or gone within a week or two
whether or not anybody does anything.

## Are wolf spiders dangerous?

No, and the regional authorities are not shy about it.

The Burke Museum states plainly that North American wolf spiders are not
dangerous to humans. The Washington State Department of Health, listing the
spiders of medical significance in this state, names two — the black widow,
which is mainly an eastern Washington animal, and the yellow sac spider. Wolf
spiders are not on that list and have never been on it.

What they have instead is presence. A two-inch leg span moving at speed across a
kitchen floor at eleven at night produces a genuine physical reaction in people
who are not frightened of spiders in principle, and that reaction is not
something to be argued out of. It is the reason this species has a reputation
out of all proportion to anything it does.

The honest framing is the one we use for most of this library: the animal is
harmless, the startle is real, and the two facts do not cancel. If a spider in
the house is costing you sleep, that is a legitimate reason to have something
done, and nobody here will tell you to simply get over it. But the work that
follows should be sealing and tidying, not spraying the inside of a house for an
animal that lives in the lawn.

## Can a wolf spider bite you?

Physically yes. Practically, almost never.

A wolf spider is big enough to break skin, unlike most spiders people worry
about. What it lacks is any motivation. OSU Extension describes them as shy, and
notes they run when disturbed — which matches what we see. A hunting spider's
entire survival strategy depends on not being caught by something bigger, and a
human hand is something bigger.

The bites that do happen are nearly all defensive and nearly all the same
scenario: the spider ended up inside clothing, bedding, a work glove or a boot,
and got pressed against skin. The result is a sharp pinch, local redness, and
some swelling for a day or so. OSU notes some people react more than others,
which is true of any insect or spider bite and is about the person rather than
the venom.

What a wolf spider bite does not do is cause a spreading, ulcerating wound. That
belief comes from the brown recluse literature, it has been transplanted onto
every large brown spider in North America, and it is covered properly on the
[hobo spider](/pest-library/hobo-spider/) page, where the same false claim did
real damage for twenty years.

If a bite blisters, spreads, or you feel ill, see a doctor — not because the
spider is likely to be the cause, but because the things that actually do that
are worth catching early. Skin infections are common. Confirmed spider bites are
rare.

## Wolf spider vs brown recluse

A comparison people make constantly, and it has an easy answer in this state.

Brown recluses are not established in Washington. Not rare here, not
occasionally here — not established, which is the word the entomologists use and
the one the
[spiders in Washington state guide](/guides/spiders-in-washington-state/)
explains at length. So in Whatcom County the question is not which of the two
you have. You do not have one of them.

That said, the two animals do not look alike once you know what to check, and it
is worth knowing:

- **Hair.** A wolf spider is visibly hairy. A recluse is smooth and matte.
- **Markings.** A wolf spider is striped or mottled, usually with a lengthwise
  band down the back. OSU Extension points out wolf spiders lack the
  violin-shaped marking behind the head that gives the recluse its other name.
- **Build.** A wolf spider is stocky with thick legs. A recluse is spindly and
  long-legged for its body.
- **Size.** A recluse is small — well under an inch of body. A wolf spider that
  made someone look twice is generally bigger.
- **Eyes.** A recluse has six eyes in three pairs. A wolf spider has eight, in
  the three-row arrangement described above.

If you are in Texas or Missouri, that list is useful. Here, the first sentence
of this section already settled it.

## Wolf spider vs giant house spider

This is the comparison that actually matters in Whatcom County, and almost
nobody makes it.

- **Where it lives.** The wolf spider is outdoors on the ground. The giant
  house spider lives in garages, basements and crawlspaces, and WSU describes it
  as ideally adapted to life indoors.
- **Web.** None at all on a wolf spider. A flat sheet with a funnel at one edge
  on the giant house spider, usually low and dusty in a corner.
- **Build.** Stocky, hairy and striped against long-legged, browner and less
  obviously hairy.
- **Eyes.** Three rows with two very large ones against eight small ones in a
  roughly even arrangement.
- **Numbers.** A wolf spider indoors is a stray and stays one. Giant house
  spiders will happily occupy an undisturbed garage in quantity.
- **The bathtub.** A wolf spider rarely. A giant house spider constantly.

That last line is the giveaway. WSU describes giant house spiders indoors as
usually found on the floor or trapped in sinks and bathtubs after looking for
water. If you have scooped a big spider out of a bath, you have almost certainly
met a giant house spider, whatever the internet told you to call it.

## The egg sac, and the spiderlings on her back

The one piece of wolf spider natural history worth going out of your way for.

Most spiders anchor their egg sac somewhere and leave. A wolf spider female
attaches hers to her spinnerets and carries it with her everywhere, a pale
round ball dragged behind like luggage. When the spiderlings hatch, they climb
onto her abdomen and ride there, dozens of them, until they are developed enough
to disperse. OSU Extension describes exactly this.

A female carrying young is sometimes mistaken for a spider with a strange gray
growth on its back, and it is occasionally the reason somebody kills one. If you
see it, it is worth a closer look before deciding anything. It is one of the
more remarkable things happening in an ordinary Whatcom County lawn.

## Why one walks in through the door in autumn

Because autumn is when spiders move, and a doorway is a hole.

OSU Extension notes wolf spiders turn up around homes in fall, looking for
somewhere to overwinter — around doors, windows, in basements and garages. That
is the same seasonal movement that produces the general September spider
increase, which the Burke Museum attributes to mature males walking to find
mates rather than to weather or to spiders fleeing the cold.

The practical consequence is narrow and fixable. A wolf spider cannot climb a
smooth wall into an upstairs window. It arrives at ground level, which means:

- the gap under an exterior door with a flattened sweep
- an unsealed garage door, particularly at the two bottom corners
- a crawlspace hatch that no longer sits flush
- sliding door tracks
- unscreened crawlspace vents

All of which are on the list in
[sealing a building before fall](/guides/sealing-a-building-before-fall/), and
all of which keep out a great deal more than spiders.

## What we would actually do about it

Very little, and we would say so on the phone.

There is no sensible treatment program for a wolf spider, because there is no
wolf spider population in the house to treat. What there is, when somebody calls
about them, is usually one of three real problems underneath:

1. **A gap at ground level.** Fixed by
   [exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/), permanently,
   rather than by anything sprayed.
2. **Habitat tight against the building.** Bark mulch banked against siding,
   firewood against a wall, long grass to the foundation, boards and pots left
   on the ground beside a door. Pull that back eighteen inches and the hunting
   ground stops at the house line.
3. **Exterior lighting.** Lights draw insects, insects draw hunting spiders, and
   a porch light over a door is a dinner bell directly above the gap. Warm-toned
   or motion-activated lighting changes it measurably.

Where there is a genuine spider problem in a garage or crawlspace — webs,
numbers, something people are living with — that is funnel weaver work, and
[spider control](/services/spider-control/) sets out what we do and what it
achieves.

## What you will not hear from us

That you have a wolf spider infestation, because there is no such thing in a
Whatcom County house.

That a wolf spider bite can rot your skin, because that claim was never true of
this family and is barely true of any spider in North America.

That the one in your bathtub is a wolf spider, when the odds say giant house
spider and the species page is one click away.

And that you need a quarterly spray program to deal with a single animal that
walked under a door. If a spider came in under a door, the door is the problem.
We would rather sell you the door sweep.

If you want one identified, photograph it from above with something for scale —
