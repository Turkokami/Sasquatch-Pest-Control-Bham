## This is one package, not a house

An Indianmeal moth infestation is a container of food. It is almost never a
building problem, it is almost never a hygiene problem, and it is almost never
worth treating a room over.

That is the honest headline and it is worth stating before anything else,
because the instinct when moths start crossing a kitchen in the evening is to
think of the kitchen as infested. It is not. Somewhere in the house — and
frequently not in the kitchen at all — there is a bag, a box or a sack with silk
running through it, and clearing that one item does most of the work.

Kentucky's extension guidance names the usual origin directly: partially used
boxes or bags of product that have been forgotten at the backs of pantries and
shelves. That is the shape of nearly every case.

Which is why the verdict at the top of this page reads as it does. This is a
search and a cleaning job. We are happy to do the search, and where we do it the
outcome is usually that a household throws out a sack of birdseed and puts the
rest of the shelf into jars. That is a genuinely better result than a treatment,
and it costs less.

## The moth itself

Small, and unmistakable once you have looked at one properly.

UC IPM describes the forewings as reddish brown with a coppery luster on the
outer two-thirds and whitish gray on the inner portion. Clemson gives the same
two-toned pattern — reddish brown with a copper luster on the outer two-thirds,
gray on the inner third — and a wingspan of about half an inch. Minnesota puts
the wingspan at half to five eighths of an inch, with a pale gray or tan base to
the front wing and the remainder reddish brown.

That sharp division across the wing is the identifying feature. Sitting still on
a cupboard door the moth reads as two colors joined by a line, and no other small
moth people meet indoors here looks like that.

The behavior matters as much as the color. These adults fly to light, which is
exactly why they get noticed — a household sees them near a lamp, a range hood
or a window in the evening and concludes the problem is everywhere in the house.
The flying is real; the spreading is not. Adults disperse; the food source is
still one container.

Adults are short-lived and their only job is reproduction. Clemson records
females laying two hundred to three hundred eggs over one to eighteen days, so a
single overlooked package produces a great many moths in a short time.

## Webbing is the evidence

The insects are hard to see inside food. What they leave is not.

UC IPM says the larvae spin a web as they grow and leave behind silken threads.
Minnesota describes silk webbing present on the surface of the product. Kentucky
notes they produce silk webbing throughout the food source. Clemson adds that
the larva weaves a tunnel-like case of frass and silk around itself as it feeds.

In practice that means infested grain, flour, cereal, seed or dried fruit looks
slightly wrong before it looks obviously infested. Clumped. Stringy. Matted at
the surface, or webbed into the folds of the bag. Tip a suspect bag into a pale
bowl and the silk shows against the material immediately.

The larvae themselves are pale caterpillars. Minnesota calls them whitish worms
with shades of yellow, pink, green or brown, growing to half an inch. Clemson
gives a quarter to three quarters of an inch and notes the color varies with what
they have been eating. UC IPM describes fully developed larvae at about half an
inch, white with a greenish or pinkish hue.

Uninfested product has no webbing in it. That is the whole test, and it is far
faster than looking for insects.

## The wandering, and why people search the wrong place

This is the behavior that sends households in the wrong direction, and it is
worth understanding because it saves an enormous amount of pointless searching.

When an Indianmeal moth larva finishes feeding, it leaves the food. UC IPM states
that mature larvae can often be found in parts of the house far from the original
food source because they move quite a distance to pupate. Minnesota says larvae
often leave the food when mature and may move long distances before spinning a
cocoon. Kentucky describes the caterpillar crawling away from the infested
product to find a place to transform. Clemson notes it may travel a considerable
distance away from its food source, and puts the pupal stage at fifteen to twenty
days.

They crawl upward and outward. So the cocoons and the little pale caterpillars
people find are on the junction of wall and ceiling, on the top edge of a door
frame, along a picture rail, on the underside of a shelf lip, in a light fitting
— routinely in a bedroom or a hallway with no food in it at all.

Two consequences follow, and both are useful.

**Finding larvae far from food is normal.** It does not mean the infestation has
spread through the house. It means the larvae walked, which is what they do.

**The location of the cocoons tells you almost nothing about the source.** People
strip and scrub the room where they found the cocoon and are baffled when it
happens again. The source is wherever the silk-filled package is, which may be
two rooms and one floor away.

Search for webbing in food. Do not search for caterpillars on ceilings, other
than to confirm what you are dealing with.

## Where the source actually hides

Kitchens get emptied first and the source is frequently not in the kitchen. In
rough order of how often it turns out to be the answer:

**Birdseed.** This is the single most under-suspected reservoir on properties in
this county, and it is usually a large sack in a garage or a shed that nobody has
emptied to the bottom in a year. Kentucky and the Pacific Northwest handbook both
name bird seed explicitly. If you feed birds, look here first, before you throw
out a single thing from the cupboard.

**Dry pet food.** Same problem, same scale, same storage. Minnesota and the
Pacific Northwest handbook both list pet food and treats.

**Dried decorative material.** Ornamental corn, seed pods, potpourri, dried
flower arrangements, wreaths and craft supplies. Kentucky notes decorative ear
corn as a common source for a related stored-product moth, and the principle
carries. Nobody thinks of a dried arrangement as food and it is exactly that.

**Spices, nuts and dried fruit.** Whole spices, nutmeats, raisins, dates, dried
apricot. UC IPM's pantry pest list runs through nuts, dried fruit and
confectionery, and these are the items households buy in bulk and then keep for
years.

**The bag at the back since last year.** Flour, cornmeal, rice, oats, pancake
mix, birdcage grit, bulk grain. Kentucky's line about forgotten partially used
packages describes this precisely.

**Spilled material out of sight.** Under a shelf liner, in the seam at the back
of a cupboard, or in the shelf-support holes drilled into cabinet sides. A very
small quantity of meal supports a population indefinitely.

Two further places worth checking in this county specifically: a chicken or
livestock feed bin in an outbuilding, and a bag of grass seed or cover crop in a
garage. Both are common on rural properties here and neither reads as food to
the person who bought it.

## It was probably in the package before you bought it

This is worth saying because people are embarrassed about these calls and they
should not be.

Stored-product insects are introduced during processing, packing, warehousing,
transport or retail storage, and the eggs are microscopic. A package can be
sealed, undamaged and brand new and still carry the beginning of an infestation.
Nothing about a household's cleaning standards affects that. Minnesota's
explanation of how these insects get into packaging — chewing in, or crawling in
through folds and seams — describes a route that is open at every point in the
supply chain before you get there.

What household practice does change is what happens next: whether one introduced
package stays one package, or seeds the whole shelf. That is a storage question,
and it is answerable.

## The season here

Development runs on temperature, and a heated Whatcom County kitchen is warm
year-round, so this species never really stops.

UC IPM puts the complete egg-to-adult cycle at about six to eight weeks in warm
weather, and Kentucky records a life cycle as short as twenty-five days. Clemson
gives eggs hatching in seven to eight days and larvae feeding for six to eight
weeks. Those are fast numbers compared with the fabric pests, and they are why a
pantry situation escalates from a first moth to a room full of them in a couple
of months.

The seasonal shape locally comes from storage rather than from weather.

**Summer** is the peak, because warmth accelerates everything and because it is
when bulk buying and outdoor birdseed storage put the most material on
properties.

**Autumn** brings the second wave, when feeders go back up, decorative dried
material comes out and the garage fills. The Pacific Northwest handbook's
recommendation — not storing food longer than two months unless it is frozen —
is aimed squarely at this.

**Winter** does not stop it indoors. An unheated garage in Ferndale slows a sack
of seed down considerably, but a warm cupboard in the same house does not, and
our mild winters never get cold enough to clear an outbuilding.

**Late winter and spring** is when households finally investigate, having watched
moths cross the kitchen since the previous summer.

## Around the county

Where the source turns out to be tracks the kind of property more than the town.

**Rural and agricultural land** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Livestock and
poultry feed, seed stock, grain and bulk storage in outbuildings, all of it in
quantities that make a domestic cupboard irrelevant to the search. On these
properties the barn is the first place to look and the kitchen is the last.

**Properties with feeders**, which is most of the county. A sack of wild birdseed
in a garage is the reservoir we find more often than any other single item, and
it usually sits within a few feet of a door into the house.

**Older city housing** — the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York, South
Hill. Deep original cabinets, shelf-support holes, gaps behind trim, and pantries
with corners a modern kitchen does not have. The source is easy enough to find;
the spilled material that keeps it going is harder.

**Second homes and seasonal places** around Lake Whatcom and out toward Blaine
and Birch Bay, where a cupboard is stocked, left for months and opened again in
spring. Time is the ingredient this insect needs and those kitchens supply it
generously.

**Anywhere with a craft or holiday storage box.** Wreaths, dried arrangements and
ornamental corn get put away in October and rediscovered in a cloud of moths.

## Not the clothes moth, and not a weevil

Two separations worth making quickly.

**Against the clothes moth**, the tests are light and location. This species goes
toward a lamp; the [webbing clothes moth](/pest-library/webbing-clothes-moth/)
actively avoids light and hides when disturbed. This species is two-toned; that
one is a plain gold or buff all over. This one webs food; that one webs wool. If
there is silk in a cereal box, nothing about your wardrobe is at risk, and the
reverse holds too.

**Against beetles and weevils**, the test is webbing again. Silk binding the
contents together points to a moth. Fine sawdust-like debris in the bottom of a
container, small round exit holes in individual grains or beans, and small brown
beetles crawling at the top of a bag point at a beetle instead. The
[sawtoothed grain beetle](/pest-library/sawtoothed-grain-beetle/) is the flat one
that turns up in flour and dried fruit, and the weevils that develop inside a
grain are covered in our post on
[weevils in the pantry](/blog/weevils-in-your-pantry/).

Both can be present at once in a cupboard that has been left alone long enough,
and both are cleared the same way.

## Clearing it out

The sequence matters, because doing it in the wrong order is how the "we threw
everything away and it came back" case happens.

**Find the source before you throw anything away.** Check every container,
including the ones that look fine, and check the four suspects — birdseed, pet
food, dried decorative items, spilled material — before the kitchen shelves.

**Take infested material out of the building**, not into the kitchen bin.

**Empty the cupboard and vacuum the structure.** UC IPM's advice is to use a
crevice attachment in corners and cracks to remove eggs and pupae, then wash
shelves with soap and water. The shelf-support holes drilled into cabinet sides
are a standard harborage and are worth doing individually.

**Treat what you are keeping.** Minnesota gives a freezer at zero degrees
Fahrenheit for at least four days, or an oven at one hundred thirty degrees for
at least thirty minutes on shallow trays, and states those temperatures kill any
eggs or insects present. Kentucky gives one hundred fifty-five degrees for about
twenty minutes, or zero degrees for four days, specifically for decorative items
and bird feed. The Pacific Northwest handbook gives three or four days in a
freezer. Choose by what the item can survive: freezing suits food you will eat,
heating suits seed and craft material.

**Re-store in containers that seal.** UC IPM specifies containers with
tight-fitting lids rather than plastic bags, and says not to mix old food with
new or refill an unwashed container. The Pacific Northwest handbook warns
specifically against thin plastic bags of the supermarket produce variety. Glass,
metal or rigid plastic with a gasket is the standard, and it is the single change
that stops one introduced package from becoming a shelf-wide problem.

**Freeze bulk purchases on arrival**, as a habit rather than a response.

**Then monitor.** Pheromone traps catch adult males. UC IPM's guidance is to
place them where the infestation was, check weekly, and expect most traps to work
for about three months. Falling catches over several weeks is a result; one empty
trap is not.

## Why nobody should spray your pantry

UC IPM is unambiguous, and we agree with it entirely: insecticides are not
recommended for controlling pantry pests, and spraying pesticides on or near food
may cause greater harm to a household than the pantry pests would.

That is a strong statement from a land-grant program and it deserves to be quoted
rather than softened. The answer to an infested package is removal. The answer to
a cupboard is a vacuum and a cloth. There is no product application that improves
on either, and any proposal to fog or spray food storage is worth declining on
the spot.

Where anything is applied at all in this kind of work, it is narrow crack-and-
crevice application in cabinet structure, well away from where food sits — and
even that is a supporting move after the source has gone, not a substitute for
finding it.

## When we are actually worth calling

The verdict on this page is *rarely-warranted*, and we mean it. Most households
solve this themselves once they know where to look, and the pages you are reading
exist partly so that they can.

There are cases where a visit earns its cost.

**A source that will not be found.** Two or three genuine rounds of searching, no
webbing located, moths still flying. At that point a second pair of eyes and a
methodical run through the outbuildings, the garage and the less obvious
containers usually settles it in an hour.

**A property with real storage.** A farm building, a feed store, a large bird
seed or grain reservoir, or a household with several outbuildings — where the
number of candidate containers exceeds what one person will work through.

**Commercial food handling.** Restaurants, groceries, bakeries and food storage,
where the same species arrives in deliveries and the answer is receiving
inspection, stock rotation and monitoring rather than treatment. That is
[commercial](/commercial/) work and it is a program rather than a visit.

**Recurrence across seasons** in a household that has genuinely done the storage
work, which usually means a reservoir nobody has thought of yet.

What we will not do is book a treatment for a kitchen with moths in it. If the
answer is the sack of seed in the garage, that is what you will be told, and the
estimate that produced the answer is free.

## Knowing when it is over

Because adults keep appearing for a while after the source has gone, households
routinely conclude the clearance failed when it did not. A few markers help.

**Expect a tail.** Larvae already wandering in the structure when you emptied the
cupboard will still pupate and emerge, and Clemson's fifteen to twenty day pupal
stage sets roughly how long that tail runs. Moths in week two are not proof of a
missed source.

**Watch the trap count rather than the sightings.** A falling weekly catch over a
month is the signal. A single moth seen in a hallway is noise.

**No new webbing in anything you open.** This is the strongest household test
available, and it is worth deliberately opening and checking the containers you
kept rather than waiting to find out by accident.

**No fresh cocoons appearing at ceiling lines.** Old ones stay stuck where they
are, so scrape or vacuum the ones you find so that anything new is obviously new.

**Judge it across a season.** A pantry that is quiet in July and moth-filled again
in September had a reservoir that was never located — most often outside the
kitchen — and that is worth one more search rather than a treatment.

## Where to start

Before you call, do three things.

**Look for webbing** and tell us where you found it, or that you have not found
any. That is the fastest single piece of information.

**Check the birdseed and the pet food** before the cupboards, because that is
where it most often is.

**Leave the cocoons alone** on the ceiling line — they confirm the identification
