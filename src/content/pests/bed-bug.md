## The one thing to take from this page

Bites do not confirm bed bugs. Nothing else on this page matters as much as that
sentence, and almost every expensive mistake made with this insect starts by
ignoring it.

A person can be bitten nightly for months and show nothing on their skin. A
person can wake covered in welts that were caused by a detergent, a mite, a
spider beetle's cousin or nothing external at all. The two situations look
identical from inside the household, and no amount of squinting at a forearm
separates them.

What separates them is physical evidence: fecal spotting, cast skins, eggs and
the insects themselves. Those either exist in a room or they do not, and finding
them is a search rather than a judgment call.

The second thing worth carrying away arrives immediately behind the first, and it
is the reason this page is written the way it is. Bed bugs are not a hygiene
problem.

## They are not a cleanliness problem, and the shame is expensive

Washington's Department of Health describes people bringing bed bugs into their
homes on infested luggage, furniture, bedding or clothing. That is the whole
mechanism. There is no dirt in it, no neglect, and no correlation with how a
household is kept.

The reason is biological rather than charitable. This insect eats blood and only
blood. Crumbs on a counter, dishes in a sink, dust under a bed — none of it is
food, none of it is shelter it particularly wants, and none of it changes the
odds that a suitcase carried something home from a hotel. An immaculate house and
a chaotic one are equally available to an insect that is looking for a sleeping
mammal and a crack to sit in.

What clutter genuinely does change is how hard the insect is to reach once it is
established. That is a treatment problem, not a cause.

We put this near the top because the delay it produces is measurable. People sit
on a suspicion for weeks because of what they think finding bed bugs would say
about them, and those weeks are exactly when a single introduction in one bed
turns into a population spread along a baseboard and into the room next door. The
embarrassment costs more than the treatment does.

## What the insect actually looks like

The University of Kentucky puts an adult at about three-sixteenths of an inch,
reddish-brown, with an oval flattened body — apple-seed coloring on something
closer to a lentil in size. The Pacific Northwest handbook describes them as
roughly half the size of a pencil eraser, which is the more useful comparison for
somebody holding one on a piece of tape.

Flatness is the identifying quality. Unfed, the body is thin enough to sit in a
gap you would not think of as a gap. After feeding, which Washington's health
department describes as taking three to ten minutes, the body swells, lengthens
and turns a brighter red, and the insect looks like a different animal
altogether. People who have seen a fed bed bug and an unfed one often think they
have found two species.

They cannot fly and they cannot jump. They walk, quickly, on flat surfaces.

Immatures pass through five molts, and Kentucky notes that each of the five
requires a blood meal first. A newly hatched nymph is close to translucent and
about a millimeter long, which is why the small ones go unseen for months in
rooms that have been looked at repeatedly.

Eggs are whitish, roughly the size of a speck of dust, and hatch at about a week
at room temperature.

## Fecal spotting, cast skins, eggs, insects

These are the four findings that establish an infestation, and they are worth
knowing individually because they appear at different stages and in different
places.

**Fecal spotting** is the most reliable and the earliest to appear in volume. It
is digested blood rather than a solid pellet, so on fabric it soaks in and
feathers at the edge like ink on blotting paper, and on painted wood or metal it
dries as a small raised dark bead. Look at the mattress piping and seams, the
tape edge of a box spring, the underside of a slat, the joint of a frame, and the
wall immediately behind a headboard. A scatter of dark dots in a line along a
seam is what a resting group leaves behind, and it is far easier to see than the
insects that made it.

**Cast skins** are the pale, translucent, bug-shaped shells left after each of the
five molts. They are more brittle than the insect and they collect in the deepest
part of a harborage. Their significance is specific: a shed skin means something
grew up in that spot, which means breeding rather than a single insect that
arrived last week.

**Eggs** are about a millimeter, pearly, and cemented rather than loose — they do
not brush off. They sit in cracks and seams in small clusters and they are
genuinely hard to see without a light held at an angle. They are also the reason
one treatment is rarely the end of the job.

**The insects** are the least reliable of the four to go looking for, because they
are nocturnal, they are good at being in cracks, and their absence proves very
little. Finding them is confirmation. Not finding them is not a clearance.

## Where they actually are in a room

Bed bugs stay close to a sleeping person. That is the single most useful
organizing fact about searching for them, and it is why an infestation in a
detached house is usually a bedroom problem rather than a house problem.

Working outward from the sleeper, the order is roughly this. The mattress, along
every seam, both faces, and around the label and the handles. The box spring,
which is a better harborage than the mattress and is where a great deal is
missed, particularly under the dust cover and inside the corner protectors. The
frame, at every joint, bolt hole and slat pocket, and especially where two pieces
of timber meet with a hairline gap between them. The headboard, front and back,
and the wall behind it if it is fixed there — an upholstered headboard is
first-rate accommodation.

Then the room. The nightstand, underside and drawer runners. The carpet edge
where it turns up against the wall. Loose or separated baseboard. Outlet and
switch plates. The backs of pictures and mirrors. Any upholstered chair somebody
naps in.

The Pacific Northwest handbook makes the point that low-level infestations stay
near the bed and spread outward through the dwelling as the population grows.
That is why extent matters so much to what a job costs: an early one is
contained, and a long-running one is not.

## How they get from one place to another

They are carried. Bed bugs do not come in from the yard, they are not drawn by
food waste, and they do not arrive on the wind.

**Luggage and belongings.** A bag that spent a night on a hotel floor, a backpack
left in a dorm room, a coat on a theater seat. This is the dominant route and it
is the one that produces our January calls, after holiday travel puts a lot of
Whatcom County suitcases through a lot of other buildings.

**Secondhand and moved furniture.** Upholstered items, mattresses, bed frames and
anything with joinery. This is the dominant route in Bellingham's student housing,
where an enormous quantity of furniture changes hands in June and again in
September on an academic rather than a seasonal calendar.

**Shared construction.** In a duplex, an apartment block or an old house
subdivided into rooms, the boundary between one tenancy and the next is a legal
idea rather than a physical barrier. Wall voids, the gaps around supply pipe and
cabling, the space behind a loose baseboard and the underside of a door all
connect one unit to the next. Treating the complaining room while the room on the
other side of that wall goes untouched is the standard way a shared-building job
fails.

**People, occasionally.** Rarely on a body, more often on a bag somebody set down.

## Bed bug or carpet beetle

This is the confusion that costs the most money in both directions, and it is
worth separating carefully.

A carpet beetle larva is elongated rather than oval, bristly rather than smooth,
and it moves like a tiny caterpillar rather than scuttling. It is often golden or banded brown. It lives at carpet edges, in
closet floors, under furniture and in stored wool and textiles, feeding on animal
fiber, lint, pet hair and feathers rather than on blood.

The reason it lands on this page is that some people react to the larval hairs
and to the insect's hemolymph, producing itchy welts with no bite involved at
all. Penn State Extension characterizes carpet beetle dermatitis as an acquired
allergic reaction rather than a simple irritation, meaning sensitization builds
up over long exposure instead of appearing on first contact — which is why one
person in a household can be covered in welts while everybody else sleeping in
the same building notices nothing. Somebody with those welts, looking for a cause
in a bedroom, finds a small insect in the carpet and arrives at bed bugs.

The way to tell is not the insect. It is the evidence around it. Carpet beetles
leave shed larval skins that are bristly and hollow, and no fecal spotting on
bedding. Bed bugs leave dark spotting at seams and joints, and the shed skins are
smooth and oval. If a room has welts and beetle larvae but no spotting anywhere
on the bed, the answer is very unlikely to be the one everybody is afraid of.

Spider beetles, booklice and fleas fill out the list of things mistaken for this
insect. Fleas are the frequent one, and the giveaway is where the marks are —
around ankles and lower legs rather than on skin exposed above the covers — and
that a flea jumps, which a bed bug is entirely unable to do.

## What they do to people, and what they do not

Washington's Department of Health is direct: there are no known cases of
infectious disease spread by bed bug bites. That is not a hedge, and it is
genuinely reassuring in a way the internet is reluctant to be.

What they cause is a local skin reaction in the people who react, a stronger
allergic response in a minority, and secondary infection where welts are
scratched open. The health department also names anxiety, insomnia and
irritability, and in our experience that is the half of the problem that people
underrate before they have lived with it.

The reason to treat this quickly is not medical danger. It is that somebody who
knows there are insects in their bed stops sleeping, and stops sleeping for
weeks, and that has real consequences at work and at home. Speed is the kindness
here.

If a reaction is severe or spreading, that is a physician's question rather than
ours. We deal with the insect.

## Heat, cold and the numbers behind them

The domestic tools that work on this insect work because of temperature, and the
figures are worth having.

Purdue Extension gives the thermal death point at around forty-five degrees
Celsius, with practical heat treatment run in the range of one hundred twenty to
one hundred twenty-five degrees Fahrenheit for twenty to thirty minutes. For a
household, that translates into one instruction: hot water and detergent followed
by at least twenty minutes in a dryer on high heat kills every stage, eggs
included.

The dryer is the part doing the work. A wash cycle spends much of its time
cooler than that, and an egg cemented into a seam sits protected inside wet
fabric. Anything that cannot be washed but can be tumbled should still go
through.

Cold is far less useful than people hope. Purdue records that bed bugs tolerate
five degrees Fahrenheit for short periods and survive at or below freezing
continuously for several days; practical freezing means below zero Fahrenheit for
at least four days. A cold garage in a Whatcom County winter does not come close.

And waiting them out does not work either. The Pacific Northwest handbook states
that a bed bug can live up to a year without a blood meal, and Kentucky puts
survival at a year or longer at fifty-five degrees or below, with one to four
months more typical in a heated building. An empty room does not clear itself.

## Preparing for treatment, which is most of the outcome

This is the part households are least warned about and the part that most often
decides whether a job holds.

A treatment applied to an unprepared room is close to wasted. Clutter on a floor
gives the insects harborage that cannot be reached. Bedding that has not been
laundered and dried hot puts the population straight back into the room. Furniture
jammed against a wall hides the junction that most needs working on. Belongings
in open boxes are both harborage and a transport mechanism into whichever room
they get carried to next.

What preparation actually involves, in rough order:

**Launder and heat everything textile in the affected rooms**, then bag it sealed
and leave it sealed until after the work is finished. Clean items returned to an
untreated room are simply re-exposed.

**Reduce clutter rather than relocating it.** Bagging and consolidating within the
room is useful. Carrying an armful of belongings to the spare room is how a
one-room problem becomes a two-room problem, and it happens constantly.

**Pull the bed away from the wall and get bedding off the floor**, so the legs are
the only route up onto it.

**Leave the evidence alone.** Stripping and scrubbing a bedroom before a search
removes exactly what the search is looking for. Change the sheets if you must,
but keep the old ones rather than laundering them.

**Do not spray anything first.** Retail products scatter a population into voids
and into adjoining rooms, and they disturb the spotting and skins that would
otherwise confirm what you have.

We give you the list in writing beforehand, and where a room is not ready we
reschedule rather than working around it. That decision occasionally annoys
people on the day and it is right every single time.

## What the work involves

Treatment is aimed at harborage rather than sprayed across surfaces, because that
is where the insect spends its life. Seams, joints, voids, frame hardware, the
underside of furniture, the edges of the room and the penetrations that connect
one room to the next.

Which method fits depends on the room, the furniture, how far the population has
spread and what kind of building it is. A single bedroom in a detached house on
Alabama Hill and a third-floor unit near campus with activity on both sides of a
wall are different problems, and they should not receive the same answer just
because a company owns a particular piece of equipment.

Follow-up visits are planned from the start rather than added when somebody
complains. Purdue's interval — a check ten to twenty-one days after each control
effort — is the shape of it, and the reason is eggs, which are protected and which
hatch on their own schedule regardless of what happened in the room.

Seeing activity in the days after a treatment is ordinary and is not a sign of
failure. Disturbed insects move more. What matters is the trend across the
follow-ups rather than any single night.

We do not promise a room will be clear after one visit, and we are wary of
anybody who does. What we commit to is telling you the method, the reason for it
and the follow-up schedule before you agree to anything.

Encasements on the mattress and box spring are usually part of it. They trap what
is inside, remove the best harborage in the room, and give you a plain pale
surface where new spotting shows up immediately. Purdue specifies covers of at
least 0.08 millimeters, left on for a minimum of a year.

## Monitoring, and what a clear result actually means

Between a suspicion and a certainty there is a gap that a household can close
cheaply, and it is worth knowing what is in it.

**Light-colored encasements** on the mattress and box spring do three jobs at
once. They shut in anything already living in those items, they take away the
seams and piping that make a box spring such good accommodation, and they leave a
plain pale surface where any new dark spotting shows up without a search.

**Interceptor cups** under the bed legs are the other half. They are shallow
traps that an insect climbing toward the bed falls into and cannot get out of.
They are passive, they cost almost nothing, and over a few weeks they turn a
question into an answer. They only work if the legs are the only route up, which
means the bed away from the wall and nothing — bedding, clothing, bags — bridging
to the floor.

Retail detectors and lures are a different matter and we would skip them.
Performance varies enough that a negative reading from one is worse than having
no device at all, because it manufactures confidence.

On what a clear search means: it means no evidence was found in the places
evidence collects, and for this insect that carries real weight, because it is
much better at hiding itself than at hiding its spotting and its skins. It is
not proof against an introduction that arrived last week. Continued monitoring
for a few weeks is the sensible follow-through, and it is a far better use of
money than treating a room on suspicion.

## Shared walls change the scope

In a duplex, an apartment or a subdivided old house, the extent of the problem is
a property question rather than a tenancy question, and the arithmetic is
unforgiving.

An insect that lives in the crack at the base of a wall does not distinguish
between the side of that wall it started on and the side it did not. It follows
carbon dioxide and warmth along the path of least resistance, which is the wall
line, the gap under a door, the opening cut for a supply pipe, and the void behind
a run of baseboard. Older Bellingham housing supplies all four generously,
because a century of movement has separated the trim from the plaster and every
retrofit since has cut new holes.

The consequence is that treating the room that complained, while the room sharing
its wall goes untouched, buys a result determined by whether the neighbor happened
to be affected too. Where the population is established, the scope that works
follows the structure: the confirmed room, the rooms sharing a wall with it, and
the shared living space where somebody naps on a couch.

That is a larger number at the outset and a much smaller one across a year, and
in a multi-unit building the decision belongs to whoever manages the building
rather than to the occupant who found the spotting.

## When it is not a bed bug

If the marks are around ankles and calves and the insect jumps, look at the
[cat flea](/pest-library/cat-flea/) instead, and start by asking what animal has
been living in or under the building.

If the larvae are bristly and the bedding carries no dark spotting at all, the
answer is likely a carpet beetle, and the fix is a cleaning and storage job
rather than a bedroom treatment.

If biting started abruptly in one upstairs room a couple of weeks after noise in a
ceiling stopped, consider mites left behind by a nest that has been abandoned.
That is a structural search rather than a bedroom one, and it is squarely our
work.

And if a thorough search of the sleeping area turns up no spotting, no skins, no
eggs and no insects, that is a real result rather than a failed visit. It closes
off the branch that was keeping somebody awake, and it is worth taking to a
physician as information.
