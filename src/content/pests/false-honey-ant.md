## The ant that turns up before spring

There is a call this company gets every year in late winter, and it surprises
people every time. Ants — a proper trail of them — running into a kitchen in
February or early March, when the weather is still miserable and nothing else is
moving.

Most people assume the season has arrived early, or that something must be
nesting in the house to be active this soon.

Neither is true. What is happening is that one species in this region forages at
temperatures that shut every other ant down, and it has the field entirely to
itself for a few weeks each year.

The regional extension guide puts it plainly: homeowners become aware of these
ants in early spring when other ants have not begun foraging.

## The names, and what they mean

This ant carries three common names and all of them appear in the literature.

**False honey ant**, because some workers store liquid food in a distended
abdomen and dispense it to the colony later — a habit shared with the true honey
ants of the southwest, which is where the "false" comes from.

**Small honey ant**, same reason, less confusingly.

**Winter ant**, which is the one that actually describes the animal and the one
worth remembering. It forages in very cold conditions, and that is the whole
story.

## Recognizing them

**Size and color.** Two to four millimeters, yellow to brown, monomorphic.

**The hourglass.** This is the feature to look for. The thorax carries a
constriction that gives it an obvious hourglass shape viewed from above. Most
ants at this size do not show anything so distinctive, so if you can get a hand
lens on one it settles the identification quickly.

**The season.** Honestly, in this county, the time of year does a great deal of
the identification work on its own. An established trail of small brown ants in
February is this species far more often than it is anything else.

By the time high summer arrives they have largely gone quiet, which is the
reverse of every other ant on this site.

## Why the cold does not stop them

The short answer is that they are adapted to it, and the strategy is a good one.

Foraging in cold weather means no competition. Every other ant species in the
region is inactive, the honeydew and the overwintered insects and the scavenging
opportunities are all unclaimed, and there is nothing to fight for them.

The trade is that they are relatively inactive through the hot part of the year,
spending it deep in the nest. So a property with a winter ant problem in
February frequently has no ant problem at all in July, and people reasonably
conclude that something they did in spring worked.

Nothing worked. The ants went to bed.

## Where they nest, and why it is not your house

They nest in **soil**, outdoors, and they may **trail into structures in
inclement weather**.

That distinction is the practical core of this page. A trail coming into a
kitchen is a foraging line from an outdoor nest, not evidence of a colony in the
wall. Nobody needs to open a wall, and nobody needs a treatment aimed at a nest
inside a building.

Nests tend to be deep — considerably deeper than most ants in this region — which
is part of how they survive both the cold they work in and the heat they avoid.
That depth also means digging one out is not a realistic proposition and would
not be worth doing if it were.

## How they get in, and the fix that lasts

The extension note is specific and it is the most useful sentence on this page
for a homeowner: **vegetation in contact with structures provides avenues to
enter**.

In a wet Whatcom County February, that means:

**A branch touching a wall or a roof.** A bridge that bypasses everything at
ground level.

**Shrubs planted against siding**, which is the condition described at length on
our established-neighborhood pages, and which does as much for ants as it does
for rodents.

**Ivy or climbers on a wall.** A permanent, sheltered highway.

**Ground cover growing up over a foundation**, hiding the line where wall meets
soil.

Trimming that contact is the fix, and it is permanent in a way a treatment is
not. Combine it with sealing the specific gap the trail is using — usually a
utility penetration, a gap under a threshold or a poorly fitted vent — and the
problem does not come back next February.

That is exclusion work rather than pest control, it costs a morning, and it is
what we would recommend before anything else.

## Why we rarely treat them

Four reasons, and they compound.

**The nest is outdoors and deep.** Treating a foraging trail does not touch it.

**The activity is seasonal and short.** By the time a treatment program got
going the ants would be winding down anyway, which makes it very easy to sell a
result the weather delivered.

**The entry is fixable.** A branch cut back and a gap sealed solves it
structurally.

**They are doing no harm.** They contaminate food if they reach it and they are
unpleasant in numbers. They do not damage buildings, they do not sting, and they
do not carry the problems some indoor ants do.

Where the trail is persistent, the food source is genuinely attractive and the
householder wants it dealt with now rather than at the next dry weekend, a bait
placed on the trail will finish it. That is a small job and we will price it as
one.

## What people usually do instead

Two things, and both are worth heading off.

**Spraying the trail.** It kills the workers on the surface at that moment and
does nothing to a colony in soil twenty feet away. The trail re-forms within a
day or two, usually slightly displaced, and the householder concludes that the
ants are resistant to everything.

**Waiting it out and assuming the worst.** The other common response is to
assume a nest in the wall, which leads to anxiety out of proportion to the
insect and occasionally to somebody opening up a perfectly sound piece of
building.

Neither is necessary. Look at what is touching the outside of the house, and
find where the trail crosses the threshold.

## Around this county

This is a genuinely common local call and it clusters in a way that fits
everything above.

Older houses with mature planting against them get it most — the same
condition that produces so much of the work described on our Meadowview,
Homestead and The Meadows pages, where landscaping installed decades ago is now
hard against the siding.

Properties with a lot of evergreen cover, ivy, or laurel against a wall get it
reliably.

And it arrives on the first mild spell after a cold stretch, which in this
county can be any time from late January onward. A run of days above freezing
with rain is the trigger, and the call usually follows within the week.

## What we would actually do

**Confirm the species**, which at that time of year is usually quick.

**Find the entry.** Where does the trail cross from outside to inside — a
penetration, a threshold, a vent, a gap behind trim.

**Look at what is touching the building**, because that is nearly always how they
reached the entry in the first place.

**Recommend the trim and the seal**, and tell you honestly that this is the part
that ends it.

**Bait if you want it dealt with today**, on the trail, and explain that the
structural fix still matters.

Most of these visits end with a short list of things to cut back and one gap to
close, and no product applied at all.

## What we will not sell you

We will not sell a treatment program for an ant that is active for six weeks
and nests in somebody's lawn.

We will not describe a February ant trail as an infestation of the structure.
It is a foraging line from outside, and using the more alarming word to justify
a bigger job would be dishonest.

We will not spray a trail and take the money knowing the colony is untouched.

And if the answer is a branch cut back from the wall and a gap sealed under a
door, that is what we will tell you, even though it is the smallest possible

## What else is moving in February

Since the timing is the identifying feature of this ant, it is worth knowing what
else could be responsible for an insect indoors in late winter, because a couple
of them matter much more.

**Overwintering invaders waking up.** Cluster flies, lady beetles and stink bugs
that went into a wall void in October come out on the first mild days and appear
at windows. Those are not ants and they are not new arrivals; they have been in
the building all winter.

**Carpenter ants indoors in winter**, which is the one genuinely significant
finding at that time of year. Ants active inside a heated building in the middle
of winter, in numbers, when it is cold outside, can mean a colony inside the
structure rather than a trail from outside. Large ants — a quarter of an inch or
more, black or bicolored — in a kitchen in January are worth a proper look at
the crawlspace and the framing.

**Moisture ants**, small and yellow, which are a report about wet decaying wood
somewhere in the building.

So the timing tells you a great deal and it does not tell you everything. Small
brown ants on a trail from outside in February are almost always this species.
Large black ants indoors in January are a different conversation and a more
serious one.

## The honeydew connection

Their food list includes honeydew, and that shapes where the outdoor trails run
in a way worth knowing if the indoor trail is being traced back.

Honeydew is the sugary excretion of aphids and scale insects, and ants that use
it will travel to reach a productive colony. In late winter and early spring the
sources are limited — overwintering aphid populations on evergreens, scale on
ornamentals, early sap flow — which concentrates the traffic.

If a trail is running up the side of a building and disappearing into a shrub or
a climbing plant, that is very often where it is going, and the plant is worth
looking at. Dealing with a scale or aphid population removes the reason the
trail exists in the first place.

It also explains why the trail may cross a house rather than end in it. Not
every line of ants on a wall is trying to get inside; some of them are simply
using the wall as the shortest route between the nest and something growing on
the other side.

## Why the timing makes this ant easy to mis-sell

There is a commercial hazard specific to this species and it is worth naming.

A treatment carried out in late February against an ant whose activity naturally
declines through spring will look like it worked, every single time, regardless
of whether it did anything at all. The ants were going to go quiet anyway.

That makes the false honey ant close to ideal for selling a result the season
delivers. A spring treatment, a summer with no ants — which there would have been
regardless — and a satisfied customer renewing the following year.

We would rather point that out than benefit from it. If somebody has been paying
annually for a spring ant treatment and the ants are small, brown and only ever
appear before April, it is worth asking what exactly is being treated and
whether the trail was ever traced to a nest.

The honest test is simple: deal with the vegetation contact and the entry point
one year without any treatment at all, and see what happens the next February.
Our experience is that the ones fixed structurally do not come back and the ones
treated chemically do.

## Around this county

This is a common winter call across the older parts of Bellingham and in the
established neighborhoods of the towns, and the pattern is consistent enough to
predict.

Houses with mature planting hard against the walls get it most, which is the
condition described at length on the pages for The Meadows, Meadowview and
Homestead. Decades of growth has put evergreen cover, ivy or laurel in contact
with siding, and that contact is the bridge.

Older housing gets it more than newer housing, not because of the age of the
building but because the landscaping around it has had longer to reach the wall
and the building has had longer to accumulate small unsealed penetrations.

Properties on the wetter ground get it more, because a soaked soil profile
pushes foraging upward and outward.

And it arrives on the first mild, wet spell after a cold stretch — which in this
county can be late January in a mild year and well into March in a hard one.

## What the season is actually telling you

There is a wider point here that applies beyond this species, and it is the
reason this page exists rather than a line on the odorous house ant entry.

The calendar is diagnostic. An insect indoors in February is a much narrower set
of possibilities than an insect indoors in July, and using the time of year
properly removes most of the guesswork before anybody looks at anything.

February and March: this ant, or overwintering invaders waking up in a wall, or
— the one that matters — carpenter ants active in a heated structure.

April and May: carpenter ant swarms, the first odorous house ant activity, and
moisture ants where there is decay.

Midsummer: pavement ants, thatching ants, everything at once.

Late summer into fall: reproductive flights for most species, wasps at their
peak, and the overwintering invaders arriving on the sunny walls.

Somebody who knows roughly where in that calendar they are has already narrowed
a phone call considerably, and it costs nothing to ask what month it is before
guessing at a species.

## The trail you can follow yourself

Because the nest is outdoors and the ants are large enough to see, this is one of
the few species on this site where a householder can realistically do the
diagnostic work themselves.

Wait for a mild afternoon while the trail is active. Follow it from where it
enters the room, back to where it crosses from inside to outside — that gap is
the thing to seal, and it is usually smaller and more specific than people
expect. A gap beside a pipe under a sink. The corner of a threshold. A vent with
a perished gasket.

Then follow it on the outside. Down a wall, along a foundation, and very often
straight into whatever plant is touching the building. That is the bridge, and
cutting the contact is the durable half of the fix.

Somewhere beyond that is the nest, in soil, and you do not need to find it. That
is the useful part: with this species the two things worth changing are both
within arm's reach of the house.

Doing that once, properly, in March, is usually the end of it.

## What to do if you would rather not wait for March

If the trail is active now and you want it stopped today rather than after the
next dry weekend, a bait placed directly on the line will do it. They take sweet
baits readily, which is one of the few things about this ant that is
straightforward.

That is a small job and we price it as one. What we will say at the same time is
that the bait deals with this trail and the trimming deals with next year, and
skipping the second half means having the same conversation in twelve months.
