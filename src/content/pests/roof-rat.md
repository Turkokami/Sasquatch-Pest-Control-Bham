## The other rat

Most rat content treats the animal as a single species with a single set of
habits, and in a county with both of ours that assumption sends people to the
wrong half of the building.

*Rattus rattus* — the black rat, the ship rat, the roof rat, depending on which
century's name you prefer — is the climbing one. Washington Department of Fish
and Wildlife describes it as similar in overall length to the
[Norway rat](/pest-library/norway-rat/) but slenderer and darker, with a tail
longer than the head and body combined, and records it building nests in attics,
trees and overgrown shrubbery or vines. WDFW adds that it rarely bothers digging
a burrow at all when an off-the-ground site is available.

That single preference reorganizes everything. The entries are at roof height
rather than at grade. The travel routes are cables, branches and pipe runs rather
than foundation edges. The nesting material is attic insulation rather than
crawlspace batts. The traps go on horizontal timbers eight feet up. A technician
who searches a crawlspace for this animal can spend a thorough hour and find
nothing at all while it sleeps directly above the bedroom ceiling.

## Telling the two apart in thirty seconds

The differences are not subtle once you know what to compare, and UC IPM sets
them out in exactly the terms that are usable in the field.

**Ears.** Fold one forward. On a roof rat it is long enough to reach and cover
the eye. On a Norway rat it falls short.

**Tail.** Longer than head and body together on a roof rat; shorter on a Norway
rat. This holds on a dead animal in a trap and it is the most reliable single
check.

**Muzzle.** Pointed on a roof rat, blunt on a Norway rat.

**Build and weight.** Five to ten ounces, sleek and agile against seven to
eighteen ounces, large and robust.

**Droppings.** Cornell's identification guidance puts roof rat droppings at about
half an inch, more slender and often slightly curved, against three-quarters of
an inch to an inch and capsule-shaped for a Norway rat. On a dusty attic top
plate that difference is visible without measuring anything.

**Where the sign is.** This is the honest tiebreaker. Both species climb — WDFW
is explicit that both are good climbers — but only one of them prefers to live
overhead. Height is not proof, and combined with the tail and the ears it is
close enough.

## Where they turn up in this county

WDFW gives the distribution note that matters locally: roof rats prefer
saltwater-influenced warmer climates and are often seen in port cities and
coastal towns. That is a description of the western edge of this county.

So the properties where we expect to find them are not distributed evenly.
Working waterfront and marina areas, where gear storage and vessel traffic have
been moving rats around for as long as there have been docks. Blaine, Birch Bay,
Semiahmoo and the Fairhaven waterfront. Older commercial blocks downtown, where
false ceilings and shared service chases give a climber an interior highway that
never touches the ground.

Then, quite separately, any property with mature tree cover in contact with a
roof. Around Lake Whatcom, in Sudden Valley, along the Edgemoor and Silver Beach
lanes and through the older parts of the city where the street trees have been in
place for eighty years, the branch touching a gutter is doing exactly what a
ladder does.

None of that makes the roof rat the common rat here. The Norway rat still
accounts for most of what we find. It makes the roof rat the one that gets missed
on the properties where it is present, which is a different and more expensive
kind of error.

## Fruit, nuts and what feeds a climber

UC IPM records that roof rats travel up to three hundred feet or more for food,
and this species has an old and well-earned reputation for fruit.

In Whatcom County that means the unpicked apple and plum trees in half the older
back yards, the fallen fruit nobody clears, hazelnuts, the grape vine on the
south fence, and — reliably — bird feeders. Chicken feed matters here as it does
for every rodent, but the specific signature of a roof rat property is fruit
damage with the skin opened and the flesh taken while the fruit is still on the
branch, often overnight and often high in the tree.

Cutting that supply does not resolve an infestation on its own. It changes how
attractive the property is relative to the one next door, which over a season is
worth more than it sounds.

## Signs, read from below

Very few people find this by going into the attic. It announces itself in other
ways.

Noise overhead after dark, starting within an hour of dusk, running in bursts and
returning before dawn. Sound that travels along a ceiling line over a couple of
seconds is an animal on a run, and that is the most useful thing you can tell us.

Droppings on the tops of stored boxes rather than beside them, which is a
consequence of an animal that arrives from above.

Smudging where a horizontal surface meets a vertical one at height — along a top
plate, over a beam, at the edge of a soffit return. Rats carry oil and grit in
their coats and deposit it on the routes they repeat.

Gnawed rims on plastic tote lids in a loft, which is a rat-sized job rather than
a mouse-sized one.

A stain on a ceiling with no plumbing above it and no roof leak to explain it.

And in an occupied house, odor working down through the unsealed penetrations
every ceiling has — recessed light housings, bath fan bodies, the attic hatch,
the gap at the top of an interior wall.

## The attic insulation problem

An attic is a hostile place to live. It is hot in July, cold in January, and a
long way from anything to eat. What makes it worth the climb is the insulation,
which from the animal's point of view is a warehouse of soft, dry, warm, endlessly
available building material.

So roof rat activity in a roof space is insulation activity almost by definition.
The material gets pulled into heaps, tunneled through, flattened along the routes
used most, and wetted. Each of those reduces the volume of still air the material
holds, and since that trapped air is the entire mechanism by which insulation
works, the loss in performance follows the loss in loft closely.

The visual from the hatch is misleading. A roof space can look adequately
insulated while a large share of its effective value has gone, because
compression is invisible from twenty feet away and the bare bays are behind the
ones you can see.

What to do about it is a genuine judgment rather than a script. Material that is
matted across an area, distributed with nesting and waste, saturated to the point
where you smell it before you see it, or tunneled until its structure has gone,
should come out — that is [attic insulation removal and
replacement](/services/attic-insulation/), and it is frequently the largest line
on the job. Material that is dry, lofted and intact under a scatter of droppings
should be cleaned and left, and being told that is one of the reasons to have
somebody competent look rather than somebody selling.

The one option that is never right is blowing new insulation over contaminated
material. It buries the waste, keeps the odor, and buys the same job again in
three years with more of it to remove.

## What the damage adds up to

The nuisance case for dealing with this is the noise. The actual case is what
gets chewed while nobody can see it.

**Wiring.** A roof space is full of cable — lighting circuits, low-voltage runs,
thermostat and alarm wiring, sometimes communications — and cable sheathing is
one of the materials a rodent works on because it has to. Teeth that grow five
inches a year are maintained by gnawing rather than by choice. This is the damage
with the worst consequence and the least visibility.

**Ducting at height.** Where an air handler or ductwork runs through an attic, a
breach vents conditioned air into an unconditioned space and moves air from a
contaminated one back into the rooms below.

**Plumbing.** Supply lines to an upstairs bathroom pass through the same voids. A
pinhole in plastic pipe above a ceiling is discovered as a stain rather than as a
leak, usually some weeks later.

**Stored belongings.** Cardboard is a resource and everything soft in it is
nesting material. Clothing, linen, camping equipment, upholstered items, paper
records and photographs all get shredded for material rather than eaten, and the
loss is frequently the thing people mind most. After a roof problem is resolved,
moving anything that matters into rigid sealed containers is worth doing for its
own sake.

**Secondary pests.** An established rodent presence supports flies and dermestid
beetles that feed on waste and nesting material, and when the rodents are removed
those look for somewhere else to be — which is usually downward, into the rooms
you use.

## The timing of it here

The seasonal curve is the same one that governs everything else in this county,
and it is driven by water and cover rather than by temperature. Pressure builds
from late September, peaks through October and November, and stays elevated
through a winter that never resets anything.

WDFW records rats breeding year-round with reproduction concentrated in the warmer
months — litters of six to ten, three weeks of gestation, weaning at about twenty
days and breeding maturity at three to four months. The summer population is the
one that arrives at your roofline in the autumn, which is why the arithmetic
rewards acting before the arrival rather than after it.

There is a practical argument for doing roofline work in July or August that has
nothing to do with pests. The weather is workable, the roof space is accessible
and dry, and the sealing is being done to an empty building rather than around a
live population. In a Whatcom County November the number of days suitable for
working at height is genuinely limited, and a job booked in the autumn frequently
waits on the weather before it waits on anything else.

Almost nobody does this in August. It is still the cheaper version of the same
project.

## False ceilings, shared roofs and commercial buildings

The roof rat's preference for horizontal voids off the ground makes it
disproportionately a commercial and multifamily problem, and those buildings fail
in ways a single-family house does not.

A suspended ceiling is a continuous highway above a whole floor plate, connected
to every tenancy that shares it. A roofline that runs across four units belongs to
none of them individually. Service chases between suites carry pipe, conduit and
animals in the same direction. And restaurant and grocery premises supply the food
that makes the whole arrangement worth occupying.

In a shared building the problem is frequently not yours in origin and reaches you
anyway, through a party wall or over a common roof. Treating one tenancy while the
adjacent one is untouched relocates a population rather than reducing it, which is
why the sensible unit of work in those buildings is the structure rather than the
suite. If you are a manager or a tenant in that position, say so when you call,
because the scope of what actually needs doing is set by the building and not by
your lease.

## The numbers, and one comparison to retire

Roof rats are smaller than Norway rats and the exclusion threshold does not
change for it.

The eXtension wildlife damage guidance sets the working figures at any opening
greater than half an inch for rats and larger than a quarter inch for mice, with
nineteen-gauge half-inch mesh specified for rat exclusion and twenty-four-gauge
quarter-inch mesh for mice. Whatcom County's health department gives residents
the simpler version: seal holes and gaps bigger than a quarter of an inch.

The quarter-inch figure is the one to build to, and the reason is in WDFW's
phrasing rather than in the arithmetic. A rat needs only a quarter-inch hole to
chew larger and squeeze through. Teeth that grow five inches a year make a
half-inch requirement academic wherever a quarter-inch edge is available to work
on.

A comparison worth deleting from your mental toolkit while we are here: a hole
"the size of a dime" is not a mouse-sized hole and is nothing like a rodent
exclusion standard. The dime is 0.705 inches across by the Mint's own
specification — most of three quarter-inches, and larger than the half-inch rat
threshold. Seal to a pencil width instead. It is the right size and it is in
everybody's drawer.

## Trapping a climber

Everything about trapping this species is a variation on one point: the animal is
not on the floor.

**Traps go where the runs are, which is up.** UC IPM's guidance for roof rats is
off-the-ground placement — ledges, shelves, beams, branches — as against the
wall-and-corner placement that works for a ground species. In a roof that means
top plates, rafter ties, joists beside a run of smudging, and the pipe or cable
the animal is using as a bridge. Traps get fastened down rather than balanced,
because a struck trap that falls into insulation is lost.

**Expect several nights of nothing.** UC IPM notes that rats detect and avoid new
objects and new foods, and that this neophobia is thought to be more pronounced
in roof rats than in Norway rats. Of the two species, this is the one that will
step around a trap for the better part of a week. Setting traps unset for a few
days, then arming them, is a real technique rather than a delay.

**Numbers, not cleverness.** A run that supports a colony needs a line, not a
pair. Under-trapping produces a small catch and a wary remainder.

**And do not put bait inside a roof.** An animal that dies on a wall top or in a
soffit return is somewhere nobody can reach without opening the building, and the
odor and the flies that follow it run for weeks. Bait belongs in tamper-resistant
stations on the exterior where it belongs at all, which is a decision about
property pressure rather than a default.

The secondary-poisoning question deserves the same plain answer here as anywhere.
Anticoagulants kill slowly enough that a poisoned rat keeps moving, and whatever
takes that rat takes the dose with it. WDFW notes that carcasses can carry
several lethal doses and that pets and wildlife are poisoned this way. EPA
withdrew the second-generation compounds from consumer products for that reason,
citing the risk to non-target species that eat bait once or eat animals that
have. Owls and hawks are the ones with the most to lose, and on most properties
they are already working the same problem for nothing.

## What a roof rat carries

WDFW lists bubonic plague, salmonella, leptospirosis and tularemia among the
diseases spread from Old World rats to people. That is the accurate general
statement and it is worth reading with proportion: the everyday mechanism in a
Whatcom County house is contamination of food and surfaces, not anything dramatic.

What this animal is *not* is the hantavirus story. Washington's health department
attributes hantavirus pulmonary syndrome here to deer mice, states that infected
deer mice live throughout the state, and reports one to five cases in a typical
year. Whatcom County's guidance says the same. If you have read that rats in an
attic are a hantavirus risk in this region, the attribution is wrong.

The cleanup rule is unaffected by any of that, because you cannot species-identify
dried waste by looking at it. Air the space for at least thirty minutes before
entering, never sweep or vacuum dry droppings or nesting material, wet everything
with a ten percent bleach solution and give it ten minutes, wear gloves, use
disposable towels and bag the lot. An attic is the worst possible place to
improvise on this, because the material is dry and the ventilation is poor.

## Exclusion at height

Closing a roofline is the same discipline as closing a foundation performed
somewhere less convenient, and the difference in convenience is most of why it
gets skipped.

The recurring defects are consistent: the soffit-to-fascia junction, especially
where it returns at a corner; open rafter tails on older construction; gable and
box vents with corroded or missing screening; the junction where a porch or
garage roof runs into an upper wall; and service penetrations at height, which
are sealed to a poorer standard than anything at ground level because whoever ran
them was on a ladder.

Then the ladder the animal uses. Branches over or touching a roof, ivy and dense
climbing growth on a wall, a trellis, a downspout, a fence run that arrives at a
roof edge. Cutting to a genuine clear gap is often the cheapest single measure
available on a property and it is frequently the one that decides the outcome.

Materials are the other half. Metal mesh behind vents, sheet metal at gaps that
will be gnawed at directly, blocking at rafter tails, proper vent covers fastened
to sound substrate rather than stapled over a hole. Expanding foam is an air seal
and never a barrier, at any height. This is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work and it is the half
of the job that keeps working after the invoice is paid.

Which is the honest qualification on the verdict above. We treat this, and
treatment on its own is a subscription. A roof rat colony removed from an
unsealed building is a vacancy, and vacancies in this county fill from late
September onward.

## When it is not a roof rat

Daytime noise overhead, heavier than a rat, is usually something else. Squirrels
are the common mistake and they are not rodent work as we scope it; bats and
birds sit further outside it again, with legal timing attached. We will tell you
what your description sounds like and point you toward somebody equipped for it,
and it costs nothing to ask.

Norway rats also reach attics, particularly in buildings where an interior chase
gives them a route up from the crawlspace without ever going outdoors. So height
alone does not settle the species question — which is why the tail and the ears
are worth the thirty seconds.

Mice in a roof space are quiet, leave rice-grain droppings and build discrete
fist-sized nests rather than sprawling through several bays. That is a
[house mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse/) job and a different trap line.

## Getting it looked at

The useful visit walks the roofline from outside, gets into the roof space where
it is safe to do so, checks the tree and utility contact, and then checks the
crawlspace anyway — because a meaningful share of attic activity arrives from
below through a wall rather than over the roof.

You get a direct account of what is up there, whether it is even ours to handle,
where it is getting in, what the roofline needs and whether the insulation can
