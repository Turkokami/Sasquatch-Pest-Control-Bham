## Read the verdict first

This profile exists because voles get confused with things we do handle, and
because the wrong diagnosis here sends people to the wrong trade and sometimes to
an unnecessary treatment. It does not exist to sell you anything.

We are a structural pest control and exclusion company. Our rodent work is about
what gets inside a building — how it got in, how it comes out, and how the shell
gets closed. A vole is not doing any of that. It lives in the grass, eats the
plants, and by UC IPM's own account is a poor climber that usually does not
enter homes or other buildings at all.

So the honest answer is that vole damage in a lawn, a bed, an orchard or a
nursery row is not our work, and we will say so on the phone rather than after
driving out. What follows is what we would tell you on that call, at more
length, because a correct identification and a realistic list of what works is
worth more than a service call anyway.

Moles come up constantly in the same conversation and they are a separate animal
altogether. They are also outside what we do. There is a section on them below,
because telling the two apart is most of the value of this page.

## What a vole actually is

A vole is a small rodent built low and compact, and once you have seen one the
outline is hard to mistake.

UC IPM describes a compact heavy body, short legs, a short-furred tail, small
eyes and partially hidden ears, colored blackish brown to grayish brown, at five
to eight inches overall. The impression is of a stocky animal with no neck, a
blunt face and a tail that looks too short for it.

Set that against the two mice on this site. A [house
mouse](/pest-library/house-mouse/) has a tail about as long as its head and body
together, large ears and a pointed muzzle. A [deer
mouse](/pest-library/deer-mouse/) has larger eyes and ears still, and a furred
two-tone tail. A vole has none of that: the ears are half buried in fur, the
eyes are small, and the tail is a stub by comparison.

They are also called meadow mice, and WSU uses that name alongside the genus,
which is worth knowing because a lot of the older material a gardener will find
files them that way.

Diet is the other half of what a vole is. UC IPM describes them as mostly
herbivorous, feeding on grasses, herbaceous plants, bulbs and tubers. WSU adds
that in winter they turn to the bark and roots of woody plants. That single
seasonal shift is the origin of nearly all the expensive vole damage anybody
ever sees.

## The species that is actually here

This matters because most vole content online is written about the meadow vole
of the eastern states or the California vole, and neither is what is in a
Bellingham lawn.

WSU's vole publication is specific about Washington: two species cause most of
the damage in this state, and the one west of the Cascade Mountains is the
Townsend's vole. The montane vole takes over east of the mountains. So on this
side, in Whatcom and Skagit, Townsend's vole is the default answer.

WSU lists its habitat as lawns, open grassland, gardens, farms, orchards,
meadows, cropped areas and young forested plantations. That is a fair
description of a great deal of this county, and it explains why this is a
routine complaint here and a curiosity elsewhere.

## The runways are the diagnosis

If you take one identification method from this page, take this one.

UC IPM calls the clearest evidence of voles the presence of well-traveled
aboveground runways connecting burrow openings, usually hidden under a canopy of
grass. WSU describes the same thing from the animal's side: voles cut runways
through grass, feeding on the vegetation as they move.

To find them, part the grass and look at the soil surface. What you are looking
for is a network of narrow paths, an inch or two wide, worn down to bare or
matted ground, running between small open holes. They are not tunnels and there
are no mounds of pushed-up soil along them. They connect, they branch, and they
usually follow cover — the base of a hedge, the edge of a bed, along a fence
line, under a shrub skirt, beneath a tarp or a sheet of weed cloth.

Two corroborating signs are worth checking while you are down there. UC IPM
names fresh clippings of green grass in the runways, cut and left rather than
carried away, and greenish droppings about three-sixteenths of an inch long near
the runs and burrows. Clippings plus droppings plus paths is a complete
identification and it takes about a minute.

The other thing the runways tell you is scale. A few short runs at the edge of a
bed is a different situation from a lawn with a visible network across it once
the grass is parted, and that difference should govern how much anybody spends.

## Vole, mole, or something else entirely

This is where most of the confusion in this county sits, and the two animals
have almost nothing in common beyond living underground in a lawn.

**Moles are not rodents and do not eat plants.** Oregon State Extension is
direct: moles tunnel while hunting prey — worms, grubs and insects — rather than
feeding on vegetation. A mole in a lawn is not eating your lawn. It is eating
what lives underneath it, and the damage it does is mechanical rather than
dietary: raised ridges where a surface run has been pushed up, and mounds of
excavated soil.

**The mounds are the fastest check.** Oregon State describes mole mounds as
volcanic and rounded, thrown up from below with no visible entrance. Gopher
mounds, for completeness, are flatter and fan-shaped with the hole off to one
side. Voles produce no mounds of any kind. They produce surface paths and small
open holes.

**The damage is different in kind.** Plants dying back, bark gone at the base of
a shrub, bulbs disappearing, hostas grazed to the crown — that is a plant-eater
and it points at voles. A lawn full of soil heaps with the plants otherwise
healthy points at moles. The two can occur on the same property at the same
time, and voles will happily use a mole run as a highway, which is how the two
get blamed for each other.

**Neither is our work.** Moles are outside what this company does. Voles are
outside what this company does. We are telling you the difference because you
asked what you have, not because either answer leads to us.

Pocket gophers get raised occasionally and are a much less likely answer on the
wet side of the Cascades. UC IPM's distinction is still useful if the question
comes up: vole girdling damage generally occurs above ground, while similar
gopher damage generally occurs below it.

## What the damage looks like

**Turf and ground cover.** Grazed patches, the runway network itself, and in a
bad year a lawn that looks scored and thinned across a large area once the grass
lifts in spring. UC IPM notes damage to turf and landscape plantings where the
dense vegetation they live in gives them cover.

**Bark at the base of woody plants.** This is the one with real financial
consequence. UC IPM describes gnaw marks about an eighth of an inch wide and
three-eighths long, in irregular patches. WSU gives tooth scars generally a
sixteenth of an inch wide, appearing in a crisscross pattern on the damaged
plant. Either way, the texture is distinctive: irregular patches of crossed,
short gouges rather than the clean chisel work of a rabbit or the ragged strip
damage of a deer.

**Bulbs, tubers and roots.** Tulips that never come up. Crocus that vanished.
Root damage on young trees below the soil line.

**Nursery and orchard rows.** Young stock is the most vulnerable thing there is
— thin bark, small caliper, and usually planted into exactly the mulched,
weed-clothed, ground-covered conditions that suit a vole.

## Girdling, and why it all appears in one week

The mechanism behind the worst of it is worth understanding because it explains
the timing, and the timing is what makes people think it happened overnight.

Girdling means gnawing all the way around a stem, trunk or root. UC IPM sets out
the consequence: when voles gnaw completely around a trunk or roots, the flow of
nutrients is interrupted and the plant can die. A tree girdled at the base in
February can leaf out perfectly in April and then fail in July, which is why the
diagnosis often arrives months after the damage.

The reason it concentrates in winter is cover. A vole is a small prey animal
that will not spend long in the open, so bark feeding happens where something is
hiding the animal: matted grass, heavy mulch, weed fabric, a shrub skirt, a
board, and above all snow. UC IPM notes that snow cover extends damage a foot or
more up the trunk, because the vole is working on top of the snowpack rather
than at ground level.

In Whatcom County we do not get months of continuous snowpack, which caps the
height of the damage relative to a colder region. What we do get instead is
eight months of dense damp ground cover that never dries out or dies back, and
that provides most of the same protection. A mild, wet, green winter is a good
winter for a vole.

So the practical shape of it is: the animal works through the dark months under
cover, the cover comes off in spring, and the damage arrives all at once as
information.

## The cycle, and why some years are much worse

Vole populations do not sit at a steady level and it is important to know that
before spending money on the assumption that this is now permanent.

WSU records population explosions in Washington roughly every three to five
years, potentially reaching more than a thousand voles per acre, followed by a
crash. UC IPM describes the same cyclical pattern reaching peak numbers every
three to six years before dropping back to low levels.

The arithmetic behind the peaks is startling. UC IPM gives females maturing in
thirty-five to forty days and producing five to ten litters a year of three to
six young, with individuals rarely surviving beyond twelve months. WSU's
illustration of the same thing is that a single female can theoretically produce
over three thousand female descendants within about four and a half months.

Two conclusions follow, and they pull in opposite directions, which is why we
give you both.

**A bad year is usually a peak rather than a change to your property.** If the
whole neighborhood has vole damage this spring, you are living through a cycle
and it will fall away regardless of what anybody does.

**Waiting it out does not protect a young tree.** A crash next year is no
comfort to something girdled this year. The barrier work below is worth doing on
anything you would be sorry to lose, independently of where the cycle is.

## Why this county suits them

Nothing about vole pressure here is a reflection on how a property is kept. It
is a straightforward consequence of the climate and of how western Washington
landscaping is done.

**No hard freeze.** Populations are not reset by winter the way they are inland,
so the animals come through in numbers.

**Green cover year-round.** Grass keeps growing through the mild season and
ground cover plantings never die back, so the protective canopy the species
depends on is available for twelve months rather than five.

**Heavy mulching.** Bark mulch laid deep is standard practice on landscaped lots
here, and a deep mulch bed against a shrub is close to purpose-built vole
habitat.

**Weed cloth.** UC IPM specifically names weed cloth alongside vegetation and
mulch as protective ground cover that voles occupy. It is everywhere in local
landscaping and it is doing exactly the wrong job.

**Edges.** Field margins, greenbelt boundaries, drainage ditches, creek
corridors and the untended strip along a fence line are all reservoir habitat,
and most residential lots in this county have at least one within a few yards.

## What actually works on voles

None of this is a treatment, which is the whole point. Both extension sources
land in the same place.

**Take away the cover, which is the single highest-value measure.** UC IPM
recommends removing weeds, heavy mulch and dense vegetative cover by mowing,
herbicide or tilling. WSU's version is to keep grass short and to hold mulch
down to one to two inches rather than the four or five that gets laid on a
typical bed. A vole crossing open ground is exposed to every predator on the
property, and that is the mechanism doing the work.

**Clear a circle at the base of anything young.** UC IPM specifies keeping a
four-foot-diameter circle around the base of a young tree free of vegetation.
Mulch pulled back from the trunk rather than piled against it, which is better
for the tree in several other ways as well.

**Guard the trunks you care about.** UC IPM describes cylinders of hardware
cloth, sheet metal or heavy plastic around individual trees; WSU points to
quarter-inch hardware cloth barriers. The mesh needs to be a quarter inch or
smaller, and the base needs to be pushed into the soil rather than resting on
it.

**Fence a bed if it is worth fencing.** UC IPM's specification is wire fencing
or aluminum flashing standing at least twelve inches above ground, quarter-inch
mesh or smaller, with the bottom edge buried six to ten inches. That is a real
piece of work and it is only worth it around something valuable.

**Trap, in the runways.** Both sources support it and both are realistic about
the numbers. WSU describes ordinary mouse traps baited with apple or peanut
butter, set at right angles across a runway. UC IPM's guidance on quantity is
the part people underestimate: a dozen traps for a small garden, fifty or more
for a larger area, placed along the runs. Trapping a lawn with three traps is a
gesture.

**Let the predators work.** Barn owls, hawks, herons on wet ground, and every
other thing that eats small rodents are doing this for nothing. WSU is honest
that cats kill voles but damage other wildlife while doing it, and that
predation on its own will not resolve a peak year.

## What does not work

**Rodenticide, for a homeowner in this state.** WSU states that no rodenticides
are registered for home use against voles in Washington. UC IPM notes that zinc
phosphide is the only toxicant usable against voles in residential settings in
California, which is a different state with different registrations, and it is
the kind of difference that catches people out when they read national advice.
If somebody is offering to put bait down for voles on your lawn, ask what the
product is and whether its label covers what they are proposing.

**Repellents.** WSU records their effectiveness as unproven, listing blood meal
and castor oil products among what is sold. We would not spend money there.

**Ultrasonic devices, vibrating stakes and windmills.** No credible mechanism
and no evidence.

**Flooding a run or dropping something down a hole.** Runways are a surface
network with many openings and the animals simply leave and come back.

**Treating the lawn with an insecticide.** This is worth naming because it gets
sold. Voles eat plants. Killing the grubs in a lawn does nothing to them
whatever — and if grubs were the issue, the animal digging for them would be a
mole rather than a vole.

## The one case that is ours

There is a narrow exception and it is worth stating so nobody is caught by an
absolute.

Voles very occasionally get into an outbuilding, a garage, a crawlspace or a
greenhouse, particularly where a structure sits directly on grade with heavy
vegetation right up against it. UC IPM's own framing is that they usually do not
enter buildings, and usually is not never.

If something is genuinely getting inside a structure, the sealing work is
ordinary [exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) and the
standard is the same quarter-inch threshold that governs every rodent on this
site. That part we do.

But the far more likely explanation for an animal inside a building is that it
is not a vole. Check the tail and the ears before anything else. A long bare
tail and prominent ears is a mouse, and that is a different page with a
different answer.

## Where to go instead

For the horticultural side of this — which is most of it — the useful contacts
are the WSU Extension Master Gardener program for Whatcom County, which handles
exactly this kind of question, and the extension publications listed below,
which are written for a homeowner rather than for a commercial operator.

For a landscape at scale, an orchard or a nursery block, a landscape contractor
or an agricultural adviser is better placed than a pest control company, because
the fix is cultural: mowing regimes, mulch depth, weed control, clear circles and
trunk guards.

And if what you actually have turns out to be something in the building rather
than something in the grass, call us and we will pick it up from there.
