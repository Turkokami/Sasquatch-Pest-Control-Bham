## Why this one gets its own page

Most of what this library has to say about spiders is that the frightening ones
are harmless. This page is the exception, and it is worth being straight about
in both directions.

The yellow sac spider is small, pale, easy to miss, and almost never mentioned
by the companies that will happily sell you a treatment against a hobo spider.
It is also the spider in a Whatcom County house most likely to actually bite
somebody. Washington's Department of Health names exactly two spiders in the
state as being of medical significance: the black widow, which is a species of
the drier country east and south of the Cascades and uncommon on this side of
them, and this one.

So on our side of the mountains, in an ordinary bedroom, this is the realistic
answer to "what could bite me."

And then the second half, which matters just as much: the bite is a minor
event. It stings sharply, it goes red, it itches, and within a day or two it is
finished. The reputation this spider carries for eating a hole in people's skin
is the same kind of story the hobo spider carries, built the same way, and it is
not supported by the evidence either.

Getting both halves right is the whole job of this page. A real bite is a real
if small thing, and the horror stories are not.

## What it looks like

Small, pale, and unremarkable, which is most of why people do not spot them.

**Size.** Penn State Extension puts females at 5 to 10 millimeters in body
length and males at 4 to 8. UC Davis gives the same range as roughly a quarter
to half an inch. That is a spider the size of a grain of rice with legs, and it
is a fraction of the bulk of the large brown spiders that generate autumn phone
calls.

**Color.** Pale throughout — cream, light yellow, or with a faintly greenish
cast to the abdomen. UC Davis notes that the legs and the front body section
run darker than the abdomen, which reads as a pale body with a slightly
tea-colored head end.

**Jaws.** On the agrarian species, Penn State records distinctly dark brown
chelicerae against the pale body, which is often the most visible marking on the
animal.

**Feet.** Utah State Extension gives the field mark most people can actually use
without a lens: brown-to-black tufts of hair at the ends of the legs that look
like small socks. On a pale spider on a white ceiling, the dark tips are the
part your eye catches.

**Eyes.** Eight, in two level rows of four, which matters only if you have the
animal in a container and a magnifier, but it does separate the group cleanly
from the funnel weavers.

There are two species involved in most of North America. Penn State describes
*Cheiracanthium inclusum*, the agrarian sac spider, across much of the country,
and *C. mildei*, a European arrival, now established throughout the United
States north of Virginia, Utah and California — which includes all of
Washington. The regional handbook that WSU, Oregon State and the University of
Idaho publish jointly lists both under the yellow-legged sac spider for the
Pacific Northwest. For a household the two behave identically and nothing turns
on which one is on the ceiling.

## The silk pocket, which is the real field mark

You are far more likely to find the retreat than the spider.

This species does not build a capture web. What it builds is a small flattened
tube or pocket of dense white silk, closed at one end, tucked exactly where two
surfaces meet — Utah State's description is where walls meet other walls or
ceilings. Behind a picture frame, at the top corner of a door casing, in the
angle above a window, along the underside of a shelf, in the folds of a curtain
where it hangs against the wall, or under the lip of outdoor siding.

The spider spends the day inside it. At night it leaves, hunts, and either
returns or spins a new one somewhere else, which is why an actively occupied
house accumulates small silk patches in ceiling corners over a season.

On a white or cream ceiling these are close to invisible. Penn State's note is
that the retreats blend in with the background coloration of light-colored
walls, and in practice most people only notice them once they have been told
what to look for and then find eleven of them in one room.

If you are trying to work out whether you have this spider, look at the
junctions rather than for the animal. A flashlight held at a low angle across a
ceiling corner picks the silk out immediately.

## It hunts, and that is why it bites

The behavior is the whole explanation for the medical entry, so it is worth
laying out plainly.

A funnel weaver or an orb weaver sits in a structure and waits. It has no reason
to cross a person and considerable reason to avoid one. A yellow sac spider does
the opposite: it leaves its retreat after dark and walks, over ceilings, down
walls, across furniture, over bedding, hunting insects and — Utah State includes
this — other spiders. It climbs slick surfaces easily, so glass, gloss paint and
polished trim are all routes rather than barriers.

An animal that patrols a bedroom at night eventually ends up somewhere awkward.
Both UC IPM and Washington's health department describe the same mechanism:
bites usually happen when the spider becomes trapped against a person's skin
inside clothing or bedding. A sleeve pulled on in the morning, a sheet rolled
into at two in the morning, a towel left on a bathroom floor overnight.

Utah State's phrasing is that yellow sac spiders are not known to be a health
hazard but can aggressively bite when trapped against the skin, and both halves
of that sentence are doing work. The spider is not hunting you. It is responding
to being crushed, which is the only situation in which almost any spider bites
anything the size of a person.

That is also why the practical advice on this page is about shaking things out
rather than about fear.

## What a bite is actually like

From the verified-bite literature rather than from the internet.

The onset is sharp. Both UC IPM and the verified-case research describe an
immediate pain comparable to a bee sting — not a bite that goes unnoticed and
turns up as a mystery lesion three days later, which is worth knowing because
that pattern is the signature of the *other* things people blame on spiders.

Then redness, mild swelling and itching around the site. UC Davis adds that a
blister sometimes forms at the point of the bite and may break, leaving a small
sore that takes a few weeks to close over.

For duration, the verified-bite study is the best figure available: pain
typically running one to two hours, occasionally persisting 24 to 48, with
minimal systemic effects. In other words, unpleasant for an evening, mildly
annoying for a day or two, and then over.

UC Davis makes one further point that is genuinely reassuring and rarely
repeated: this spider's jaws cannot pierce the skin of everyone, and it does not
necessarily inject venom on every bite, which leaves a large margin of safety.
A great many contacts produce nothing at all.

## The necrosis story, and why the evidence does not support it

This is the same correction this site makes about the hobo spider, applied to a
different species, and it comes from the same body of work.

For decades the yellow sac spider was carried in medical references as a cause
of dermonecrotic lesions — wounds where tissue dies and leaves an ulcer. Some of
that reputation was borrowed from the brown recluse, which is not established in
Washington at all. Some came from animal studies and from circumstantial cases
where a spider was assumed rather than caught.

Then somebody went and collected the cases where a spider was actually caught
and identified.

The 2006 study in the *American Journal of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene*
gathered 20 newly documented bites in which the spider was verified — ten in the
United States, ten in Australia — and reviewed 39 more verified bites from the
existing literature, for 59 in total. Not one of the 20 new cases produced a
dermonecrotic lesion. Across the 39 from the literature there was a single
instance of mild necrosis, in a European species not found here. The authors'
conclusion is that dermonecrosis in this genus is a rare event and mild at best,
that the reputation rests on misread circumstantial evidence and animal work,
and that clinicians should stop reaching for these spiders as an explanation for
necrotic ulcers in the absence of a verified bite.

Penn State's own entry now carries the same caveat: studies of a large number of
verified human bites have failed to confirm the necrotic lesions the species was
credited with.

We publish that because it is what the evidence says, and because a pest control
company has every commercial incentive to publish the opposite. A spider that
eats holes in people sells a treatment. A spider that stings like a bee and is
forgotten by Thursday does not.

What survives the correction is still worth taking seriously. This is a spider
that genuinely bites people, more often than any other one in a Washington
house, and Washington's health department lists it as medically significant for
that reason. It just does not do the thing it has been accused of.

## When to see a doctor

We are not qualified to give medical advice and this is not it. What we can do
is repeat what the state health department says and be clear about where our
competence stops.

Seek care promptly if a wound is spreading, if it is getting worse rather than
better after a couple of days, if there is pus, streaking or fever, or if there
is any difficulty breathing or swallowing, chest pain, or swelling of the face
or mouth — those last ones are emergencies regardless of cause.

And carry the health department's more important warning with you: unexplained
wounds are frequently misdiagnosed as spider bites, and that misdiagnosis leaves
the actual condition untreated. The conditions that get mistaken for spider
bites include bacterial infections such as MRSA and a range of fungal and other
skin problems, some of which are far more consequential than anything a
*Cheiracanthium* can do.

If you caught the spider, bring it. We will identify it at no charge and tell
you what it is. What we will not do is look at a wound and tell you what made
it, and any pest control company willing to do that is going beyond what it
knows.

## Telling it from the big autumn spider

Almost every spider call this county produces in September is a giant house
spider, and the two are not easily confused once you have a size in mind — but
people who have read about sac spiders start seeing them everywhere, so it is
worth a short list.

**Size.** A giant house spider runs half an inch to an inch in the body with a
leg span that can reach four inches. A yellow sac spider tops out around ten
millimeters overall. If it startled you from across a room, it was not this
species.

**Color.** Brown to grayish-brown against pale cream or yellow.

**Where you find it.** Floor level, bathtubs, sinks, crawlspaces and window
wells for the funnel weavers, because they are poor climbers. Ceiling corners,
walls, curtain folds and bedding for the sac spider, because it climbs
extremely well.

**Web or no web.** A flat sheet with a funnel at one edge means a funnel weaver.
A small closed pocket of silk with no capture structure attached means a sac
spider.

**Season.** Funnel weaver sightings spike hard from August into October when
mature males walk. Sac spiders are present through the year and move indoors
more noticeably as the weather cools in autumn.

Both identifications matter, and for once they matter differently. Getting the
funnel weaver right saves you an unnecessary treatment. Getting this one right
occasionally means the bites somebody has been getting have an explanation.

## Where they turn up in this county

The pattern is about vegetation outside and junctions inside.

**Houses with planting against the walls.** The regional handbook associates
this group with dense vegetation, and a shrub line touching siding is both a
population and a bridge. Properties around Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor
and the greenbelt margins run heaviest for that reason.

**Bedrooms and upper floors** rather than basements. This is not a crawlspace
animal. It goes up, and the retreats end up in the rooms people sleep in, which
is the entire reason it is on this list at all.

**Older housing** in the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland and York, where
original window casings, picture rails and deep trim give a spider that likes
junctions a great many junctions to choose from.

**Newer construction** gets them too, in the ceiling corners of bedrooms and
around window heads, but usually in smaller numbers because there are fewer
routes in.

**Rural properties** toward Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas, where field
margins and outbuildings sit close to the house.

**Autumn** is when indoor numbers rise, along with everything else in this
county, and it is the season when bites are reported.

## The year, and the eggs

The seasonal shape is less dramatic than the funnel weavers and it still has a
shape.

Through spring and summer the outdoor population lives in vegetation — shrubs,
tall grass, the leaf edges of ornamental planting, and the field margins that
surround so much property in this county. That is where the regional handbook
places the group, in dense vegetation rather than in buildings, and it is why a
house with planting hard against the siding runs a different risk from a house
with a gravel strip.

As nights cool in autumn, movement toward buildings increases, along with
everything else that happens here in October. Retreats start appearing in
ceiling corners of rooms that had none in July.

Egg-laying happens inside a retreat. The female closes herself into a silk
chamber with the eggs and stays with them, which is why a sac that feels
slightly bulky and that a spider will not leave is worth removing rather than
leaving alone. Vacuuming a retreat takes the eggs with it, and a retreat
cleared in winter is a small number of spiders that never hatch into a bedroom
in spring.

Indoors, in a heated building, none of this is strongly seasonal — a warm house
in this climate lets the cycle run on regardless of what the weather is doing.
Which is the practical reason the retreat check is worth doing twice a year
rather than once.

## What actually reduces them

Physical work, in this order, because the product does the least of it.

**Clear the retreats.** Vacuum the ceiling and wall junctions in bedrooms, then
behind headboards, picture frames, curtain rods and wardrobes. Removing the
retreat removes the spider and its eggs together, and it is the single most
useful thing anybody does about this species.

**Shake things out.** Clothing left on a floor, bedding that has been folded on
a chair, towels, gloves, boots. Almost every bite in the literature happened
because something with a spider in it was put on or laid on. This one habit
removes most of the actual risk.

**Move the bed off the wall** and keep the bedding off the floor, which cuts the
routes onto it.

**Pull the planting back from the siding**, thin dense ground cover against the
wall, and get vines off the building. That is where the outdoor population
lives, and vegetation touching a house is a bridge for a spider that climbs
this well.

**Fix the lighting.** Warm-toned bulbs, motion sensors, or fixtures moved away
from doors and bedroom windows. Fewer flying insects at the wall at night means
less reason for a night hunter to be on that wall.

**Close the gaps.** Utah State's list is the right one: seal exterior cracks and
crevices, fit tight door sweeps, and make sure window screens seat properly.
That work is [exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) and it
pays against several unrelated problems at once.

**Then treat, if it is warranted.** Targeted application to harborage and entry
points, not blanket interior spraying. UC IPM's general finding on spiders is
that spraying is only temporary without the housekeeping alongside it, and that
holds here as much as anywhere.

## Our verdict, and where the line sits

This one genuinely depends, and here is what it depends on.

**Worth doing something about:** a population established in living space,
particularly bedrooms; a household where somebody is getting bitten
repeatedly; retreats accumulating in ceiling corners faster than they can be
cleared; or a building where the outdoor population against the walls is heavy
enough that clearing indoors does not hold. In those cases the work is real —
retreat removal, harborage treatment, the vegetation and the gaps — and we will
do it without apologizing for it.

**Not worth doing anything about:** one small pale spider on a wall; a few
retreats in a garage or a shed; a bite that has already happened and is
resolving on its own. A single sac spider in an outbuilding is a predator doing
useful work on insects, and the same species outdoors in shrubbery is part of
the ordinary fauna of every garden in this county.

The honest middle ground is that this is the one local spider where the answer
is not automatically "leave it," and also not "panic." It is a small animal with
a small bite that lives in the corners of rooms, and how much attention it
deserves depends entirely on whether it is in a bedroom or a barn.

## What we will not tell you

**That it causes flesh-destroying wounds.** Fifty-nine verified bites say
otherwise, and we would rather cite them than trade on the story.

**That you need a treatment because you found one.** You almost certainly do
not.

**That we can tell you what caused a wound on your arm.** We identify spiders.
A physician reads skin, and the health department's warning about misdiagnosis
exists because that distinction gets blurred constantly.

**That a single autumn treatment settles it.** The population is outdoors on the
vegetation and indoors in the junctions, and clearing the retreats plus closing
the building does considerably more than product does alone.
