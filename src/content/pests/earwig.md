## The pincers first, because nothing else gets read until they are dealt with

Almost every call about this insect is really a call about the back end of it,
so we will settle that before anything else.

The forceps are a bluff. UC IPM's Pest Notes describe them as a pair of
prominent appendages used in defense and state directly that earwigs do not
attack humans. There is no venom gland attached to them. There is no sting.
There is no bite in the sense that matters — no mouthparts driving into skin,
nothing injected, no reaction to manage afterward. A large male can close the
forceps on a fingertip firmly enough that you would rather he did not, and that
is the entire extent of the risk this animal presents to a person.

The forceps are mostly aimed at other earwigs. Males carry curved ones, females
straighter, and Penn State records male forceps varying in length from three
sixteenths to three eighths of an inch — the kind of variation you see in a
structure used for contests within a species rather than for defense against
something a thousand times its size.

**And the ear.** Penn State's fact sheet names the belief for what it is: a
widespread superstition that these insects crawl into the ears of sleeping
people, once extended to a story about boring into the brain, and it states
that they do not crawl into the human ear. The English name is very old, the
folklore is older, and neither is evidence.

That is the whole of it. Everything else on this page is about mulch.

## What the animal actually is

**Size and color.** Adults run about five eighths of an inch by Penn State's
figure and up to three quarters by UC IPM's, dark reddish-brown, with a reddish
head and yellow-brown legs. The Pacific Northwest handbook gives about
five eighths of an inch, light to dark brown, with pale legs — which is the
description that matches what turns up here.

**Body.** Flattened and elongate, built for wedging into a tight gap. That shape
is the single most useful thing to know about the animal, because it explains
both where it spends the day and how it gets under a door.

**Habits.** Nocturnal. During daylight they are jammed into something dark and
humid; after dark they come out and forage. Lift a board in a Bellingham garden
bed at two in the afternoon and you get a sudden scatter of them, which people
routinely report as an infestation and which is simply what the ground looks
like here.

**Maternal care**, which is genuinely unusual among insects and worth knowing.
UC IPM describes the female digging a cell in the soil, laying a mass of thirty
or more eggs, and protecting the nymphs until their first molt. Penn State gives
thirty to fifty-five eggs in the spring brood and describes high maternal care
of the eggs and early nymphs, with the female gathering them into a pile and
standing guard. Insects that tend their young are a small minority, and this one
does it in the soil under your hydrangea.

## Why this county produces so many

One generation a year, and the population is decided before anybody notices it.

UC IPM describes overwintering pairs in soil cells, with the female laying in
late winter or spring and typically producing two broods. Penn State has the
nymphs moving through four stages, first inside the nest under maternal care and
then foraging on their own, reaching adulthood around August and September.
Where winters are mild, UC IPM notes, some populations stay active rather than
truly hibernating.

Whatcom County supplies exactly the conditions that arithmetic needs. There is
no hard freeze to cut the overwintering females back. There is roughly
thirty-five inches of rain keeping the top inch of soil and everything lying on
it damp from October through May. And there is the landscaping convention — deep
bark mulch installed to the siding on essentially every lot — which supplies the
damp, dark, insulated shelter the young need at exactly the moment they need it.

The practical consequence, and it is the most useful sentence on this page for
anybody planning work: **the number you experience in August was set in March.**
Clearing shelter in early spring removes harborage before the population builds.
Treating in late summer is managing a number that has already been decided.

## The population is in the first three feet

This is where the job is, and it is a narrow band.

**Bark mulch held against the foundation.** The largest single factor on most
properties in this county, and the one nobody installed on purpose. Mulch does
precisely what it is sold to do — holds soil moisture and evens out temperature
— and held to the wall it produces a permanently damp strip at grade in the dark
against the siding.

**Leaf litter** banked into beds and against walls, particularly on the shaded
lots around the lake and along the greenbelts, where it never really dries.

**Anything lying flat on soil.** UC IPM's list of daytime refuges is boards,
loose clods of soil, dense vines and weeds. Add to it, from local experience:
pavers, stacked pots, firewood on the ground, landscape fabric, a tarp that has
been there since spring, a child's plastic sled.

**Dense low ground cover** against the wall — ivy, vinca, thick perennial
planting — which holds humidity at soil level through the day.

**The gap behind trim and siding at grade**, and the joints of retaining walls.

**A damp open crawlspace**, which through unscreened vents is functionally part
of the same outdoor habitat rather than part of the house.

Every one of those is outside. None of them is in your kitchen.

## They do not infest a structure

Stated flatly, because it is the fact that decides the verdict.

The Pacific Northwest handbook says that earwigs do not establish breeding
populations inside homes. They are outdoor animals that wander in, and indoors
they are in the wrong environment: dry heated air, no damp harborage, nothing
they recognize as food in useful quantity, nowhere to make a soil cell. What
they do next is dry out.

So the earwig walking across your bathroom floor is not part of an indoor
population. It is a single animal that came under a door within roughly the last
day and is already losing. The handbook's only note about indoor damage is
occasional damage to clothing, and even that is not something we are often
called about.

That is why interior treatment is the wrong instrument. Applying product along
baseboards puts it where people live in order to kill insects that were not
going to last the week, and it does not touch the population that keeps supplying
replacements from a mulch bed eight feet away.

**The correct indoor response is a vacuum or a dustpan.** That is not us being
coy about scope. It is the accurate answer.

## Where they come in

They are flat, they are small, and they are following dark and damp.

Under exterior doors with worn or missing sweeps, which is the single most
productive gap on most houses. Along sliding door tracks. Around ground-level
window frames. Through unscreened or corroded crawlspace and foundation vents.
Around utility and hose-bib penetrations. Up through gaps at the sill plate.
And straight through an open door on a warm evening, because the porch light is
on and there are insects at it.

Indoors they head for the humid rooms — bathroom, laundry, kitchen — because
those are the only rooms that suit them at all. That is why the same two rooms
produce nearly every sighting in a house, and it is a useful thing to mention
when you call.

UC IPM adds a detail worth acting on: replacing outdoor lighting with yellow or
sodium-vapor lamps makes it less attractive to them. Moving a fixture away from
a door does the same job with no bulb to buy.

## Outdoors they are mostly on your side

This is the part of the page most likely to change what somebody does, so it is
worth the space.

UC IPM describes earwigs as voracious feeders on soft-bodied insects such as
aphids and insect eggs, states that they have been shown to be important
predators of aphids, and says they can exert significant biological control
under some circumstances. WSU's Hortsense lists their prey as aphids, mites,
thrips, leafhoppers, centipedes, millipedes, caterpillars, insect eggs and
whiteflies, and concludes that at moderate levels the earwig probably does far
more good than bad in most gardens.

The rest of the diet is decaying plant material. They are working the litter
layer, breaking down what falls, in the same way the sowbugs and millipedes
alongside them are.

And they do chew living plants, which is the other half of an honest account.
UC IPM records seedlings losing leaves and stems outright, mature plants left
with irregular holes or edges chewed away, shallow gouges in stone fruit, and
feeding on corn silk that prevents pollination and produces poor kernels. The
ornamentals named are zinnias, marigolds and dahlias. In this county the
recurring complaints are dahlias, strawberries, lettuce and other tender greens,
and corn.

So the animal is genuinely both, and the sensible response is targeted rather
than broad. Where a specific bed is being damaged, UC IPM's trapping methods
work and cost almost nothing: a shallow can of fish or vegetable oil sunk to
soil level, or a rolled newspaper, a piece of corrugated cardboard, a bamboo
tube or a short length of hose set near the affected plants before dark and
emptied into soapy water in the morning. The Pacific Northwest handbook gives
the same rolled-newspaper method.

What those traps will not do is reduce a property-wide population, because they
are competing with every board, pot and inch of mulch already available. They
protect a row of dahlias. They do not clear a yard, and nothing does.

## Why the verdict is "rarely warranted"

Because on most properties the honest work is an afternoon with a rake and a
door sweep, and that is what "rarely warranted" means on this site.

The order that actually reduces them:

**Pull the mulch back.** A clear band between mulch and siding — the Pacific
Northwest handbook puts the figure at six inches minimum, and a foot is better
where you can spare it. Gravel, bare soil or nothing. It removes the habitat
sitting against the wall, it keeps damp material off the siding, and it costs
the price of an afternoon.

**Thin the mulch that remains.** Depth matters as much as position. A deep
blanket holds a humid layer under it all season.

**Lift what is on the ground.** Boards, pots, bricks, tarps, landscape fabric,
firewood — off the soil and away from the wall, or gone.

**Cut the ground cover back from the foundation.** UC IPM is explicit about
removing refuge in the form of ivy, weeds and piles of leaves.

**Fix the exterior water.** Downspouts discharging well clear of the building,
gutters clear, grade falling away, irrigation not spraying the siding. Damp is
the requirement; take it away and the shelter stops being shelter.

**Close the threshold.** Door sweeps, weatherstripping, vent screening and
sealed utility penetrations. That is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work, it takes an hour
or two, and it stops several unrelated things at the same time.

**Deal with a wet open crawlspace** if you have one, because it is functionally
outdoor habitat directly under the floor. Why so many of them here stay wet, and
what the code actually asks for, is set out in the
[crawlspace guide](/guides/why-crawlspaces-here-stay-wet/).

## And what makes it warranted anyway

Having spent this page recommending a rake, here is the other side, because
"rarely" is not "never".

**A heavy year on a property where the landscaping cannot practically change.**
Some sites are stuck — a strata-managed complex, a mature garden nobody is going
to tear out, a lot that backs onto a field margin. A perimeter and harborage
treatment timed to spring, before the population builds, genuinely reduces what
arrives in August. Timed to August it mostly does not.

**Ground-floor units in a landscaped complex.** The residents get the complaints
and the cause is the planting specification. Treating unit by unit is expensive
and ineffective; the beds and the perimeter are the job.

**A commercial setting where the tolerance is legitimately zero** — food
handling, hospitality, healthcare — where an insect on a floor is a problem
regardless of whether it was dying.

**A crop being lost.** Market gardens and berry rows in the Lynden, Everson and
Nooksack country, where the damage is measurable rather than annoying.

**Where the crawlspace is the real reservoir** and the earwigs are one of
several things it is producing. Then the moisture work is warranted on its own
terms and this species comes along with it.

What is not on that list is a house with a few earwigs in the bathroom in
August. That is the common case, and the answer to it is free.

## Where they turn up hardest in this county

The distribution follows landscaping and shade rather than housekeeping, which
is worth saying because these calls often arrive with an apology attached.

**The gardened city neighborhoods** — Columbia, Sunnyland, York, Roosevelt,
Cornwall Park, Happy Valley. Mature planting, generous beds worked right up to
the foundation, and prewar houses whose door sweeps and vent screens are as old
as the rest of them.

**Newly landscaped subdivisions** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain and the
newer stock around Ferndale. Fresh deep bark laid to the siding on every lot in
the development at once. These properties often have their worst summer two or
three years after planting, once the mulch has broken down enough to hold water
like soil.

**Shaded and forest-edge lots** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor, the
margins along Whatcom Falls. Deep leaf litter, ground that does not dry between
rain events, and ground cover pressed to cedar siding.

**Farm country** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas. Field margins, irrigation,
outbuildings, stacked material, and the only local setting where crop damage is
worth a treatment decision on its own.

**Waterfront and seasonal housing** — Blaine, Birch Bay, Semiahmoo. Damp ground,
stored gear, driftwood, and a perimeter that nobody manages for months at a
time.

None of that is a statement about the households. It is a statement about
shade, planting and rain.

## Telling it from what it is not

Misidentification is less common here than with the spiders, but it happens in
three predictable directions.

**Rove beetles** are the usual mix-up, and they are the more alarming-looking of
the two to anybody who does not know them. They are elongate and dark with very
short wing covers that leave most of the abdomen exposed, and when disturbed
many of them curl the tail end upward in a threat display that reads as a
scorpion. No forceps at the back. Beneficial predators, and not this animal.

**Young cockroaches** get reported as earwigs in older commercial buildings and
basements. They have long trailing antennae, no forceps, and they run for cover
in a way an earwig does not. That identification matters a great deal more than
this one does, so it is worth confirming rather than assuming.

**Silverfish** come up because both are quick and both live in the same damp
band. Silver scales and three tail bristles against reddish-brown armor and a
pair of forceps — a photograph settles it instantly.

The check that ends all three: look at the tail end. Forceps mean earwig, and
nothing else in a Whatcom County house carries them.

## What people have usually already tried

Most households have done two or three things before they call, and it is worth
saying which of them were wasted effort.

**Indoor perimeter sprays from the hardware store.** These treat the wrong side
of the wall. The insects reached are the ones already failing.

**Bug bombs.** No reach into the mulch bed, which is the only place that
matters, and product distributed across living space for no gain.

**Sealing without clearing.** A door sweep is genuinely worth fitting, but a
sealed house next to a saturated bark bed is still a house with a large
population pressed against it, finding the next gap.

**Cleaning the beds the morning of the visit.** Understandable, and it removes
the evidence that would have told a technician where the harborage was.

**Waiting for a hard winter to fix it.** It is not coming. That is the premise
of this whole library: without a real freeze, nothing here gets reset, and
population control has to be structural rather than seasonal.

## What we will not do

**Spray the inside of a house for an insect that is dying on the floor.** The
ones indoors have days at most. Product applied to interior baseboards changes
their outcome very little and the outdoor population not at all.

**Sell a recurring plan for a garden predator.** An annual schedule for an
animal that eats aphids is a poor trade for the customer and we would rather
say so.

**Treat a whole garden.** Broadcast work in a planted yard takes out the
beneficial side of this insect along with everything else in the litter layer,
and the beneficial side is real.

**Frighten anybody about the pincers.** They are a bluff, we have said so at
length, and a company that leans on that image to sell a treatment is trading on
a superstition.

## What to tell us if you do call

**Which rooms.** Bathroom, laundry and kitchen means humidity and a threshold.
An upstairs bedroom is a different picture and worth mentioning.

**How many and how often.** One or two after a warm night is normal in this
county. A dozen a night for a week means a heavy population immediately outside
and a specific gap.

**What is against the wall.** Mulch depth, how close it runs to the siding,
whether there is ivy or heavy ground cover, and what is lying on the soil. That
is most of the assessment before anybody arrives.

**Go out after dark with a torch**, an hour after sunset, and look at the mulch
bed against the house. If the surface is moving, you have found the population
and you have found where the work goes.

**Leave the perimeter as it is** until somebody has seen it. Clearing everything
the day before makes the visit less useful, and the point of the visit is to
tell you what to clear.
