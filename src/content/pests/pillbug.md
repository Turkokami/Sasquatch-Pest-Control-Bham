## The one fact that explains everything

A pillbug is not an insect.

It is a crustacean — order Isopoda, the same broad group as shrimp, crabs and
crayfish — and Iowa State's extension service puts the relationship plainly:
these animals are more closely related to shrimp and crayfish than they are to
insects. The University of Maryland goes a step further and describes sowbugs
and pillbugs as the only crustaceans that have fully adapted to life on land.

That is not a piece of trivia. It is the reason for every single thing this
animal does.

A crustacean breathes with gills. The pillbug's are modified into gill-like
structures on the underside of its abdomen, and the University of Idaho's
homeowner guide states the constraint exactly: the gills must be kept wet in
order to extract oxygen, which restricts these creatures to moist habitats.

An animal that has moved onto land while keeping a breathing apparatus that only
works wet has one non-negotiable requirement, and everything follows from it. It
lives under things. It is active at night. It stays in mulch, leaf litter,
compost and the damp layer against a foundation. It cannot cross open dry ground
for long. And it cannot survive inside a heated building.

So when you find one on the kitchen floor in November, you are looking at an
animal that has walked into an environment it cannot breathe in. It is not
establishing, it is not feeding, and it is not going to be there next week.

## What one looks like

Small, oval, armored, and instantly recognizable once you have the shape.

**Size.** UC IPM gives about a third to three-quarters of an inch; Maryland says
half to three-quarters. Either way, roughly the size of a pencil eraser up to
about a fingernail.

**Shape.** Oval, convex on top and flat underneath — a small dome. The back is a
series of overlapping hard plates, which is what makes the rolling possible and
what makes a dead one feel like a tiny shell rather than like a squashed insect.

**Color.** Slate gray through to nearly black, sometimes with a mottled or
lighter margin. The Pacific Northwest handbook describes the group as light to
dark gray with a hard shell.

**Legs.** Seven pairs, short, pale, tucked underneath and mostly invisible from
above. That count is the definitional difference from an insect and it is
countable on a dead specimen with a hand lens.

**Antennae.** One conspicuous pair, angled forward and jointed, waving as the
animal walks. They are surprisingly expressive for such a small creature.

**When disturbed.** It stops, tucks its legs and antennae in, and closes into a
sphere. This is called conglobation, and it is the single most reliable
identification in the whole of this library — nothing else in a Whatcom County
doorway does it.

## Pillbug or sowbug

These two live under the same board, arrive at the same threshold, and get the
same answer, but they are different animals and the separation takes a second.

**The rolling test.** The Pacific Northwest handbook reduces it to six words:
pillbugs ball up when disturbed, sowbugs do not. A sowbug cannot make the sphere
— it lacks the plate architecture for it — so it flattens against the surface or
runs instead.

**The tail test**, which works on a dead one that will not oblige. UC IPM's
version: a pillbug has a smooth rounded rear end, while a sowbug carries
distinct appendages protruding from its rear. Idaho's phrasing is that a
sowbug's paired tails are visibly extended and a pillbug's are too short to see
from above. Two small prongs sticking out the back means sowbug.

**Shape, roughly.** Sowbugs tend to look slightly flatter and more sprawling;
pillbugs sit higher and more domed. This is a weaker character than the other
two and not worth relying on.

Nothing else differs in any way a householder would care about. Both are
isopods, both are gill-breathers, both eat decaying plant material, both die
indoors, and both are handled with the same broom. When this page says pillbug,
read it as covering the sowbug too.

## Pillbug or millipede

The other regular confusion, and this one is not close once you look properly.

**Body plan.** A pillbug is short and oval — a few segments, wider than it is
tall, an armored disc with legs underneath. A millipede is long and worm-like,
round in cross-section, with dozens of segments running the length of it.

**Legs.** Seven pairs on a pillbug, total. A millipede carries two pairs on
nearly every segment, which adds up to a great many more and produces the
distinctive rippling wave as it moves.

**Defense.** A pillbug closes into a sphere. A millipede winds into a flat
spiral, like a watch spring lying on a table. Sphere against coil is the fastest
way to tell them apart with the animal in front of you.

**Speed and gait.** Both are slow. A pillbug trundles on visible legs; a
millipede flows.

**What happens if you crush one.** Nothing much with a pillbug. Some millipedes
release a defensive fluid that stains and can irritate skin and eyes, which is
the practical reason to vacuum rather than stamp on a line of them.

Everything else is shared. Both are detritivores working the same damp organic
layer, both are moisture-dependent, both die indoors within days, and both
arrive at the same doorways after the same weather. The
[millipede profile](/pest-library/millipede/) covers the autumn migrations in
detail, and if you have one animal you very often have the other.

## Where they actually live

Outside, in the layer of decomposing material against damp ground, and the list
of preferred addresses is short and consistent.

**Under bark mulch**, particularly where it is deep and has been down long
enough to break down into something that holds water.

**In leaf litter**, especially the band that accumulates along a foundation and
never sees enough sun or wind to dry.

**Under boards, pallets, pots, bricks, tarps and firewood** lying directly on
soil. Anything flat on damp ground is a pillbug hotel.

**In compost heaps and grass clipping piles**, which are close to perfect —
warm, wet, and made entirely of food.

**Under low dense ground cover** growing right up against siding.

**In wet crawlspaces**, which is the one that matters structurally, and which is
covered further down.

They feed on decaying organic material — the Pacific Northwest handbook's
phrasing is decaying organic matter, UC IPM's is decaying plant material — which
makes them part of the machinery that turns last year's leaves into soil. UC IPM
notes they will occasionally take seedlings, new roots, lower leaves, and fruit
or vegetables sitting on the ground, and Idaho compares the feeding damage to
slug damage, ragged holes chewed between the leaf veins. That is a real
gardening consideration in a wet spring and it is a separate question from
anything happening in a building.

## The life cycle, briefly, because it is strange

Worth two paragraphs because it is unlike anything else in this library and it
explains why the population is so persistent.

A female pillbug does not lay eggs into the soil. She carries them in a
fluid-filled pouch on her underside — the marsupium — and Idaho Extension
describes the eggs being brooded there for several weeks inside what is
effectively a small sac of water. The young hatch inside it and emerge as
miniature versions of the adult. It is a functioning tide pool carried around
under a land animal, which is about as clear a statement as biology makes about
where these things came from.

Growth is slow. Idaho records ten or more molts before adulthood, reached at
about two years, which is a long life for something that size. That slow
turnover means a population in a mulch bed is stable and long-lived rather than
exploding and crashing, and it is part of why the numbers arriving at a doorway
are so similar year after year on the same property.

## Why they come inside, and why they leave feet first

Two triggers, both about water.

The common one here is saturation. When heavy rain fills the pore space in the
litter layer they are living in, the ground stops being habitat and the animals
move. They travel at night, meet the long dry obstacle of a foundation, follow
it, pile up at corners and thresholds, and a proportion find a gap. In Whatcom
County that means the wet stretch from October into the winter, following a
genuinely heavy rain onto ground that is already loaded.

The less common one is the reverse — a dry spell driving them to seek moisture,
which in a Northwest August occasionally sends them under a door toward a cool
damp bathroom or laundry room.

Either way, the arrival is a mistake from the animal's point of view. A heated
house is drier than anything it has ever experienced, the gills stop working,
and it desiccates. Idaho gives a few days at most without high moisture; Maryland
says they dry out quickly and die indoors; Iowa State says they must have
moisture and die quickly without a damp place to hide. Three extension services,
one answer.

Which is why the correct response to a pillbug on a floor is a dustpan and
nothing else. There is no colony under the boards, nothing is feeding, nothing
is reproducing, and the animals present are already on a short clock whatever
anybody does.

## So: get a broom

Written as its own heading because it is the recommendation rather than an
aside.

Sweep them up, or vacuum them, which is tidier. Empty the canister outside, well
away from the wall, and get on with your day. If you would rather not kill them
at all, a piece of card and a cup works perfectly well — they are not fast, they
do not bite, and Iowa State's summary is that they cannot damage household
furnishings and are a nuisance only.

Do not treat the inside of the building. There is no reservoir behind the wall
producing more of them, the ones on the floor are dying, and any product applied
indoors is being applied to an animal that was already leaving.

And do not credit a treatment with the result. If a wet week ends and the
arrivals stop, that is the wet week ending. The ground drained.

## What actually changes next year

The durable work is all outdoors, all cheap, and all about how much wet organic
material sits against the building.

**Pull the mulch back off the wall and keep it thinner.** UC IPM's guidance is
to keep plants, mulch and other debris several inches back from foundations.
Deep bark run right to the siding is food, nursery and humid refuge in one
strip, positioned exactly where you least want the population.

**Clear the leaf litter from the foundation band.** Not the whole garden, the
strip against the building, which in a Northwest autumn never dries on its own.

**Get things off the soil.** Boards, pallets, pots, tarps, landscape fabric,
firewood stacked flat. The Pacific Northwest handbook's list for this group
starts with removing organic matter such as grass clippings, leaf litter and
bedding mulches, and that is the highest-yield hour anybody spends on this.

**Change when you irrigate.** UC IPM's specific recommendation is to reduce the
wetness of the soil surface and to water early in the day so surfaces are drier
by evening. These animals move at night, so a bed that is soaking at dusk is a
bed that is exporting pillbugs toward your door.

**Move the roof water.** Downspouts discharging clear of the building, gutters
running, and grade falling away from the foundation. A great deal of the
saturated soil that starts a movement is soil somebody is inadvertently
watering.

**Ventilate below.** The handbook recommends improving ventilation in basements
and crawl spaces, which is the part of this list that matters for the building
rather than for the doorstep.

**Then seal.** Door sweeps first — they stop most of what walks in — then
weatherstripping, vent screening, and utility penetrations. UC IPM lists weather
stripping and door sweeps as the entry-point answer, and the handbook adds
sealing gaps with caulk. That is
[exclusion and repair](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work, and the same
half-inch of gap is being used by several other things.

## What the pillbugs are telling you about the building

Here is the part worth reading even if the animal itself does not interest you.

A dozen pillbugs on a garage floor after a storm week is normal, and it is a
comment on the weather.

Pillbugs *living* under your house is a comment on the house. If they are coming
up through the floor rather than in under a door, or if the numbers in a
basement are steady all year rather than clustered after rain, then the space
below is wet enough to keep a gill-breathing animal alive continuously. That is a
finding about moisture, and moisture is the expensive part.

A crawlspace damp enough to support isopods year-round is damp enough for decay
fungi to work on the joists above it, and fungal decay is what actually takes
strength out of a building in this climate. The
[crawlspace guide](/guides/why-crawlspaces-here-stay-wet/) explains the mechanism
and [crawlspace restoration](/services/crawlspace-restoration/) is the fix.

The pillbug is not the problem in that scenario. It is the cheapest possible
early warning about one, and it costs nothing to read.

## Where this happens around the county

The property types that produce the heaviest arrivals have one thing in common,
and it is not the age or quality of the house. It is how much wet organic
material is banked against it.

**Recently landscaped subdivisions** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain, and the
newer blocks around Ferndale, where deep fresh bark went in against the siding
across a whole development in a single season. Those properties usually peak a
few years after planting, once the bark has broken down into something that
holds water and supports a population rather than sitting dry on top of the
soil.

**Lots against woodland, greenbelt or field** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach,
Edgemoor, the greenbelt margins, and the rural blocks toward Everson and Sumas.
A mature litter layer next door is a source with no ceiling on it, and the
building is simply the first hard edge in the way.

**Ground that collects water.** Anywhere the lot falls toward the house, the
downspouts discharge at a corner, or an irrigation zone soaks the strip beside
the wall. The saturation that starts an arrival is often local and often
self-inflicted.

**Prewar city housing** — the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York,
Roosevelt. A moderate number outside becomes a large number inside, because the
envelope at ground level is more permeable: original vents, aged sweeps, a
crawlspace hatch that has not seated properly in decades. On those houses the
useful work is the building rather than the yard.

**Properties with compost or livestock bedding near the house**, common on
acreage toward Lynden and Nooksack, where a heap fifteen feet from a back door
is a standing population fifteen feet from a back door.

## The year, as it runs here

**October into December.** The main event. Heavy rain onto ground that is
already loaded pushes them out of the litter layer, and the arrivals at
thresholds follow within a day or two of the weather. This is when the calls
come.

**January through March.** Steady low-level activity outdoors, and the occasional
arrival in a garage or a crawlspace-adjacent room. Nothing resets, because the
winter here is wet and mild rather than cold enough to matter.

**April and May.** Breeding and growth in the mulch. This is also when the
garden question comes up, because seedlings and low fruit are vulnerable on wet
soil at exactly the moment the population is most active.

**June through August.** Largely invisible. A genuinely dry August can produce a
small reverse movement toward cool damp rooms, which surprises people who
associate this animal with rain.

**Late summer** is the right time to do the work — pull the mulch back, lift what
is on the soil, fit the sweeps — because everything is dry, nothing is moving,
and the next wet season is the one it pays off in.

## Things that do not work

**Interior perimeter sprays.** Transient, dying animals with no reservoir behind
the wall.

**Bug bombs.** No reach into a mulch bed, which is the only place the population
exists, and product distributed through living space for nothing.

**Diatomaceous earth broadcast outdoors here.** It abrades the cuticle so the
animal dries out, which means it only works while it is itself dry. Scattered
around a foundation during a Whatcom County October it is finished after the
first rain. In a dry crawlspace or under a covered deck it has a narrow
legitimate use.

**Sealing without clearing.** Worth doing, not sufficient on its own. A tight
house standing in a saturated bark bed still has a large population pressing at
every joint.

**Waiting for a hard winter.** We do not get one. The litter layer here stays
productive through mild wet months and the population is never reset.

## When it is genuinely worth treating

Having spent the page recommending a dustpan, the other side.

**A site where the ground cannot be changed** — a lot backing onto woodland or a
field margin, a strata-managed landscape, a house sitting downhill of everything
around it. A perimeter application timed ahead of the wet season, rather than
during an arrival, genuinely reduces what gets through.

**A crawlspace or basement holding a continuous population**, where the answer
is drying rather than treating, and the pest work is the small part of a bigger
job.

**A commercial or institutional floor** — food handling, healthcare, hospitality
— where an animal on the floor is unacceptable regardless of what it does.

**A gardening problem rather than a household one**, where seedlings and low
fruit are being grazed in a wet spring. That is a different conversation with
different answers, most of which involve keeping fruit off wet soil.

Outside those, the verdict at the top of this page stands, and it is why we
would rather publish this than sell an autumn program to everyone whose garage
filled up after a storm.

## What we will not sell you

**A treatment for animals that cannot breathe in your house.** Everything
indoors is on a few days' clock.

**Credit for the weather.** Applications made on the last day of an arrival
produce a dramatic improvement the next morning, which would have happened
anyway.

**A story about damage.** There is none. Not structural, not fabric, not stored
food. The extension consensus is that they are a nuisance and nothing more.

**A recurring plan for a seasonal event** that a rake, a downspout extension and
a door sweep handle better and permanently than we can.
