## The wall that turns red and black

Somewhere in early October a Whatcom County homeowner walks around the corner of
their house and finds a couple of hundred insects packed onto the siding in the
afternoon sun. Flat, dark, half an inch long, marked with sharp red lines, sitting
shoulder to shoulder and not doing anything in particular.

That is the boxelder bug, and the number is genuinely startling the first time.
Everything else about it is mild. It has no jaws, no sting, no interest in your
food or your timber, and no capacity to produce a single new individual inside
your house.

This page exists to say that at length, because a wall covered in insects is one
of the easiest things in this trade to sell a treatment against, and it is mostly
not a treatment problem.

## Which boxelder bug

Worth a paragraph, because most of the boxelder bug writing online describes a
different animal from the one on your siding.

The insect on the eastern seaboard and across the Midwest is *Boisea trivittata*.
Ours is *Boisea rubrolineata*, the western boxelder bug, and the Pacific Northwest
Pest Management Handbooks — published jointly by WSU, Oregon State and the
University of Idaho — cover it under that name.

The two are close relatives with near-identical habits, so the general advice
travels. Where they differ is in the fine detail of markings and in the host lists
that matter locally, which is why the regional handbook is the source we work from
here rather than a national page.

## Recognizing one

**The adult** is about half an inch long, flat and elongated. The wings and thorax
are dark gray to black, overlaid with thin bright red or orange lines: a set
running lengthwise on the plate behind the head, a line down each side, and a
diagonal on each wing. Folded at rest, those lines resolve into a rough X or a
narrow triangle, depending on how the wings have settled.

**The nymphs** look like adults that have been left out in the rain — smaller,
wingless, and a startling bright red with dark markings that increase as they
grow. People often report them as a separate and more alarming insect. They are
not; they are the same species four weeks earlier.

**The eggs** are red too, laid in the bark crevices of host trees in spring. You
will rarely see them, and there is no reason to look.

The overall impression at a distance is of something narrow and dark with red
pinstriping, gathered in a dense patch rather than scattered. Both halves of that
description matter, because the clustering is as diagnostic as the color.

## What it is not

Three insects arrive on the same walls in the same fortnight here, and boxelder
bugs are the easiest of the three to place.

**Not a brown marmorated stink bug.** WSU's own identification publication, FS079E,
separates the two explicitly. The stink bug is a broad shield, mottled gray-brown,
with pale bands near the antenna tip and no red anywhere. It also tends to turn up
as scattered individuals rather than in a packed group.

**Not a western conifer seed bug.** That one is longer, brown, and carries an
obvious flattened widening on the hind leg. It also flies loudly, which a boxelder
bug does not.

**Not a beetle.** Boxelder bugs are true bugs with sucking mouthparts and
overlapping membranous wingtips, and they belong to a different family from the
stink bugs entirely. That is more than a taxonomic footnote: it is why they cannot
chew, cannot bore, and cannot do anything to a structure at all.

**Not a bed bug**, which we mention only because the two words sit close together
in an anxious search and the answer is worth having quickly. A bed bug is small,
oval, wingless and reddish-brown, it does not gather on the outside of a house, and
nothing about a boxelder bug's behavior resembles it.

## The trees, and how far they travel

These insects live on maples. The primary host is boxelder itself, *Acer negundo*,
with other maple species and ash as regular alternates. The handbook notes that in
high-population years they will also feed on developing fruit — apple, cherry,
peach, pear, plum — and its pear entry files the species as a minor orchard pest
where boxelder or maple stand nearby, particularly along riparian ground.

Damage to the trees is slight. The landscape entry describes little damage to
ornamentals beyond some leaf distortion, and notes that harm to host seed is
minimal because the damaged seeds drop later in the season anyway.

So the tree is where they come from and not something they are destroying. Which
brings up the question every second customer asks.

**Would removing the maple end this?** Almost certainly not, and we would rather
say so than sell the reasoning behind an expensive afternoon with an arborist. The
regional handbook records that adults fly several miles from their feeding sites.
Minnesota Extension puts it more bluntly still: removing boxelder trees in your
yard is not a good way to manage boxelder bugs, because the adults can travel a
couple of miles from their food source.

There is a contrary view in print. Colorado State's fact sheet suggests removing
female boxelder trees as the durable fix. We follow the regional source and the
flight-distance evidence: cutting one tree in a landscape full of maples changes
very little, and a mature tree is worth more than an October inconvenience. If a
seed-bearing boxelder is coming out anyway for another reason, fine — but do not
take it out on the strength of this.

## The year they have

One generation, or possibly two, and the sources are not unanimous.

The handbook's boxelder entry states there is only one generation per year. Its
maple entry describes two. Both cannot be right for the same latitude, and in a
cool maritime climate with a short warm season, one is the safer expectation for
Whatcom County. We flag the disagreement rather than picking the more dramatic
number, because the practical advice does not change either way.

The cycle runs like this. Adults that survived the winter emerge as the weather
steadies and move to their host trees, where the females lay red eggs into bark
crevices. Nymphs hatch and feed through the summer on leaves, flowers, twigs and
then, most importantly, on the developing seeds. By late August and September the
new adults are mature, and it is that cohort — well-fed, fully grown and looking
for shelter — that arrives on your siding.

Only fully grown adults get through a winter. Any nymph still on a tree in November
is not going to become anything.

## Why they choose your wall

The mechanism is heat, and it is the same for every overwintering insect in this
library.

On an autumn afternoon a sunlit wall is measurably warmer than the ground and the
vegetation around it, and a light-colored south or west elevation is warmest of
all. Insects motivated to find shelter move up the thermal gradient and end up
concentrated on that surface, which is why the north side of the same building
stays empty.

From the wall, they explore. Any crevice gets investigated and any crevice that
leads somewhere gets used — the joint where a window frame meets siding, a lifted
lap, the open end of a vinyl panel, a soffit return nobody closed, a vent screen
that rusted through some years ago. The handbook's own management advice for this
species is to repair screens and caulk around windows and doors, and to screen
soffits, corner trim and attic vents, in summer.

Then they go still. Adults overwinter in protected sites — hollows in trees,
accumulated debris, and the cracks and crevices of buildings — and stay largely
inactive until something warms them.

## What they do indoors, which is nothing

Set this out plainly, because the fear is usually larger than the fact.

They do not feed. There is no plant tissue in a wall cavity and nothing in a
kitchen cupboard interests an insect built to drain maple seeds. Colorado State
notes they may attempt to feed on a houseplant without causing damage, which is
about the limit of it.

They do not breed. Reproduction happens on the host tree in spring, not in your
attic, and the population inside a building can only shrink across a winter.

They do not damage anything structural. No chewing, no boring, no nesting, no
tunneling.

The one genuine cost is marking. The handbook warns that their body fluids stain
fabrics and surfaces, and Minnesota Extension adds that they can stain walls,
curtains and other surfaces with their excrement. On white trim, pale curtains or
a light sofa arm that is a real annoyance, and it is the whole reason for the
advice below.

## Vacuum, do not crush

The correct tool is a vacuum cleaner and the correct instinct is not to squash.

Crushed boxelder bugs mark light-colored surfaces and produce an unpleasant odor.
Minnesota Extension's position once they are indoors is that the only practical
control is physical removal, by vacuum or broom, and that insecticides are not
recommended once the bugs have emerged into the inside of the house.

Empty the canister outdoors and seal what is in it, because a proportion of them
will still be alive.

While we are here, a related point that gets misapplied from a neighboring species:
the National Pesticide Information Center states that killing one of these
overwintering bugs does not attract more, and that the odor released will not
attract other bugs. Crush them if you must — you will simply have a mark on the
wall and a smell in the room, and no further consequence.

## Why our verdict is that treating is rarely warranted

The line at the top of this page says rarely worth treating, and it is not
modesty. It is what the regional handbook says.

Its household entry on this species records that insecticide treatment has not been
very successful, that residual sprays applied by a professional may reduce entry
but will not remove the population, and that treating infested trees near a
structure has limited effect. That is the pest management handbook for this
region — written by three land-grant universities, with nothing to sell — telling
you that the product is the weak half of the answer.

What we can honestly offer is two things.

**An exterior application in late August or early September**, to the elevations
they gather on, before the gathering starts. It reduces the number that get in. It
is a residual on siding in a climate with thirty-five inches of rain, so it does
not last the season, and we would rather you knew that at the point of sale than
in January.

**The sealing**, which is the half that keeps working. Boxelder bugs are small,
slow and toothless, so caulk and intact insect screen are enough — no hardware
cloth, no sheet metal. That is [exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work,
it is bought once, and it closes the same openings that admit cluster flies and
overwintering stink bugs.

What we will not do is treat the wall void once they are in it. Very little reaches
a dormant insect wedged in a cavity, and a treatment that worked would leave an
accumulation of dead insects in an inaccessible space for carpet beetles to breed
in. Swapping a staining nuisance for a fabric-destroying one is a poor trade at any
price.

## The spring repeat

In March or April they come back, and it looks like the treatment failed.

It did not. Minnesota Extension describes the surviving overwintered bugs becoming
active, attempting to move outdoors, and a good many becoming trapped inside
instead. What you are watching is the population leaving, or trying to.

Vacuum them, open a window on the sunny side if that is practical, and know that
by May the building will be empty of them until the new adults mature at the end
of summer. Nothing you do in spring affects next autumn. The month that affects
next autumn is August.

## Around this county

**Riparian ground and the river corridors.** Boxelder is a floodplain tree, and the
Nooksack corridor, the sloughs and the wet margins around Everson, Nooksack and
Deming carry the host in quantity. Properties on that ground see the heaviest
autumn numbers in the county.

**Rural and agricultural Whatcom** — Lynden, Everson, Sumas. Maples in shelter
belts and hedgerows, outbuildings with generous gaps, and the handbook's pear note
is relevant wherever an orchard sits near that kind of planting.

**Older city stock** in the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland and York, where
street maples are mature and original window framing gives them a hundred ways in.

**Newer developments** around Ferndale, Lynden and the Bellingham edge, where the
walls are large, pale and sunlit but the detailing is tight, so plenty land and
comparatively few get through.

**Waterfront and seasonal property** at Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo. A house
closed up from October to April accumulates a population with nobody to notice, and
the owner meets the whole winter's arrivals at once on a spring windowsill.

## Why it is always the same wall

Two things fix the location, and neither of them is anything you have done.

The first is geometry. The elevation that held the afternoon sun last October holds
it this October, the tree line that shades the north side has not moved, and the
openings the insects used are still open. A building is a very stable object from
an insect's point of view.

The second is that these are gregarious animals. They gather in dense clusters
rather than spreading out, both on the wall and in whatever cavity they settle into,
and a site that has worked before tends to keep working. That is why a household
can describe the affected corner of the building to us over the phone with real
precision, year after year, and why we can usually predict which side of the house
we will be working on before arriving.

The useful consequence is that this is a predictable problem rather than a random
one. If a house gathered them last autumn it will gather them this autumn, which
means the sealing can be scheduled in August on the strength of last year's
experience instead of waiting to see what happens.

## Children, pets and the things people worry about

The questions arrive in a fairly fixed order and they all have short answers.

**Are they dangerous to a dog or a cat?** No. An animal that eats one is eating an
insect with an unpleasant defensive taste and nothing else. There is no venom and
no toxicity of consequence, and the usual outcome is a pet that does not try it
twice.

**Can a child pick one up?** Yes. They are slow, they do not sting, and the rare
bite reports in the literature involve handled insects rather than aggression. A
child holding a boxelder bug will end up with an unremarkable insect on a palm and
possibly a faint smell on their fingers.

**Do they carry disease?** No. They feed on maple seeds. There is no route from a
seed to a person.

**Will they get into food?** They have no interest in it and no equipment for it.
An individual might wander across a counter on the way to a window, which is a
hygiene inconvenience rather than a contamination event.

**Do they bite the way a bed bug does?** They do not feed on blood at all, and
nothing in a boxelder bug's biology has any use for a person.

This is one of the better animals in the county to be relaxed about, and a
household that gets comfortable with the identification stops paying attention to
them within a season or two, which is the outcome we would prefer.

## When it is worth a phone call anyway

Given everything above, there are still three situations where we would rather
hear from you.

**When the identification is not settled.** A dense group of dark insects on a wall
is worth a photograph, because a boxelder bug and a brown marmorated stink bug
carry entirely different implications — one is a nuisance and one is worth
reporting to WSU. Photograph one on a flat surface next to a coin, and the answer
usually takes seconds.

**When they are getting into rooms in quantity.** Insects reaching living space
means there is a route from the cavity into the house — around a recessed light
housing, a bathroom fan surround, an attic hatch, the top of an interior wall. That
is worth closing, and it is a different job from sealing the outside.

**When there is an accumulation.** A building that has hosted these for years may
have a quantity of dead insects in an attic or a wall, and dead insects feed carpet
beetles, which do damage wool and textiles. Where the accumulation is accessible it
is worth clearing as part of the sealing work rather than as an emergency.

Outside those three, a broom and a vacuum in October and a call in August is the
whole of it, and we will say so on the phone rather than booking a visit that
cannot change anything.

## What is not worth your money

**A fogger.** It does not reach a wall void, which is where they are, and it puts
product through the rooms, which is where they mostly are not.

**Ultrasonic devices.** Nothing.

**An interior residual through the winter.** They are dormant, not foraging, so
they are not crossing treated surfaces in any useful pattern.

**Sealing in November.** Understandable and backwards. It shuts in what is already
there and stops the warm-day escapees leaving. Minnesota Extension's timing advice
is to make the repairs before the end of August, and that is the right instruction.

**Removing a healthy tree**, for the reasons above.

**Anything at all, in a light year.** Some autumns they barely appear. The handbook
attributes the swing to environmental conditions, and it means a quiet October is
weather rather than an achievement — yours or ours.

## The short version

Black with red lines, in a crowd, on the sunny wall, in October. Harmless. Not
breeding indoors, not eating anything, not damaging the structure, effectively not
biting. They stain, so use a vacuum rather than your hand.

The one thing that changes next winter is closing the gaps in late summer, and the
one thing we would talk you out of is cutting down a maple to solve it.
