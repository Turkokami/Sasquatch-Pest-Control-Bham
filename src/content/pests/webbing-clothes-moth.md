## One fact explains the whole page

Clothes moth larvae digest keratin. Everything else here follows from that.

Keratin is the structural protein in animal hair and skin, which in a house means
wool, cashmere, mohair, silk, fur, feathers, felt, leather and the natural
bristles in a good brush. Kentucky's extension fact sheet puts it plainly: these
insects feed on animal fibers because those fibers contain a protein the larvae
can digest. Cotton, linen, viscose, nylon, polyester and acrylic contain none of
it, and a larva put on a clean cotton shirt will starve on it.

That is why the damage in a real infestation is so selective, and why households
describe the pattern as though it were malicious. The wool coat is ruined. The
cotton shirts hanging against it are perfect. A blended sock with thirty percent
wool is grazed in patches and the rest of the fiber is left as a skeleton.

Hold on to the second half of the rule as well, because it is where the
exceptions live. UC IPM records that fabrics carrying food, perspiration or urine
stains are more subject to damage, and Kentucky adds that synthetics are attacked
when heavily soiled with food stains or body oils. Soiling puts digestible
residue onto a fiber that had none.

So there are two questions worth asking about any damaged item: was it animal
fiber, and was it dirty. One of those two is nearly always yes.

## It came in with something

This is the honest framing for the whole fabric-and-pantry group, and it is worth
saying before anybody starts thinking about the building.

A clothes moth problem is not a structural failure. It is not a sign that a house
is badly sealed, badly kept or badly built. Whatcom County houses are not
colonized by moths drifting in off the Nooksack. What happens instead is that
keratin and an egg-laying female end up in the same dark, still place — and the
keratin was almost always already yours.

The usual routes in are a secondhand wool rug, a box of textiles out of a
relative's attic, a bag of vintage clothing, a taxidermy piece, a wool item
bought abroad, or a felt-lined case that has been in a garage since a house move.
The Pacific Northwest handbook specifically flags caution with imported woolens
and recommends dry cleaning them before they go into storage.

The other route is the one covered further down: a bird or rodent nest already
inside the structure, quietly supplying feathers and hair for years.

Both of those are findable. Neither of them is fixed by treating a room, which is
why this page spends more words on searching than on spraying.

## The adult, and why the moth at your lamp is not it

The adult is small and unremarkable. UC IPM gives a wingspan of about half an
inch and a resting length of roughly a quarter inch, gold in color, with reddish
golden hairs on the top of the head and a row of golden hairs fringing the wings.
The Pacific Northwest handbook describes them as yellow or tan and about half an
inch across the wings. Kentucky calls them beige or buff, with narrow wings
fringed with small hairs.

Two behavioral facts matter more than the color.

**They avoid light.** UC IPM describes them as weak flyers that are not attracted
to lights. Kentucky says adults are seldom seen because they avoid light and
prefer dark undisturbed areas such as closets, basements and attics. So a moth
tapping at a lit window, circling a lamp or gathering on a porch light in July is
almost certainly an outdoor species that came through a door. Discount it.

**They do not eat.** Kentucky states that clothes moth adults do not feed and
cause no injury to fabrics. The adult exists to mate and lay eggs. This is the
fact that explains the household that swatted moths all summer and still found
holes in October — the swatting was aimed at the wrong life stage.

Their flight is weak, low and fluttering, and they duck into shadow when a light
comes on. That evasive scuttle is a more reliable identifier than any wing
marking a person is likely to see without a lens.

## The larva does everything

Kentucky describes the damaging stage as creamy-white caterpillars up to half an
inch long. UC IPM calls the larvae shiny white with dark colored head capsules.

The webbing species is named for what it leaves. Rather than carrying a portable
case around the way its casemaking cousin does, this larva spins patches of silk
where it feeds, and those patches accumulate fiber fragments and fecal pellets
until they read as a grubby mat or a soft tube glued to the surface. The Pacific
Northwest handbook describes the larvae as wormlike and sometimes encased in
silken tubes.

**Webbing is the evidence.** Not the moth, not the holes on their own. Silk on a
wool surface, with grit in it, means larvae have been feeding on that item.
Kentucky notes that both clothes moth types leave tiny fecal pellets, and those
pellets take on the color of whatever the larva has been eating, which is a small
and useful detail when a rug is a strong color.

Development is slow and wildly variable, which is the practical problem.
Kentucky's range is one month to as much as two years. UC IPM records larvae
molting anywhere from five to forty-five times over a period lasting thirty-five
days to two and a half years, with egg-to-egg development typically four to six
months and usually two generations a year. A cool Bellingham basement sits at the
slow end of every one of those ranges.

That is why a single burst of cleaning can look like it worked and then be proved
wrong in spring.

## Where the damage concentrates, and why

Put the keratin rule together with the light rule and the map draws itself.

**Under collars, in cuffs, along folds and in seams.** These are the parts of a
garment that hold body oils and sweat, and they are also physically sheltered.
Both conditions point the same way, which is why holes cluster there rather than
in the middle of a panel.

**The dark end of a closet.** The rail nobody reaches, the shelf above the rail,
the garment bag that has not been unzipped since a wedding.

**Under furniture that never moves.** A wool rug is grazed at the edge that lives
under the sofa and is untouched in the walked-on center. UC IPM's carpet beetle
guidance makes the same point about inspecting carpeted areas beneath heavy
furniture and along carpet edges, and it applies equally here.

**Inside stored boxes.** Cardboard in a garage, an attic or a crawlspace hatch,
holding blankets, uniforms, a christening gown or a folded rug.

**Wool felt in odd places.** UC IPM specifically lists wool felt pads in pianos,
animal bristles in brushes and fish meal in fish food alongside the obvious
clothing and carpets.

The unifying condition is stillness. An item worn weekly is effectively safe. The
things that get eaten are the things nobody has touched since the last time the
season turned.

## The Whatcom County version of the season

Our climate changes the timing rather than the biology, and it changes it in one
direction: toward slower and longer.

There is no hard freeze here to reset anything. A garage, a crawlspace or an
unheated back bedroom in Ferndale or Lynden sits cool and damp for months without
ever getting cold enough to kill an insect that Kentucky says survives a week at
zero degrees only under duress. So an infestation in unheated storage does not
die back over winter. It idles, and idling for this species can last a year or
more.

Meanwhile the heated part of the house stays warm and dry enough for two
generations a year, which is UC IPM's typical figure.

The call pattern that produces is predictable. February and March, when winter
woolens come out of storage and the damage from last spring is discovered.
Then again in October, when the same items go back in — which is the moment that
actually decides what next February looks like.

The long gray season also keeps a great deal of textile in storage rather than in
use, and that is the raw material this insect needs. A drier climate with a real
summer gets things aired, shaken and moved. Ours does not.

## Clothes moth or carpet beetle

Both eat keratin, both damage the same items, and both feed in the dark. They are
separated by what they leave behind rather than by what they ate.

**Silk webbing means moth.** Patches, tubes, or a matted crust of fiber and
frass on the surface of the material.

**Bristly cast skins mean beetle.** UC IPM's carpet beetle guidance is direct
about this: carpet beetle larvae leave brown, shell-like, bristly-looking cast
skins when they molt, and those skins together with a lack of webbing are usually
good clues that carpet beetles are responsible.

The damage itself differs a little as well. Moth larvae tend to chew through in
one place and leave a defined hole or a grazed patch under a mat of silk. Beetle
larvae are more likely to thin the pile of a rug across an irregular area and to
work in places with more light than a moth will accept.

If you find both, believe both — a house with old wool and a nest in a void
supports the two species side by side, and that is common rather than unusual.
The [carpet beetle](/pest-library/carpet-beetle/) profile covers that species in
full, including the skin reaction that gets read as bites.

## The other clothes moth, briefly

There is a second species and it is worth being able to name, because the
evidence looks different enough to confuse somebody who has only read about the
first.

The casemaking clothes moth, *Tinea pellionella*, is close to the same size. UC
IPM describes its wings as more brownish with faint dark colored spots, and the
hairs on its head as lighter than those of the webbing species. The real
difference is the larva: it builds a small silken case and carries it around as
it feeds, enlarging the case as it grows, so what you find is not a mat of silk
on the fabric but a little tapered tube lying on the surface or attached to it,
often colored by whatever the larva has been eating.

Finding those cases is unmistakable once you have seen one, and it changes
nothing about what to do. The food is the same keratin, the storage habits are
the same, and laundering, freezing and sealed containers work identically on
both. We separate them for identification rather than for treatment.

The webbing species is much the commoner of the two in houses here, which is why
this profile is written about it.

## And it is definitely not the pantry moth

The other confusion runs toward the kitchen, and one question settles it.

Does it go to light? The [Indianmeal moth](/pest-library/indianmeal-moth/) does,
which is precisely why people notice it flying across a room in the evening. The
clothes moth does not, and will head for shadow when disturbed.

The second check is color. An Indianmeal moth forewing is two-toned — UC IPM
describes reddish brown wings with a coppery luster on the outer two-thirds and
whitish gray on the inner portion — and once seen it is unmistakable. A webbing
clothes moth is one plain gold or buff shade all over, without markings.

The third check is the room. Webbing in a cereal box is a pantry moth. Webbing on
a wool coat is this one. They do not swap.

## Heat, cold and laundering — the actual numbers

Domestic tools work on this insect, and the figures are specific enough to be
worth writing down.

**Laundering.** UC IPM gives twenty to thirty minutes in water that is at least
one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. Kentucky's summary is that dry cleaning or
hot laundering kills any eggs or larvae present, and that a clothes dryer's heat
is effective as well. Washable wool is the easy case, and most household
infestations are mostly washable wool.

**Oven or applied heat.** UC IPM gives at least thirty minutes at temperatures
higher than one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. This suits a rigid item — a
brush, a felt pad, a hat block — and suits almost nothing that is old, dyed,
glued or structured.

**Freezing.** Kentucky gives zero degrees Fahrenheit for at least one week, and
adds that freezers reaching minus twenty degrees kill all life stages within
seventy-two hours. UC IPM gives several days below eighteen degrees. The Pacific
Northwest handbook gives seventy-two hours at minus twenty. Bag the item, press
the air out, freeze it, and for a valuable piece let it warm fully and freeze it
a second time — the second cycle catches anything the first only stunned.

The failure mode with freezing is the appliance rather than the method. A
fridge-top freezer compartment that is opened daily may never hold zero, and a
chest freezer will. If you do not know what yours runs at, a cheap thermometer
left inside overnight answers it.

## Mothballs and cedar, said honestly

Both are trusted far beyond what they do.

**Mothballs** are a fumigant. UC IPM notes the active ingredient and states that
they work only in airtight containers, and the Pacific Northwest handbook says
the same while warning that long-term exposure may have health effects for
people. That is the whole story: in a sealed box, at a concentration you would
not want to breathe, they work. Scattered on a closet shelf they scent the wool
and achieve very little else. The smell then outlives the moths by years.

**Cedar** deters egg-laying while the aromatic oils are fresh. The Pacific
Northwest handbook allows that a cedar chest may work if it is tightly fitted,
which is the important qualifier — the container matters more than the timber.
Cedar does not kill larvae that are already feeding, and the oils fade. A cedar
closet lined decades ago and never sanded is joinery, not protection.

Neither is a response to an active infestation. Cleaning, freezing and sealed
storage are.

## Clean, then store — the entire prevention strategy

If you take one action from this page, take this one, because it costs nothing
and it removes the conditions rather than the insects.

**Clean everything before it goes away.** Wash or dry clean each wool, silk, fur
or felt item at the end of the season it was worn in. This is the single highest
leverage habit available, and it works because it removes the perspiration and
food residue that make one garment more attractive than its clean neighbor.

**Store in something that actually closes.** Rigid boxes with fitted lids or
zipped bags that seal, rather than cardboard cartons and the dry cleaner's
polythene. The Pacific Northwest handbook recommends containers with tight
fitting lids for stored woolens.

**Do not store anything soiled, ever.** A single unwashed item can seed a
container of clean ones.

**Vacuum before you refill a space.** Closet floors, shelf corners, carpet edges,
baseboard lines and heating vents, where lint and hair collect. Empty the machine
outdoors.

**Move things once a year.** Lift the rug under the sofa, unfold the blankets,
shake out the coats. Handling is genuinely preventive because stillness is the
condition the whole problem depends on.

**Monitor rather than guess.** Pheromone traps catch adult males, and UC IPM
describes them as attracting males with a sex attractant. They tell you whether
anything is still flying. They are a measuring instrument and not a control, and
anything sold as though a trap alone will finish the job is oversold.

## When treatment is warranted, and when it is not

This species carries a verdict of *depends*, and it depends on two things.

**A cleaning job — most cases.** Damage confined to identifiable items, no
recurrence after the wash-freeze-store cycle, no wool carpet, no unexplained
source. Launder, freeze what cannot be laundered, vacuum thoroughly, re-store
properly, hang a monitor, and check again in three months. A household that does
this competently does not need us and should not pay for us.

**A treatment job — the minority.** Wall-to-wall wool carpet or a large rug where
the feeding site is under fixed furniture and cannot practically be lifted.
Repeated damage in the same closet after a genuine cleaning effort. A large
textile collection where the cost of being wrong is high. And above all, a
population that is being fed from inside the structure.

That last one is the case where calling somebody is straightforwardly the right
answer. Kentucky lists bird or animal nests in an attic, chimney or wall cavity
among the places infestations develop, and a nest is a standing supply of
feathers and hair that no amount of laundering reaches. Removing the nest is the
work; the treatment is a footnote to it. Where a rodent is involved that is
[rodent control](/services/rodent-control/), and the
[exclusion](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) side of it matters because closing
a hole without taking the nest out leaves the food in place.

What we apply, when we apply anything, goes on structure — closet framing, carpet
edges, baseboard voids, the perimeter of a fixed rug. Not on clothing, not on
bedding, and not broadcast across a floor.

## What we will not do

**Spray your wardrobe.** Garments are worn against skin and there is no version
of this problem where treating them is the right method.

**Treat a closet and leave the garments alone.** The larvae and eggs are on the
fabric. Product on the shelving does not reach them, and a closet treated without
the cleaning is a closet that will produce damage again.

**Sell you a trap as a cure.** Pheromone monitors measure. They do not clear an
infestation and we will say so before you buy any.

**Promise a single visit settles it.** Development runs from a month to two years
depending on the temperature, so the honest plan has a follow-up in it and judges
the result across a season.

## What a result looks like on this species

Development this slow makes progress genuinely hard to read, so it is worth
naming what counts as evidence and what does not.

**No new damage on items that were previously being fed on.** Mark or photograph
a damaged edge so you have something to compare against, because memory is
unreliable about whether a hole has grown.

**Monitors quiet across consecutive checks**, not one. A single empty trap in a
cool month means nothing much.

**Storage that opens clean a season later.** This is the strongest signal
available to a household, and it takes a season to collect, which is the point.

The weakest signal is the absence of adults. They are short-lived, sporadic and
easy to miss even when a population is healthy, so not having seen one since
April is not information.

Judge the whole thing on a year rather than a fortnight. A closet that looks
settled in June and produces fresh holes in the following March had a feeding
site that was never found, and that is worth going back for rather than
re-treating the same space.

## Where to start

Look for silk and tell us whether you found any, because that one observation
separates this species from the beetle and changes what the visit involves.

Note where the damaged items were sitting and what was next to them. Do not empty
the closet first — where the feeding happened is most of the information, and a
cleared shelf tells nobody anything.

If there is no wool in the house and the damage keeps happening anyway, say that
too. It moves the search off the contents and into the structure, which is where
the answer usually is in that situation.
