## The name is wrong, and the mistake is expensive

Almost everything people believe about this insect follows from a word that does
not apply to it.

It is not a hornet. WSU Hortsense states the point without hedging — technically
a yellowjacket, not a hornet — and it belongs to the genus *Dolichovespula*,
which makes it a first cousin of the aerial yellowjacket and a slightly more
distant relative of the ground-nesting species that causes most of the trouble
in this county. What it has is the size, the coloring and the reputation of
something rarer and more dangerous.

True hornets are *Vespa*. Penn State Extension records only one established in
North America, the European hornet, and its range is the eastern half of the
country rather than anything on this coast. The other *Vespa* that reached
Washington, the northern giant hornet, was found in this county and dealt with:
four nests located and destroyed in 2020 and 2021, three subsequent survey years
without a detection, and a formal announcement in December 2024 that the species
is no longer present in the United States.

So when somebody in Ferndale photographs a large black insect and calls it a
hornet, the accurate answer is that they have a big yellowjacket and the county
has no hornets in it at all.

That correction is not pedantry, and it changes three practical expectations.

**It changes what you expect from the colony.** People assume a nest that large
holds a proportionate number of insects. It does not, and the numbers further
down this page are the reason.

**It changes what you expect from the behavior.** A hornet in the popular
imagination hunts people. This insect defends a radius and ignores everything
outside it.

**It changes where you expect to meet it.** The wasp at an outdoor table in
September is not this species, and knowing that stops a good deal of unnecessary
alarm about a nest in a tree that has nothing to do with the problem at the
table.

## What it looks like

Bigger than anything else in the family here, and the color is the giveaway
before the size is.

The body is black, marked in ivory white rather than yellow — a broad pale
pattern across the face, which is where the name comes from, and further white
banding on the thorax, the legs and the last segments of the abdomen. Penn State
puts colony members at 13 to 20 millimeters depending on caste, with queens the
largest, so a worker is somewhere around three quarters of an inch and a queen
seen in spring is genuinely startling.

The flight is heavier and slower than a common yellowjacket's, which is part of
why the insect reads as a different order of thing to people who have only ever
seen the small yellow ones.

There is no yellow on it anywhere. That single fact resolves most
identifications sent to us as photographs.

## The nest, which is the thing people actually see

Very few households ever identify the insect. They identify the structure, and
it is the most recognizable object in this whole subject.

Chewed wood fiber worked into a gray, layered, papery envelope, roughly
spherical to teardrop-shaped, with tiers of horizontal comb suspended inside it
and a single entrance low on the underside. Penn State records nests reaching 24
inches in height and 18 inches across in Pennsylvania, though most of what turns
up on residential property here is smaller than that.

Position varies more than people expect. Penn State describes nests in bushes
and shrubbery at least three feet off the ground, and in trees as high as sixty
feet or more. WSU's own bulletin puts the species in trees or on the outside
eaves of buildings, which is the version that generates most calls, because a
nest on a house is a nest somebody walks under.

The envelope grows outward through the season as workers add layers, so a nest
that looked like a golf ball in May and a grapefruit in July is a football by
the end of August. The size you find it at is a record of how long it has been
there, not of how dangerous it is.

## The number that surprises everybody

Here is where the hornet framing does the most damage.

Penn State describes bald-faced hornet colonies growing through the summer to
somewhere between one hundred and four hundred workers, with Pennsylvania
colonies commonly running to about three hundred individuals. That is the whole
population of that enormous gray structure.

Now set it against the species that causes most of the stings in Whatcom County.
WSU's Extension bulletin records western yellowjacket nests reaching as many as
five thousand workers and fifteen thousand cells at their August and September
peak — a colony an order of magnitude larger, living in a hole in a lawn that
nobody can see.

The insect that looks like the serious problem has the small colony and
advertises its position. The insect nobody notices has the large one and hides
it under the grass. That inversion is worth carrying around, because it is the
opposite of how these two get ranked by everybody who has not thought about it.

None of which makes a bald-faced hornet nest harmless at close range. Several
hundred defensive insects is plenty. It does mean that the alarm a big paper
nest generates is out of proportion to the colony inside it, and that a
household deciding what to do should be weighing position rather than volume.

## What it does with its summer, and where it does not go

This is the most useful thing on the page and it is almost never said.

UC IPM makes a distinction between the two groups of yellowjackets that matters
enormously in practice: the aerial nesters do not become scavengers at the end
of the season. The ground-nesting species flip to sugar and meat scavenging as
brood rearing winds down, which is what puts them at bins, fallen fruit, cans
and plates from mid-August. *Dolichovespula* does not make that switch. It stays
a predator through to the end of the colony.

So the black and white wasp with the nest in your maple is not the reason wasps
are on the picnic table. It never was. Those are almost always *Vespula*, from a
nest in the ground or in a wall, and treating the visible nest in the tree will
not change anything about the table.

We say this often enough on the phone that it is worth putting in writing,
because the sequence goes the same way every year. Somebody is being bothered by
wasps at an outdoor eating area in late summer. Somebody else spots the big gray
nest twenty feet up in the same yard. The two get connected, the nest gets
treated, and the wasps at the table carry on exactly as before, because the
actual colony is an unmarked hole in a bank behind the shed.

What this species does instead is hunt. Workers take flies, caterpillars and
other soft-bodied insects, and they will take other yellowjackets given the
chance, which is a service worth having on a property in the same season the
ground nesters are at their worst. WSU also records the adults visiting flowers
in late summer, which is where most people encounter one away from the nest and
never realize what they are looking at.

## Defensiveness, in proportion

The reputation is earned close in and unearned everywhere else.

Within a few paces of the nest this is a species that responds hard and in
numbers, sustains the response for a while, and follows. Penn State's practical
threshold — nests within about ten feet of a building entrance are the ones to
deal with — is a reasonable working figure for how much space the colony
considers its own.

Step outside that and the picture reverses completely. A worker foraging on a
shrub, on a flower head or along a fence line is not defending anything and has
no interest in whoever is standing there. People share gardens with these
colonies all summer without incident, which is exactly what makes the leaf-fall
discovery so common: the nest was never a problem, it was simply invisible.

The conditions that shorten the fuse are worth knowing. Vibration through
whatever the nest is attached to, which means hedge trimmers, a ladder against
the same wall, a chainsaw in the same tree. Direct light at the entrance after
dark. Repeated close passage along a flight line. Weather that keeps the colony
at home. And the last few weeks of the season, when there is nothing left to
gain from tolerance.

## The nest hanging in a bare tree in December

This is where a pest control company either tells you the truth or takes your
money, so here it is plainly.

The colony is annual. It runs from a single overwintered queen in spring to
several hundred workers in late summer, produces new queens and males, and then
ends with the cold. WSU's bulletin describes the abandoned nests decomposing and
disintegrating over the winter, and states that they are not used again.

Not "usually not." Not "sometimes." A bald-faced hornet nest is a single-use
structure. Next April's queens each start something new somewhere else, and a
queen that overwintered forty feet from last year's football has no more
interest in it than you do.

Which means the nest that becomes visible when the leaves come down in late
October or November is, in the great majority of cases, already finished or
within weeks of it. There is nothing in it to treat. There will not be anything
in it next year. Weather and birds take it apart over the winter, and if it
irritates you to look at, it can come down with a pole in January at no risk to
anybody.

How to tell whether it is genuinely done, from the ground and from a distance:

**Watch the entrance for a few minutes on a mild afternoon.** A live colony
produces continuous two-way traffic at the hole. A finished one produces
nothing, however long you stand there.

**Look at the envelope.** An active nest is clean, layered and pale gray. A
finished one weathers to a mottled brown, softens in the rain, and frequently
shows a torn opening where a bird has been through the side of it for the brood.

**Consider the date.** Once we are past a hard frost or two, the arithmetic is
already decided.

If somebody has quoted you for removing a nest in December without mentioning
any of that, the quote is for taking down a paper shell, and you are entitled to
know that is what you are buying.

## The April version of the same nest

Everything on this page describes an August problem, and there is a version of
it in April that almost nobody acts on because almost nobody looks.

In the first warm stretch of spring, a queen that mated the previous autumn and
spent the winter under bark or in a sheltered cavity comes out and starts
building on her own. There are no workers. There is one insect, a stalk, a few
cells and a thin paper cap around them, and the whole thing is the size of a
golf ball or smaller. She forages, builds, lays and broods the first generation
herself, and until those workers emerge the colony has no capacity to defend
anything.

That window runs roughly from the first settled weather through May, and it is
the cheapest moment in the entire year to deal with this species. A starter nest
under a porch roof or in the crotch of a shrub can be knocked off with a pole,
from a distance, by a homeowner, with essentially none of the risk that the same
location carries in September.

The reason it does not happen is that nobody is looking at the underside of
their eaves in April. So here is the short version of where to look, once, on a
mild afternoon in spring: the sheltered face of every eave and soffit return;
the inside of an open shed, carport or barn; the underside of a deck and its
rails; the top of a porch or a bay window roof; and the dense middle of any
evergreen shrub near the house.

Ten minutes, once a year. It is worth more than any product anybody can sell
you, and it applies equally to every wasp on this site that builds where you can
see it.

## Aerial yellowjacket, and why the nest will not tell you

The one species this gets genuinely confused with is its close relative, and the
confusion runs in a direction people do not expect.

Both build an enclosed gray paper envelope above ground. Both hang it in a
shrub, a tree or under an eave. Both put the entrance at the bottom. Looking at
the nest, from any normal distance, will not separate them — which is why
"football-shaped gray nest" is a description of a genus rather than a species.

The insect separates them instantly. Black with ivory-white markings is this
page's species. Banded yellow and black is the
[aerial yellowjacket](/pest-library/aerial-yellowjacket/), which is smaller,
builds a smaller envelope, and is more inclined to put it somewhere awkward —
inside a void, low in a hedge, tight under a deck rail.

Getting it right matters less than usual here, because the handling is broadly
the same. It matters for expectations: an aerial yellowjacket nest is more often
in a position that forces the issue, and its occupants are more often the ones
somebody blunders into while trimming.

Two other things get called bald-faced hornets and are neither. The elm sawfly
is a large dark wood wasp, harmless, with no colony at all. And a
[western yellowjacket](/pest-library/western-yellowjacket/) coming out of a hole
in a lawn is sometimes reported as a hornet purely on the strength of how much
it hurt.

## Where the nest sits is what decides the job

Species is not the variable that matters. Position is, and it sorts these nests
into four practical categories.

**High in an open tree.** The easiest case to decide and the one people worry
about most. Well up in a canopy, away from the house, away from paths, it is a
predator colony that will be gone by winter. Height also makes treatment
awkward, and the honest math frequently comes out on the side of doing nothing.

**Low in a shrub or a hedge.** The genuinely hazardous version, because the nest
is at or near head height in something that gets cut, and hedges get cut in
August. A nest a person can walk into is a different proposition from the same
nest thirty feet up, and this is the position we move fastest on.

**On the building.** Under a soffit, against a gable end, above a porch, in a
carport, tucked into a deep eave out of the rain. Access is usually better than
in a tree, which makes the work easier, but the defended radius now overlaps
with a door, a window or a walkway that gets used daily.

**Inside a structure people enter.** An open shed, a barn, a garage left ajar
through the summer, a covered moorage. This one deserves its own line because an
enclosed space takes away the ability to back off, and the sensible interim
measure is simply not to use that building.

Everything else — the color of the wasp, the size of the envelope, whether
anybody has been stung yet — is secondary to which of those four you have.

## When we would leave it, and when we would not

The case for leaving it: a nest away from paths, doors, seating, play areas and
anything anybody has to maintain; nobody in the household reacting badly to
stings; a colony that has already spent the summer removing insects from the
property without a single incident; and a date past the middle of September,
when the whole thing has weeks left regardless.

The case for treating it: a nest within a few paces of a door, a gate, a
driveway, steps, a play area, a vegetable bed, a woodpile or an outdoor table; a
nest at head height in anything that gets trimmed; a nest attached to a building
near a window that has to open; a household where somebody carries an
epinephrine auto-injector for stings, which removes the judgment call entirely;
and a discovery in June or July, because acting on a small nest early is a
smaller piece of work in every respect than acting on the same colony in
September.

That is why this profile carries a verdict of *depends* rather than *treat*. The
insect does not decide it. Where it built decides it.

## What the work involves

Where a nest does need dealing with, the shape of it is set by the fact that the
target is enclosed and elevated.

Proper protective equipment, which for an aerial nest means a suit and veil
rated for the job rather than heavy clothing and optimism, because the response
comes from above and from all sides of a structure rather than from a single
hole in the ground.

Work at dusk or before first light, when the great majority of the colony is
inside the envelope rather than distributed across the neighborhood. Treating in
the middle of a warm afternoon leaves a large population of foragers arriving
back to a nest that is no longer functioning, which is worse for everyone in the
vicinity.

Delivery through the entrance and into the interior of the nest, with equipment
that reaches the height involved. Standing directly beneath an entrance at close
range with a hardware store can is the version that produces the stories.

Then a pause. Foragers keep returning for a day or so, and the structure stays
where it is until that traffic has stopped rather than being pulled out of a
tree on the same visit.

Taking the envelope down afterwards is optional and usually a separate,
uneventful piece of work. On a building it is worth doing, because a nest left
attached to siding sheds material through the winter and leaves a mark. In a
tree it is entirely cosmetic.

Height and access set the difficulty. A nest at eight feet in an open shrub and
a nest twenty-five feet into a canopy over a boundary fence are different jobs
with the same insect in them.

## Around the county

**Sudden Valley, Geneva and the Lake Whatcom margins.** Mature trees, dense
understory and a lot of properties where the boundary between garden and
woodland is a suggestion. Most of the high-in-a-tree nests we are asked about
are here, and most of them get left alone.

**Older Bellingham streets.** Deep eaves, open soffits, porch roofs and mature
street trees, on lots small enough that a nest anywhere is a nest near
something. The building-attached version turns up here more than anywhere else.

**Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas.** Barns, equipment sheds, open-fronted
outbuildings and shelter belts. The nest inside a working structure is the local
pattern, and the people who find it are usually already inside the building
when they do.

**Blaine, Birch Bay and the seasonal properties.** A colony builds from April
with the place empty, and somebody opens up in September to a full-sized nest
under the eave over the deck.

**Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain and the newer hillside stock.** Young
landscaping offers fewer good sites, so what we see is soffits, deck framing and
the ornamental shrubs planted close to the front elevation.
