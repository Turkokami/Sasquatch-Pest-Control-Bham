## The fly nobody expects

It is January. There is a slow, dark, slightly hairy fly bumping against the
inside of an upstairs window, and there are eleven more on the sill. Outside it
is 40 degrees and raining. Nothing has been left out, nothing has died, and
nobody can work out where they came from.

This is a cluster fly, genus *Pollenia*, and it is one of the few insects on
this website whose entire story happens somewhere other than where you find it.

The short version: the fly in front of you grew up inside an earthworm in the
lawn last summer, came into the building in October through a gap you have never
noticed, and has spent the winter dormant in a wall void or an attic. A warm
spell woke it, it went toward the light, and the light was your window.

It is not breeding in your house. It is not feeding on anything in your house.
It cannot damage your house. And — this is the part worth saying loudest — it
says nothing whatsoever about how clean your house is.

## The earthworm, which is the whole explanation

Almost everything counterintuitive about this insect follows from one fact about
its larvae.

Adult cluster flies emerge in spring, feed on nectar and plant material, and lay
eggs on soil that contains earthworms. The larvae that hatch are parasitoids:
they find an earthworm, enter it, and develop inside it. When development is
complete they pupate, and the next generation of adults emerges from the ground.
Penn State records three to four generations, sometimes more, across a season;
Utah State puts it at up to four. So a population builds through the summer and
peaks going into autumn.

Every stage of that happens outdoors, in soil, with no reference to buildings at
all.

Two consequences follow and they explain the entire shape of this problem.

**This is a lawn and pasture problem, not a housekeeping problem.** Earthworm
populations are highest in permanent grass — lawns, pasture, field margins,
orchard floors, the grassy verges of a rural lane. A property surrounded by
those things sits in the middle of a cluster fly nursery, and a property in the
middle of a paved city block does not. That is the actual variable, and it has
nothing to do with the household.

**And sanitation is irrelevant.** Adults take nectar. Utah State's note is that
unlike other flies they avoid garbage and animal waste. There is no bin, no
drain, no forgotten produce and no dead animal in this story anywhere. The whole
diagnostic routine that works on every other fly — find the moist organic
breeding site — has no target here, which is why people who follow that advice
end up cleaning a kitchen that was never the problem.

## A blow fly that is not a filth fly

Worth a short section, because it is the one thing about the classification that
genuinely confuses people who look it up.

Cluster flies sit in the family Calliphoridae — the blow flies. That is the same
family as the metallic blue and green flies that develop in carrion and that,
found indoors in numbers, mean something has died in a wall or under a floor.

Same family, completely different life. The metallic ones are a genuine finding
worth acting on. The cluster fly, in the same family, is a soil parasitoid of
earthworms and a nectar feeder that has never been near a carcass.

That matters practically for a couple of reasons. It is why a search engine will
sometimes hand you the wrong answer and set off an unnecessary hunt for a dead
rodent. And it is why the correct first move on any fly indoors is to look at
the animal rather than to reason from family names: shiny, fast and metallic
means one thing; dull, slow and gray-gold means another entirely.

## Recognizing one

The identification is easy once you know what to look at, and it hinges on three
things.

**Size.** Penn State describes them as slightly larger than the common house
fly. Noticeably bigger without being dramatic.

**Color and the golden cast.** Dull gray with black markings on the abdomen, and
— the diagnostic feature — golden-yellow hairs on the thorax that can give the
whole front of the fly a faint golden sheen. The hair is especially dense on the
underside between the legs. In good light, on a windowsill, the gold is
visible without a lens.

**Behavior.** Slow. Genuinely slow: they bump against glass rather than flying
with purpose, they are easy to catch by hand, and they crawl as much as they
fly. That sluggishness is the single most reported characteristic and it is a
consequence of a dormant insect that has only partly woken up.

**Numbers and place.** Upper floors, at windows, in quantity, in the wrong
season. A dozen flies in a February bedroom is a pattern almost nothing else
produces.

Set against the alternatives: house flies are smaller, grayer, faster and
associated with waste and warm weather. Blow flies proper are shiny metallic
blue or green. Fungus gnats are tiny, dark and delicate, drifting around
houseplants. Drain flies are small, fuzzy and sit motionless on bathroom walls.
The [fly control page](/services/fly-control/) works through the whole set,
because on flies the species really does decide where to look.

## The name, and why we list a genus

Two small points about naming, both of which come up.

"Cluster" is literal. The insect is named for what it does in the autumn —
gathering in aggregations, on walls outside and then in voids inside. It is a
description of behavior rather than of the animal, which is unusual and which is
why the name is so useful: if the flies are not in a group, they are probably
not these.

And this profile is filed under *Pollenia* rather than under a single species.
Penn State's fact sheet works from *Pollenia rudis*, which is the classic and
the one most literature names, but several closely related flies in the genus do
the same thing and are not separable by anybody standing on a landing with a
fly on the windowsill. Nothing a household would do changes between them. They
share the earthworm biology, the autumn move, the dormancy and the answer, so
the page covers the group and says so rather than implying a determination
nobody made.

## Why your building, and why the upstairs windows

The autumn behavior is what turns a soil insect into a household one.

As the season turns, the adults produced by the last generation of the summer
look for somewhere to spend the winter. Penn State's list of what they use is
worth reading in full, because it makes clear that buildings are one option
among several natural ones: hedgerows, the space under the bark of dead and
dying trees, the crevices of south-facing cliffs and rock faces, and the cracks,
crevices and voids of sheds, garages, barns and houses.

A building, in other words, is being read as a warm south-facing rock face. The
flies gather on sunlit walls on mild autumn afternoons, work into whatever gaps
are available, and settle into the void behind. Utah State notes the preference
for the upper levels of a structure — attics, wall voids and false ceilings —
which is why the whole problem plays out on the top floor.

Then, when a warm spell in December or February raises the temperature in that
void, some of them rouse. Penn State's description of what happens next is
precise and slightly sad: they crawl out of wall voids and attics in a confused
attempt to go back outside. They orient toward light. In an upper wall cavity
the nearest light is an upstairs window, and they emerge at the frame into the
room rather than out through the siding.

That is the entire mechanism, and it explains the two things people find
maddening. The flies keep coming back because the same overwintering population
is being woken repeatedly rather than a new one arriving. And they concentrate
at two or three specific windows because those are the ones nearest the void
they are in.

## The same story as the stink bugs

Cluster flies almost never arrive alone, and the reason is that they are one
member of a group of insects doing exactly the same thing on exactly the same
calendar.

The brown marmorated stink bug, the boxelder bug, the western conifer seed bug,
some ladybird beetles, and mated paper wasp and yellowjacket queens all gather
on warm sunlit walls in October, use the same soffit gaps and window surrounds,
settle into the same voids, and emerge on the same warm days. Different orders,
different diets, identical behavior.

This is genuinely good news, because it means the work is shared. The gaps that
admit a cluster fly admit a stink bug. The late-summer sealing that keeps out
one keeps out all of them. And the timing is the same for the whole group.

We are not going to restate that case here when it is already made properly
elsewhere. The

## What the flies are actually telling you

Since the insect itself is telling you nothing about your housekeeping, it is
worth asking what it *is* telling you, because there is real information in it.

**Where your envelope leaks at height.** Cluster flies enter high — soffit
junctions, fascia gaps, roof-to-wall details on additions, gable and roof vents
with damaged screening, the open ends of vinyl siding, utility penetrations,
chimney flashing, and the surrounds of upstairs window frames. A house that
takes hundreds of them is a house with a lot of small openings up where nobody
looks.

**Which void connects to which room.** The windows that produce flies are the
windows nearest the occupied cavity, and the route they take out of it — a
window frame edge, a recessed light housing, a bathroom fan surround, an attic
hatch — is a route air takes as well. There is a heating bill inside this
information.

**Which elevations get the sun.** They gather on south and west walls, and
strongest on light-colored walls that hold heat. That tells you where the
sealing effort belongs, which is not usually where somebody would start.

For one autumn, in other words, several hundred small insects map the top half
of your building for you. Note the windows they come out of before you do
anything else.

## The dead flies, which is the part people skip

A share of the overwintering population dies in the void, and in a building that
has had this for years the accumulation can be considerable.

Dead insects are food. Carpet beetles and larder beetles breed in an
accumulation of fly carcasses in an attic or a soffit, and their larvae disperse
into the rooms below, where they damage wool and stored textiles. Penn State
makes the point specifically as an argument against indoor insecticide use:
killing the flies where they sit produces exactly the carcass supply that draws
carpet beetles in.

So a vacuum is the right tool for the ones in the room, and clearing an
accessible attic is part of doing the job properly rather than an upsell. Where
the accumulation is inside a closed wall cavity it may not be reachable at all,
which is one more argument for keeping them out rather than dealing with them
once they are in.

## Where this happens in Whatcom County

The pattern here follows land use more than anything else.

**Rural and acreage property** — Lynden, Everson, Nooksack, Sumas, the lanes off
the Guide, and the ground toward the border. Permanent pasture and grass carry
high earthworm populations, which means the fly population generated around a
rural house is far larger than around a house in town. Well-managed soil with
plenty of earthworms is exactly what a farm wants, and this comes attached to
it. Farm outbuildings compound it, because barns and equipment sheds are
additional overwintering sites sitting fifty yards from the house.

**Older city housing** — the Lettered Streets, Columbia, Sunnyland, York. Not
much lawn per lot, but a great many small openings at height: original window
casings, open soffit runs, aged flashing and complex rooflines. Moderate outdoor
pressure, high indoor penetration.

**Newer construction on open ground** — Barkley, Cordata, King Mountain and the
newer stock around Ferndale. Large unshaded south and west elevations facing
open lawn are close to ideal gathering surfaces, and the numbers on the siding in
October can be startling. The compensation is that the detailing is better and
the sealing work is straightforward.

**Waterfront and seasonal property** — Blaine, Birch Bay, Semiahmoo. Houses shut
up through the wet months fill quietly, and the owner arrives in April to
windowsills full of them and no idea what happened.

**Lakeside and greenbelt lots** — Sudden Valley, Silver Beach, Edgemoor. Mixed,
and generally lighter, because there is less permanent grass and more shade on
the walls.

## The year, as it runs in this county

Written out because the whole recommendation is a calendar, and because knowing
which month you are in tells you what is worth doing.

**March and April.** Survivors of the overwintering population leave the
building and go back to the soil to lay. You may see a last flurry at windows as
they exit. Nothing needs doing, and this is the point at which an attic
accumulation is worth clearing while it is dry and empty.

**May through July.** The population is entirely in the ground, invisible,
running through generations inside earthworms. Nobody thinks about cluster flies
in June, which is a shame, because this is the quiet stretch in which the
sealing work could be scheduled without any pressure.

**Late August into early September.** The decision window. The last generation
of adults is out, the numbers are at their annual peak, and they have not yet
moved to the walls. This is when an exterior application to the gathering
elevations does its work and when sealing has the most value per hour. It is
also, unhelpfully, when nobody has a fly problem and nobody calls.

**October.** The gathering. Mild afternoons put them on the south and west
walls in numbers, and they work into the gaps over a few weeks. By the end of
the month the population that will spend the winter in your building is already
in it, and the treatment window has closed behind them.

**November through February.** Dormancy, punctuated. Every warm spell or heating
run rouses a batch, which emerges at the nearest upper window. This is when the
calls come, and it is the least productive month of the year to do anything but
vacuum.

The awkward shape of that calendar is the honest core of this page: the problem
is visible in January and solvable in August, and those are six months apart.

## Our verdict, and what it turns on

This one genuinely depends, and the variable is the calendar more than the
property.

**An exterior treatment is worth doing** when it goes on the gathering
elevations in late August into early September, ahead of the move indoors. Penn
State's timing note is the same: exterior applications belong in the autumn just
before congregation. Done then, on the right walls, it reduces what gets in,
sometimes substantially.

**The same treatment is close to pointless in November**, because the flies are
already inside the wall and nothing applied to the siding reaches them.

**Sealing is worth doing whenever you start**, and it is the half of the work
that lasts. It also does something for rodents, wasps and every other
overwintering insect at the same time, which is why we would rather sell an
afternoon of exclusion than a schedule of visits.

**And an interior treatment is not worth doing at all**, for the carpet beetle
reason above and because product applied in living space does not reach an
insect dormant in a cavity.

So: worth treating, on the right wall, in the right three weeks. Not worth
treating outside that window, and never worth treating indoors. That is a
narrower recommendation than most companies will give you and it is the one the
extension guidance supports.

## What we will not sell you

**A monthly plan through the winter.** The population inside the building is
fixed once they are in. Repeat visits do not reduce it and the calendar proves
it.

**A treatment for the lawn.** The larvae are inside earthworms in soil across
your property and your neighbors', earthworms are the last thing anybody should
be treating a lawn against, and it would not work.

**A promise that they will stop.** The source is the ground around the building
and neither of us can change that. What good work achieves is a large reduction
in what gets in and what emerges into rooms, and on a complex older building the
honest goal is management rather than an empty attic.

**An urgent attic cleanout** where the accumulation is light. Where it is heavy
it genuinely wants dealing with, and we will show you which of the two you have.

## What to tell us if you call

**Which windows.** Two or three specific ones almost always, and that narrows
the void they are in more than anything else you could say.

**Which walls they gather on outside in October**, if you have noticed. That is
where an exterior application would go.

**Whether the flies are dull and slow or shiny and fast.** It is the difference
between this page and a search for something dead in a wall.

**What else appears on the same walls.** Stink bugs, boxelder bugs, seed bugs or
ladybird beetles alongside the flies means one sealing project rather than four
problems.

**And when you are calling.** In August we will talk about treatment and
sealing. In January we will tell you honestly that the useful treatment window
has gone, recommend a vacuum, and put the sealing in the diary for the end of
summer.
