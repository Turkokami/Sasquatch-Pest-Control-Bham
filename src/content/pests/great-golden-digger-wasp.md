## What it looks like, and why it worries people

Big. That is the first thing. A great golden digger wasp is large enough to
carry a katydid, which means she is large enough to make anybody step back
when she lands on a driveway.

The colouring is distinctive once you have seen it: rust-orange legs, an
orange abdomen with a dark rear, smoky wings, and a thorax covered in short
golden hair that gives the species its name. Nothing else here that size
looks like it.

## She is solitary, and that is the whole safety answer

Every genuinely dangerous stinging insect on this site is social. A
yellowjacket or a bald-faced hornet has a colony, workers whose job includes
defense, and a nest worth dying for. That is what makes an accidental approach
expensive.

A digger wasp has none of that. One female, one burrow, no workers. She gains
nothing by stinging a person and loses her afternoon's work. Field observers
describe watching them from a few feet away without any reaction — solitary
wasps, as the University of Wisconsin's field station puts it, are frequently
pretty laid-back.

## The burrow and the soil

She digs a vertical tunnel with cells at the bottom, and where she digs it is
particular. The nesting research on this species is about site selection:
well-drained soil of moderate compactness, avoiding substrate loose enough to
collapse and ground packed too hard to work. In practice that means sandy or
sandy-loam ground, warm, with sparse vegetation.

That soil requirement is why this is mostly an eastern Washington insect and
why finding one here is worth recording rather than assuming a
misidentification. Most of western Whatcom County is too wet for her. Pockets
of it are not — a dry gravelly bank, a sun-baked driveway edge, the sandy
shoulder of a track — and that is where ours was photographed, working a
burrow in exactly that substrate with a paralyzed katydid.

## Why we will not treat it

Because there is nothing to treat. She is a solitary predator of katydids and
crickets, she visits flowers as an adult, she will not defend a nest, and the
season is short. A pesticide application here would be sold on the strength of
her size and nothing else.

If she is nesting somewhere genuinely awkward — the middle of a path a child
uses barefoot, say — say so and we will talk about it. Most of the time the
right advice is the cheapest one, which is to watch her work.
