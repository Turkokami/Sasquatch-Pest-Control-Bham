## What it looks like, and why it worries people

Big. That is the first thing. A great golden digger wasp is large enough to
carry a katydid, which means she is large enough to make anybody step back
when she lands on a driveway.

The coloring is distinctive once you have seen it: rust-orange legs, an
orange abdomen with a dark rear, smoky wings, and a thorax covered in short
golden hair that gives the species its name. Nothing else here that size
looks like it.

## She is solitary, and that is the whole safety answer

Every genuinely dangerous stinging insect on this site is social. A
yellowjacket or a bald-faced hornet has a colony, workers whose job includes
defense, and a nest worth dying for. That is what makes an accidental approach
expensive.

A digger wasp has none of that. One female, one burrow, no workers. She gains
nothing by stinging a person and loses her afternoon's work. Field observers
describe watching them from a few feet away without any reaction — solitary
wasps, as the University of Wisconsin's field station puts it, are frequently
pretty laid-back.

## The burrow and the soil

She digs a vertical tunnel with cells at the bottom, and where she digs it is
particular. The nesting research on this species is about site selection:
well-drained soil of moderate compactness, avoiding substrate loose enough to
collapse and ground packed too hard to work. In practice that means sandy or
sandy-loam ground, warm, with sparse vegetation.

That soil requirement is why this is mostly an eastern Washington insect and
why finding one here is worth recording rather than assuming a
misidentification. Most of western Whatcom County is too wet for her. Pockets
of it are not — a dry gravelly bank, a sun-baked driveway edge, the sandy
shoulder of a track — and that is where ours was photographed, working a
burrow in exactly that substrate with a paralyzed katydid.

## Why we will not treat it

Because there is nothing to treat. She is a solitary predator of katydids and
crickets, she visits flowers as an adult, she will not defend a nest, and the
season is short. A pesticide application here would be sold on the strength of
her size and nothing else.

If she is nesting somewhere genuinely awkward — the middle of a path a child
uses barefoot, say — say so and we will talk about it. Most of the time the
right advice is the cheapest one, which is to watch her work.

## The katydid, and what paralyzed means

The footage above shows the part people find hardest to believe. She is
dragging a katydid several times her own bulk across open gravel, and the
katydid is not dead.

She stings it in the nerve centers that control the legs. The venom does not
kill; it disables. The insect stays alive, fresh and entirely immobile, and it
will remain that way for weeks. That matters because a dead katydid would rot
long before a wasp larva finished eating it, and a live one would climb out of
the burrow.

She hauls it to the tunnel, takes it down, lays an egg on it, and closes the
cell. The larva hatches, eats the provision, spins a cocoon and overwinters
underground. One female does all of that alone, several times over, in a season
that lasts a few weeks.

Whatever else anybody thinks about wasps, that is a remarkable piece of work to
watch happening on a driveway.

## Watching one work

If you find her, the sequence is worth two minutes of anybody's time, and it is
entirely safe to stand and watch.

She lands short of the burrow with the prey, sets it down, and goes into the
tunnel alone — apparently checking it. She comes back out, takes the katydid by
the antennae, and drags it in headfirst. Between trips she does maintenance:
loosening soil, backing out with a load, scattering it clear of the entrance.

She is entirely uninterested in the person standing over her. That is not
bravado on the observer's part; it is what solitary wasps are like, and it is
the single most convincing demonstration of the point the rest of this page is
making.

## The burrow, and what it looks like from above

A neat round hole, roughly pencil to finger width, in bare or thinly vegetated
ground, often with a small fan of excavated soil beside it. The tunnel runs down
several inches and branches into cells at the bottom.

There may be several burrows in the same patch of good ground. That is worth
understanding properly, because it is where people go wrong: several solitary
females nesting near each other because the soil suits them is **not** a colony.
There is no shared nest, no queen, no workers, and no collective defense. It is
a good neighborhood, not a hive.

## The one confusion that actually matters

There is a ground-nesting wasp in this county that is genuinely dangerous, and
telling it apart from this one is the most useful thing on this page.

**Western yellowjackets** nest in the ground in large colonies. The entrance
looks superficially similar — a hole with traffic in and out. Everything else is
different. A yellowjacket is banded yellow and black, thick-bodied, an inch or
less, and there will be a steady stream of individuals coming and going rather
than one animal working alone. Disturb that nest with a mower or a spade and you
will be stung repeatedly by insects whose job is to sting you.

**A great golden digger wasp** is bigger, unmistakably orange-legged, and there
is one of her. She carries katydids in; yellowjackets do not carry anything
that size.

If you cannot tell which you have, do not test it by disturbing the hole. Send
us a photograph from a safe distance, or call. The distinction is quick for us
and there is no charge for making it.

**Cicada killers** are the other large ground-nester people ask about — larger
still, black with yellow markings, and they provision with cicadas. Same
solitary temperament, same lack of interest in people. This region has far fewer
of them than the eastern states do.

## Where she nests, and why that is usually the whole problem

The soil requirement is strict enough that her burrow is nearly always in one of
a few places: a gravel drive edge, a sandy bank, a path, a dry sunny border with
thin cover, the bare strip beside a shed or a slab.

That is why she gets noticed. She has chosen exactly the ground people walk on,
and the same qualities that make it good tunnelling — dry, warm, compacted but
workable, sparsely vegetated — are what make it a path in the first place.

Which also points at the only real preventive measure. If a specific patch of
bare ground is being used year after year and it genuinely cannot be shared,
planting it, mulching it heavily or otherwise removing the bare warm surface
will make it unattractive. She wants exposed soil. Cover it and she goes
elsewhere.

That is a landscaping change, not a treatment, and it is the only intervention
on this page we would encourage.

## Adults eat nectar

Worth saying, because it changes how people think about her.

The prey is for the larvae. The adult female feeds on nectar, and she visits
flowers to do it — which makes her a pollinator in the plain sense of the word,
alongside being a predator of the insects that eat garden plants.

So the animal being reported as a menace is, in the same season, pollinating and
controlling katydid numbers. That is not a sentimental argument. It is the
reason "we do not treat these" is a technically correct answer rather than a
soft one.

## The season here

Short. Adults are on the wing in the warm part of summer, roughly July into
early September in this county, with the peak of provisioning in the hottest,
driest weeks.

By autumn the adults are gone. The next generation is underground in cocoons and
emerges the following summer. Nobody needs to do anything about the winter, and
a burrow found in October is already finished.

That brevity is part of the answer to "should I do something". A treatment
applied in August is applied to an insect that has weeks left regardless.

## What not to do

**Do not pour anything down the burrow.** Gasoline, diesel, bleach and boiling
water all circulate as folk remedies for ground-nesting insects. Fuel poured into
soil is a genuine contamination problem, it is unlawful to use a product in a
way inconsistent with its labeling, and none of it is necessary here.

**Do not treat it because it is big.** Size is not a hazard signal in wasps and
the largest ones in this region are among the least defensive.

**Do not let somebody sell you a perimeter treatment for a ground nester.** A
residual sprayed on a foundation does not affect an insect that tunnels in a
driveway edge and hunts in vegetation.

**Do not disturb a hole you cannot identify.** That is the yellowjacket
distinction above, and it is the one mistake here that can genuinely hurt.

## When we would actually do something

There is a version of this that warrants intervention and we would rather name
it than pretend there is none.

If burrows are in the middle of a path a child uses barefoot, in a sandpit, or
in the one patch of ground somebody with a serious sting allergy has to cross
daily, that is a real conflict rather than a squeamish one. Even then the first
answer is usually physical — cover the ground, reroute the path, mulch the
strip — because it lasts and a treatment does not.

Tell us the actual situation and we will tell you honestly whether it is one of
those. Most are not.

## What a visit for this looks like

We identify the insect first, and on a ground nest that is the whole job in most
cases: confirm it is a solitary digger and not a yellowjacket colony, which is a
two-minute observation and the difference between "leave it" and "this needs
handling today".

Then we look at where the burrows are relative to how the property is used, and
whether the bare ground can reasonably be covered.

Then, usually, we tell you there is nothing to buy. If you called us out for
this and that is the answer, we are not going to invent a reason to charge for a
visit.

## What we will not sell you

We will not treat a solitary ground-nesting wasp because a customer found it
alarming. If we did that we would be charging for the size of an insect.

We will not describe her as aggressive, or as a threat, or as an infestation.
One female with a burrow is none of those things and the words matter.

And we will not use the alarm around this one to sell a program aimed at
something else. If what you actually have in the ground is a yellowjacket
colony, that is a different page, a different answer and a job we will take
seriously.

## The other solitary ground-nesters here

She is not the only insect that puts holes in bare ground, and the others get
reported to us under the same description. Sorting them is quick.

**Mining bees** are the most common call by a wide margin. In spring, dozens or
hundreds of small holes appear in a lawn or a sparse bank, each with a little
cone of soil, and each with a bee coming and going. They are solitary bees, each
female with her own hole, gathering pollen rather than hunting. They are gentle
to the point of being difficult to provoke, the whole business lasts a few weeks,
and the ground closes over afterwards. Nothing needs doing, and treating a lawn
for them destroys a significant local pollinator population for a temporary
cosmetic complaint.

**Thread-waisted wasps of the genus Ammophila** are slimmer relatives of the
digger wasp with a long stalked waist and a red band, and they provision with
caterpillars rather than katydids. Same solitary habit, same indifference to
people.

**Sand wasps** are stouter, often with pale banding and green eyes, and they nest
in loose sandy ground, sometimes in loose groupings. They hunt flies.

**Bee flies** are not wasps at all — furry, hovering, no sting — and turn up
around mining bee holes because their larvae develop at the bees' expense.

All of them are solitary, none of them defends a nest, and the answer for the
whole group is the same as the answer on this page.

## Photographing one for identification

Since the entire practical question is "is this the harmless one or the
dangerous one", and since nobody should be approaching a hole to find out, a
photograph is usually the right first move.

Get the whole insect in frame rather than a close-up of part of it. Color of
the legs and the shape of the waist do most of the identification work.

If you can safely include the nest entrance in a shot, do — a neat single hole
in bare soil says something different from a hole in a bank with a stream of
insects going in and out.

Note whether it is carrying anything. Prey is diagnostic: a katydid means this
species, a caterpillar means Ammophila, a fly means a sand wasp, pollen on the
legs means a bee.

And note whether you are watching one animal or many. That single observation
separates every solitary species on this page from every colony that matters.

Send it and we will tell you what you have, without a visit and without a
charge. We would far rather answer that in a message than have somebody find out
by putting a spade into a yellowjacket nest.

## What to tell whoever mows

The single most useful thing anybody can do about ground-nesting insects is
tell the person operating the machinery where the holes are.

Almost every serious stinging incident we hear about in this county started with
a mower, a strimmer or a tractor going over a nest nobody had marked. That is
true of yellowjackets, which is the dangerous case, and it is worth extending to
this one for a different reason: a digger wasp burrow is destroyed by a pass of a
mower, and there is no need to destroy it.

Mark the spot with a cane, a flag or an upturned pot, tell whoever cuts the
grass, and mow around it. On a property where the work is contracted out,
say it at the start of the season rather than after somebody has found the
nest the hard way. It costs nothing. For the harmless species it saves a
nest that will be finished within weeks, and for the dangerous one it prevents
the accident.
