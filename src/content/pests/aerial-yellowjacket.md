## The species in the middle

Whatcom County has two famous wasps and one that does most of its work without a
name anybody uses.

The famous ones are easy to describe. There is the black and white insect with
the gray football in the tree, and there is the yellow one that comes out of a
hole in a lawn when the mower goes over it. Between them sits *Dolichovespula
arenaria*, which has the nest of the first and the coloring of the second, and
which consequently gets called whichever of the two the person looking at it
noticed first.

That is not a trivia problem. It is a practical one, because the two
misidentifications send people down two different wrong paths, and both of them
waste time in a season where time matters.

This page is about what it actually is, what each mistake costs, and the thing
that genuinely decides whether a nest is a problem — which is not the species at
all.

## What it is

A yellowjacket, in the strict sense — same subfamily, same enclosed paper
architecture, same annual colony. WSU Hortsense lists it among the state's
common social wasps as one of the species with relatively stout bodies and
distinct yellow and black banded coloring.

The Northeastern IPM Center puts workers at about half an inch, with a blunt
abdomen and yellow and black banding, and notes that queens are larger and
rarely seen except in spring and again in autumn. In the hand, or on a decent
photograph, it looks very much like the ground-nesting species that dominates
this county's sting statistics.

Its genus, though, is the one it shares with the
[bald-faced hornet](/pest-library/bald-faced-hornet/) rather than the one it
shares with the ground nesters. That single fact predicts almost everything else
about it: where it builds, what it eats in September, and how it behaves when
something knocks its nest.

## The nest, and why it will not settle the question

An envelope of gray paper wrapped around several tiers of comb, with the way in
at the bottom. UC IPM's description of the aerial nesters covers both species at
once — paper nests attached to the eaves of a building or hanging from the limb
of a tree, with the entrance normally a hole at the bottom of the nest — and
WSU's bulletin says the same thing of both, in trees or on the outside eaves of
buildings.

Which means the sentence people most often use when they call us, "there's a big
gray paper nest," identifies a genus and stops there.

Some working differences do exist, and they are tendencies rather than rules. An
aerial yellowjacket nest is usually the smaller of the two, and it is more often
found low — in the body of a shrub, tight under a rail, inside the shelter of a
porch — where its relative more often goes high into a tree. And the envelope is
frequently less tidy, with a more irregular, layered, papery surface.

But if you want to know which one you have, look at an insect rather than at the
structure. Yellow and black bands is this page's species. Black with ivory-white
markings on the face and body is the other one. There is no overlap and nothing
subtle about it.

## The first mistake: calling it a hornet

Somebody sees the paper nest, calls it a hornet nest, and adjusts their behavior
to match a reputation.

The costs here are mild but real. It produces a level of alarm out of proportion
to the situation, which occasionally leads to a bad decision made in a hurry —
knocking a nest out of a shrub, spraying it from directly underneath, going up a
ladder in a hurry to deal with something that had been quietly ignoring the
household all summer.

It also skews expectations about size. Penn State describes bald-faced hornet
colonies reaching one hundred to four hundred workers over a summer, and an
aerial yellowjacket nest is typically a smaller structure than that. Neither is
in the same league as the several thousand workers WSU records in a mature
ground-nesting western yellowjacket colony. The visible nest is rarely the
biggest colony on a property.

The one thing the hornet framing gets right is caution at close range, and that
part is deserved.

## The second mistake: hunting for a hole in the lawn

This one costs more, and we hear it several times a year.

Somebody identifies a yellow and black wasp correctly as a yellowjacket, goes
looking for what every article about yellowjackets in the Pacific Northwest
describes — an entrance in the ground, in a bank, in an old rodent burrow — and
finds nothing, because there is nothing to find. The wasps are coming out of a
paper nest in a laurel eight feet away, or from under the eave above the person's
head.

Two things follow from that dead end. Time gets lost during the weeks when the
colony is growing fastest. And the ground-nest playbook gets applied to a
situation it does not fit: people pour things into holes that are not the nest,
they flood a bank, they mark a spot that has nothing in it, and they conclude
that the treatment failed when nothing was ever treated.

Worse, the search itself puts somebody crouched over the ground, moving slowly,
directly beneath a nest they have not noticed.

The corrective is simple. Before looking down, look up, along the eave line,
into the dense middle of every shrub within thirty feet, under the deck rail and
the porch soffit, and inside any open outbuilding. Watch the traffic instead of
the ground — a flight line that converges on a point above waist height belongs
to this species or its black and white relative.

## Where it puts the nest

The Northeastern IPM Center's observation is the important one for this county:
this species will build in voids as well as trees, and in closer proximity to
human activity than its relatives. That is the difference that matters, and it
is why an aerial yellowjacket nest is more likely than a bald-faced hornet nest
to be somewhere that forces the issue.

The recurring positions on property here:

**Inside a dense shrub or hedge.** Laurel, boxwood, escallonia, an overgrown
rhododendron, a conifer hedge that has not been cut in two years. The nest sits
in the middle of the mass where nothing shows from outside, and it is found by a
hedge trimmer.

**Under a soffit or a deep eave**, tucked back against the wall where the
overhang keeps the rain off. Frequently above a window.

**Beneath a deck, a porch roof or a stair landing**, attached to joists or the
underside of decking.

**In an open shed, a barn, a carport or a covered moorage**, hanging from a
rafter or a truss, in a space people walk into.

**Against a gable end or in the angle of a roof-to-wall junction.**

**Inside a void.** A soffit run, a boxed-out corner, a gap behind loose siding,
an unscreened gable vent. This is where the species stops behaving like an
aerial nester and becomes a wall-void job, with all the complications that
carries — an invisible colony, an unknown cavity and a strict rule against
sealing the entrance while anything is alive.

**Low, in long grass at the base of a shrub or against a bank**, which happens
often enough that a nest at ankle height is not a reason to rule the species
out.

That range — canopy to ankles, open air to closed cavity — is the reason no
single answer applies to it.

## How it behaves, near the nest and away from it

Near the nest, this is not a species to take liberties with. UC IPM's language
for the aerial nesters is that they are extremely defensive when their nests are
disturbed, and it adds a detail specific to this one that is worth carrying
around: defending individuals of *D. arenaria* sometimes bite and sting
simultaneously.

That is not a folk exaggeration. It is a documented behavior, it is unpleasant,
and it explains why people who have been through this describe the experience as
worse than a yellowjacket sting rather than equivalent to one.

The disturbances that trigger it are mostly mechanical rather than personal.
Cutting or shaking the plant the nest is in. A ladder set against the same wall.
A pressure washer on the elevation. Hammering, drilling or nail-gunning on
framing the nest is attached to. Slamming a shed door under a rafter nest. In
almost every case the person had no idea the nest was there.

Away from the nest, the picture is entirely different, and it produces the one
piece of good news on this page.

## It is not the wasp at your table

UC IPM draws a line between the two groups of yellowjackets that almost nobody
outside the field knows about: the aerial nesters do not become scavengers at
the end of the season.

The ground-nesting species change diet as the colony matures and the brood stops
supplying the adults with sugar, and that is what puts them on plates, in cans,
around bins and into fallen fruit from mid-August. This species does not make
that change. The Northeastern IPM Center describes adults feeding on nectar
while workers hunt soft-bodied insects such as caterpillars for the larvae, and
that stays the pattern.

The practical translation is worth reading twice, because it saves people money
every year.

**If wasps are bothering an outdoor eating area, the paper nest you can see is
almost certainly not the source.** Treat it and the problem at the table
continues, because the colony causing it is in the ground or in a wall somewhere
you have not looked. A visible nest is a convenient target and frequently the
wrong one.

**And a nest in a shrub at the back of a property is doing predatory work** on
flies, caterpillars and other soft-bodied insects for the whole season, without
any of the scavenging nuisance that makes its ground-nesting cousins intolerable
in September.

That combination — defensive at the nest, useful away from it, and not
implicated in the late-summer food problem at all — is why so many of these
nests are genuinely fine where they are.

## Watching a nest from the ground

Once a paper nest has been found, almost every question worth asking about it
can be answered from thirty feet away with no equipment and no risk. It is worth
doing before anybody decides anything, because the answers change the
recommendation.

**Is it live?** Stand back on a mild afternoon and watch the underside for two
or three minutes. A working colony produces steady two-way movement at the
entrance. An empty envelope produces nothing at all, no matter how long you
wait, and a nest that has been finished for a while usually shows a ragged tear
somewhere in the side where a bird has been at the brood.

**How busy is it?** A few insects a minute is an early or a small colony. A
continuous stream in both directions in August is a mature one, and it changes
how much room you want to give it.

**Where is the flight line?** Workers leave and return along a repeated path
rather than wandering. Watching for a minute shows you which side of the shrub,
which end of the deck or which stretch of path actually sits in the traffic —
and that is frequently a much narrower strip than people assume, which means
carrying on using most of the garden is a reasonable thing to do.

**Is there more than one entrance?** Rare on an open nest, and worth knowing on
a void nest, where two or three active openings suggest a larger or connected
cavity.

**Is there a second nest?** Where there is one, there is often another, and the
second is usually found by looking rather than by being stung.

What not to do while watching: no throwing, no poking with a pole, no hose, no
flashlight held up at the entrance after dark, and nothing that shakes whatever
the nest is attached to. Binoculars and patience get you everything on this
list.

## The year, and what happens to the nest

One season, start to finish, like everything else in this group.

A queen that mated the previous autumn and overwintered somewhere sheltered
starts alone in spring. Her first brood is all workers, and once they emerge
they take over the building and the foraging while she does nothing but lay. The
Northeastern IPM Center describes exactly that sequence, and adds the ending:
late-season brood becomes new queens and males, only the fertilized females
survive the winter, and the rest of the colony does not live to see cold weather.

WSU's bulletin is unambiguous about what happens to the structure. The abandoned
nests decompose and disintegrate over the winter, and they are not used again.

So the paper nest still hanging in a bare hedge in January is an empty object.
Nothing is in it, nothing will be in it, and it will fall apart on its own. It
is not a colony waiting for spring, it is not a reason to treat anything, and
anybody selling a winter nest removal as pest control is selling you gardening.

What can repeat is the *site*, not the nest. A sheltered eave, a dense hedge or
a dry rafter is attractive to a founding queen for the same reasons two years
running, so a property that produced a nest last year is a reasonable place to
walk in late April looking for a new one the size of a golf ball. Dealing with
one at that stage is trivial, safe and the highest-value thing anybody can do
about this species.

## Nest position is what decides the job

This profile carries a verdict of *depends*, and it is the same verdict as its
two relatives for the same reason: the species is not what decides. Position is.
Sort the nest into one of these and the answer follows.

**High and away.** Well up in a tree, in a shrub at the far boundary, under a
barn eave nobody passes. Nothing needs doing. The colony ends with the cold and
does more good than harm before it does.

**Low and reachable.** In a hedge at chest height, in a shrub beside a path, at
the top of a fence line somebody leans on. This is the version that produces
injuries, because the disturbance arrives before the discovery does. It comes
out.

**On the building, near an opening.** Under a soffit above a door, over a window
that needs to open, above the steps. Access is usually straightforward and the
proximity settles it.

**Inside a structure people use.** A shed, a garage, a carport, a boathouse. An
enclosed space removes the option of backing away calmly, and the sensible
interim measure is to stop using the building until it is dealt with.

**In a void.** No longer an aerial nest in any practical sense. The colony
cannot be seen or measured, the cavity may run further than anybody thinks, and
the entrance must be left open until the colony is finished. That case is
covered in full on
[wasp nest in a wall void](/services/wasp-control/wasp-nest-in-a-wall-void/).

**Anywhere at all, if somebody in the household reacts badly to stings.** That
takes it out of the realm of judgment.

And the seasonal overlay applies to every one of those. A marginal nest found in
the third week of September has a few weeks left in it, and waiting is a real
option that costs nothing. The same nest in the third week of June has the whole
growth curve ahead of it, and dealing with it then is a smaller piece of work in
every dimension.

## What the work involves

Enclosed nests are treated as a unit, and the details differ from the
ground-nest method more than people expect.

Protective equipment appropriate to a target at or above head height, because
the response to an aerial nest comes outward in every direction rather than up
out of a single hole.

The right hour, which is early morning or after dusk, when the population is
concentrated inside the envelope rather than spread across the neighborhood.

Delivery through the entrance at the base and into the interior, from a position
that is not directly beneath it, with equipment matched to the height. The
species-specific note here is the shrub case: a nest inside dense foliage cannot
be seen well and cannot be approached quickly, and the foliage itself is the
thing that must not be moved.

A wait afterwards, because returning foragers keep arriving for a day or so and
nothing is pulled down while that is still happening.

Then removal of the envelope where it makes sense — on a building, where left in
place it sheds material and marks the finish, and in a shrub where somebody is
going to have to prune eventually. In a tree it is optional.

Where the nest turned out to be in a cavity, the sequence gains a step: the
opening is closed only after activity has genuinely stopped, and closing it
before then is the mistake that puts wasps in a bedroom.

## Reducing the odds next year

None of this involves treating anything, and all of it works better than
anything that does.

**Prune in winter.** Dense unmanaged hedging and shrubs that have grown into a
solid mass are the exact structure a queen wants in April. Ordinary maintenance
cutting, done in the cold months, removes sites and carries no risk.

**Walk the eaves and the open outbuildings in late spring**, when a nest is the
size of a walnut and contains a single insect.

**Screen the vents and close the gaps** in soffits, gable ends and siding, which
takes the void option away entirely and does the same for rodents and cluster
flies. That is
[exclusion and repairs](/services/exclusion-and-repairs/) work and late autumn
is the time for it.

**Note where last year's nest was.** The site is what recurs.

**Keep the outbuildings visited.** The nests that become dangerous are almost
always in structures nobody entered between May and August.

## Around the county

**Sudden Valley, Geneva and the Lake Whatcom shore.** Mature ornamental
plantings run into native cover, and the shrub nest is the local version — found
by somebody clearing an overgrown boundary in August.

**Older Bellingham streets.** Open soffits and porch roofs on small lots. This
is where the void version turns up, and where a nest under an eave is
automatically near a window.

**Lynden, Everson, Nooksack and Sumas.** Rafters in open barns, implement sheds
and equipment shelters, where the encounter happens inside a building rather
than in a garden.

**Blaine, Birch Bay and Semiahmoo.** Covered moorage, boat storage and seasonal
properties, with the added complication that the person who finds it is often on
a dock or a ladder.

**Barkley, Cordata and newer hillside stock.** Young landscaping offers little,
so nests concentrate on the structures: deck framing, vented soffits and the
ornamental shrubs against the front elevation.
