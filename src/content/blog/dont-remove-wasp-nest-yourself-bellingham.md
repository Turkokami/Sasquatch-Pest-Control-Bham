## Why is DIY wasp nest removal so dangerous?

### Wasps sting repeatedly and attack in numbers

Unlike a honeybee, a single wasp or hornet can sting you over and over. Disturb a nest and you're rarely dealing with one wasp — you're provoking the whole colony at once. **Yellowjackets** and **bald-faced hornets**, both common around Bellingham, are especially quick to defend their nests, and a startled colony can deliver dozens of stings in seconds.

### Allergic reactions can be life-threatening

Stinging insects are among the more common causes of serious insect-related emergency visits in the U.S. every year. Even people who've never reacted before can develop a severe allergic reaction (**anaphylaxis**), which is a medical emergency. If someone who's been stung shows difficulty breathing, swelling of the face or throat, dizziness, or a spreading rash, call 911 immediately.

### Ladders, panic, and falls

Many nests sit up under eaves or in trees, so DIY removal often means standing on a ladder — right where a swarm of angry wasps is least survivable. The natural reaction to being stung is to swat, flinch, and run, and doing that on a ladder is how a wasp problem turns into a fall and a broken bone.

### You can't see the full nest

The papery nest you can see may be a fraction of the problem. Yellowjackets frequently nest inside wall voids or underground, where the visible entrance hides a colony of hundreds. Treating what you can see without addressing the rest leaves the colony intact and angry.

## Why don't store-bought wasp sprays work very well?

Retail sprays can knock down a few wasps at the entrance, but they rarely solve the problem. They often fail to reach the queen deep inside the nest, so the colony rebuilds. Spraying an aerial nest usually just enrages the survivors, and spraying into a wall-void nest can drive wasps inward — into your living space — instead of out. Aerosol range is also limited, which forces you dangerously close to the nest.

## What are the biggest DIY mistakes people make?

- Knocking a nest down with a broom or stick, which releases the entire colony at once
- Using fire or gasoline — an enormous fire risk that rarely kills the colony anyway
- Sealing a wall-void entrance, which traps wasps inside and forces them into the home
- Spraying into a ground hole and stepping back into the flight path
- Working during the day when the colony is most active
- Wearing dark or bright clothing and scented products, which can provoke wasps
- Attempting removal in late summer, when colonies are largest and most aggressive

## What time of year is worst for DIY removal in Bellingham?

Late summer and early fall — roughly **August through September** — are the most dangerous. By then a colony that started as a golf-ball-sized nest in spring can hold hundreds of workers, and as natural food dwindles the wasps become more aggressive and defensive. A nest that seemed manageable in June is a very different situation by late August. That's also why early-season professional removal, while the nest is small, is so much safer.

## Common questions

### Is it ever safe to remove a wasp nest myself?

For a very small, newly started paper-wasp nest in an open, easy-to-reach spot early in spring, some homeowners handle it — but even then there's real risk. Anything involving yellowjackets, hornets, a wall or ground nest, a nest you can't fully see, a ladder, or a household sting allergy should go to a professional.

### What should I do if I get stung multiple times?

Move away from the nest calmly but quickly, and watch for signs of a severe reaction — trouble breathing, swelling of the face or throat, dizziness, or a spreading rash. Those are a medical emergency; call 911 right away. For ordinary localized stings, ice and an over-the-counter antihistamine usually help, but see a doctor if you're concerned.

### Can I just spray a nest at night?

Wasps are less active at night, which is why the advice circulates, but it's still risky. You can't see well, you may misjudge the nest's size, hidden portions in walls or the ground go untreated, and a partial hit leaves a defensive colony behind. Professional treatment is safer and far more likely to actually finish the colony off.

### Will knocking down a nest get rid of the wasps?

No — it usually makes things worse. Knocking down a nest releases the colony and doesn't kill the wasps, which will often rebuild nearby. For yellowjackets and hornets especially, this is a fast way to get badly stung.

### What if the nest is inside my wall?
