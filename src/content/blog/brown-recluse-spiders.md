Brown recluse spiders are not found in Washington State, yet they are often
wrongly blamed for bites and for encounters with native spiders like the hobo
spider or the giant house spider. The misconception is persistent enough that it
is worth setting out plainly what does and does not live here.

## What are brown recluse spiders?

Brown recluse spiders (*Loxosceles reclusa*) are known for their venomous bite
and their reclusive habits. Their range is the central and southern United
States. They have no established population in Washington State, and verified
records here are essentially absent. The occasional Washington "brown recluse"
report almost always turns out to be a misidentified local spider. On the rare
occasion a genuine specimen does surface, it arrived as a single hitchhiker in
freight or belongings shipped from within the spider's actual range, and a
hitchhiker is not a population. Where they do live, they favor warm, dry,
undisturbed spaces such as attics, basements, and storage areas.

## Common misidentifications in Washington

Because brown recluse spiders are not present here, bites and sightings get
attributed to them by default. The species most often mistaken for one include:

- **Hobo spiders (*Eratigena agrestis*)** — a common funnel-web spider in the
  Pacific Northwest, found in basements, crawl spaces, and woodpiles. They look
  intimidating, but they are not the danger they were once described as. The
  claim that hobo bites cause necrotic wounds rested on studies that later
  research did not support, and the CDC removed the hobo spider from its list of
  venomous spiders — the current federal guidance for outdoor workers names only
  the black widow and the brown recluse. The Washington State Department of
  Health states that hobo spiders are not considered dangerous as previously
  thought, and that there is no evidence their venom has caused skin necrosis in
  people.
- **Giant house spiders (*Eratigena duellica*)** — closely related to the hobo
  spider, this large species is fast-moving and frequently enters homes,
  especially in the fall. Older references list it as *Eratigena atrica*, a name
  now treated as a doubtful catch-all. Harmless.
- **Yellow sac spiders (*Cheiracanthium* spp.)** — small, pale spiders that are
  sometimes blamed for bites. They will bite if pressed against skin, but the
  result is mild, localized pain and redness, nothing like the wound a brown
  recluse is capable of.

A useful check: the brown recluse is identified by six eyes arranged in three
pairs and a uniformly colored abdomen and legs, not by the violin marking most
people go looking for. That marking is subtle, varies a great deal, and appears
on plenty of unrelated spiders. Identification from a phone photo of a squashed
spider is guesswork, which is part of why the misattribution is so widespread.

## Practical spider control

Even though brown recluse spiders do not live in Washington, native spiders can
still become a nuisance. What actually works is fairly simple:

- **A walk around the property** to find the harborage spiders are using —
  woodpiles, clutter, dense vegetation against the walls, undisturbed corners —
  and clear it
- **Exterior and interior treatments** at the foundation, entry points, and the
  problem areas inside
- **Web and egg sac removal**, which is one of the more effective steps and one
  a homeowner can keep up between visits
- **Reducing the insect population** the spiders are feeding on, since spiders
  follow their food and a heavy spider presence usually means something else is
  available to hunt
- **A plan matched to the property**, rather than the same treatment everywhere
