In the Pacific Northwest, flea season runs hardest from late spring through fall, peaking in the warm, humid stretch of late summer and early fall — often August through October. But here's what most Bellingham pet owners miss: fleas don't truly disappear in winter. Our mild winters and heated homes keep them breeding indoors year-round, which is why year-round prevention beats seasonal scrambling every time.

One thing to be clear about up front: what goes **on your animal** is a veterinary decision. Your veterinarian chooses the flea preventive that suits your pet's species, age, weight and health, and that conversation belongs with them, not with a pest control company. What we can talk about here is the other side of the problem — the home and the yard, where the overwhelming majority of a flea population actually lives.

## When is flea season in Whatcom County?

Fleas thrive in warm, humid conditions — a near-perfect description of a Pacific Northwest summer. Populations start building as temperatures climb in late spring, ramp up through summer, and typically peak in late summer and early fall, when warmth and lingering humidity give eggs and larvae ideal conditions to develop. Our maritime climate matters here: even when it isn't blazing hot, the moist, mild air around Bellingham, Ferndale, and Lynden keeps flea larvae alive in shaded, damp spots outdoors.

Then winter arrives, and this is the trap. People assume cold weather ends the problem. Outdoors, cold and frost do knock back flea activity — but indoors, your heated home stays a comfortable 65 to 70 degrees, and any fleas that came in on a pet keep breeding right through January. That's why so many Whatcom County households discover a full-blown infestation in the middle of winter and can't figure out where it came from.

## How do I know if my pet has fleas?

Fleas are small and fast, so you'll usually notice the effects before you spot the insects. Watch for these signs:

- Excessive scratching, biting, or licking — especially around the base of the tail, belly, and hind legs.
- Flea dirt — tiny black specks like ground pepper in the fur. Comb some onto a damp white paper towel; if the specks smear reddish-brown, that's digested blood and confirms fleas.
- Small, fast-moving dark insects darting through the fur, most visible on the belly and inner thighs where the coat is thin.
- Hair loss, scabs, or red irritated skin, often from flea allergy dermatitis, an allergic reaction to flea saliva.
- Bites on people, usually small, itchy red bumps clustered around the ankles and lower legs.
- Flea eggs — tiny white ovals that fall off your pet into bedding, carpet, and furniture.

If the signs point to fleas, book the animal in with your veterinarian. Skin irritation, hair loss and scabbing can have several causes, and only a vet can sort that out and prescribe accordingly.

## Why do fleas keep coming back after I treat my pet?

This is the single most frustrating thing about fleas, and understanding it changes everything. The adult fleas you see on your pet are only a small fraction of the problem — often cited as around 5%. The other 95% is eggs, larvae, and pupae developing in your carpet, pet bedding, furniture, and floor cracks. Treat only the pet and that hidden majority is left untouched, so the infestation rebounds within days.

The pupal stage is the real villain. Fleas in their protective cocoon can lie dormant for weeks — sometimes months — and are shielded from most treatments. They wait for warmth, vibration, and carbon dioxide (a passing pet or person) before emerging. That's why a home can seem flea-free and then suddenly erupt, and why real control means the pet's veterinary treatment and the property being handled together.

### The flea life cycle — why one-time fixes fail

Fleas go through four stages: egg, larva, pupa, adult. The adults you can see are the tip of the iceberg. Eggs roll off your pet into carpet and bedding; larvae burrow into fibers and dark cracks; pupae seal themselves in tough cocoons that resist sprays and can stay dormant for months. Because these stages overlap and hide throughout your home, no single treatment clears them all at once. Effective control breaks the cycle at multiple points over a few weeks — exactly why treating the property alongside consistent, vet-directed pet prevention works when a one-time spray doesn't.

## How do fleas get into a Bellingham home in the first place?

Fleas almost always arrive on a warm-blooded host. Your dog or cat picks them up on a walk, in the yard, or at the groomer or boarding facility. But pets aren't the only carriers. In Whatcom County, any animal moving through the yard can drop flea eggs as it goes — a neighborhood cat, a rodent, or an animal denning under a deck, porch, or crawl space. If something has been sheltering close to the house, you can get fleas even if your own pets rarely go outside.

## Can fleas actually make my pet or family sick?

Fleas are more than an itchy nuisance. On pets, they can cause flea allergy dermatitis, an intense allergic reaction leading to scratching, hair loss, and skin infections. Fleas also transmit tapeworms when a pet swallows an infected flea while grooming. In young, small, or elderly animals, a heavy infestation can cause anemia from blood loss, which is genuinely dangerous. For people, the main issues are itchy bites and, rarely, allergic reactions. None of this is cause for panic, but it's a solid reason not to let an infestation build — and a reason to get an affected animal in front of a veterinarian rather than waiting it out.

## What actually works to get rid of fleas?

Because fleas live in four stages spread across your pet, your home, and your yard, lasting control has to address all three at once:

1. Have every pet on a flea preventive chosen by your veterinarian, kept up year-round rather than just in summer. Treating one pet while another goes untreated leaves a reservoir. Which product, and how often, is your vet's call.
2. Wash all pet bedding in hot water and dry on high heat to kill eggs, larvae, and pupae in the fabric.
3. Vacuum thoroughly and often — carpets, rugs, upholstery, and along baseboards. The vibration even coaxes dormant pupae to emerge. Seal and discard the vacuum contents outside afterward.
4. Treat the home professionally. A targeted interior treatment reaches the eggs, larvae, and adults hiding in carpet and cracks that DIY sprays miss, with follow-up timing that accounts for pupae hatching later.
5. Address the yard. Trim vegetation and clear shaded debris where larvae live, and close off the gaps under decks, porches and crawl space access so animals can't shelter underneath.

That division of labor is the whole point. The animal is the veterinarian's job. The carpet, the baseboards, the crawl space and the shaded strip of yard behind the shed are ours.

## Common questions

### When is peak flea season in the Pacific Northwest?

Flea activity builds through spring and summer and usually peaks in late summer and early fall — often August through October — when warmth and humidity are ideal for eggs and larvae. That said, fleas stay active indoors year-round here because heated homes stay warm enough for them to keep breeding through winter, so they're never fully gone.

### Do fleas die off in the winter in Bellingham?

Outdoors, cold weather and frost knock back flea activity significantly. Indoors, though, your heated home stays warm enough that any fleas already inside keep reproducing all winter. This is why many Whatcom County households discover an infestation in the middle of winter — the fleas never left, they just moved the party indoors.

### Why do fleas keep coming back after I treat my pet?

Because the adult fleas you see are only a small fraction of the population — often around 5%. The rest are eggs, larvae, and pupae hidden in your carpet, bedding, and furniture. Treating only the animal leaves that hidden majority to mature and re-infest. Pupae are especially stubborn, staying dormant in protective cocoons for weeks or months. Lasting control means the vet's treatment for the pet and a treatment of the home and yard, running together.

### Can I get rid of fleas without treating my house?

It's very difficult. Because most of the flea population lives in your home's carpet and cracks as eggs, larvae, and pupae rather than on your pet, treating only the animal almost always leads to a rebound. Effective control combines a year-round preventive prescribed by your veterinarian with treatment of the home and, where needed, the yard, to reach every stage of the life cycle.

### Are fleas dangerous to my pets or my family?

They can be. In pets, fleas cause flea allergy dermatitis, transmit tapeworms, and — in young, small, or elderly animals — can cause dangerous anemia in heavy infestations. For people, the main problem is itchy bites and occasional allergic reactions. It's not usually an emergency, but it's a good reason not to let an infestation build, and a good reason to have an affected animal seen by a vet.

### How do fleas get into my home if my pet stays inside?

Fleas ride in on any warm-blooded host, not just your pet. In Whatcom County, an animal denning under a deck, porch, or crawl space will drop flea eggs around your home, and fleas can also hitch in on clothing or a visiting animal. If something has been sheltering nearby, you can get fleas even with strictly indoor pets — which is why closing off those spaces is often part of the fix.

### How long does it take to get rid of a flea infestation?

Because of the pupae that hatch over time, clearing a flea infestation usually takes a few weeks rather than a single day. A treatment targets the adults, eggs, and larvae right away, and correctly timed follow-up addresses the pupae as they emerge. Combined with consistent vet-directed pet prevention and cleaning, most infestations are resolved within a few weeks.
