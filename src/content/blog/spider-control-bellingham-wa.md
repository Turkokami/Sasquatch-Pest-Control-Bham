Bellingham and Whatcom County are home to dozens of spider species, and the vast
majority are harmless and actually beneficial for controlling other insects. One
— the western black widow — is genuinely medically significant, and it is
uncommon on this side of the Cascades. The giant house spider is the one people
actually meet, and it is startling rather than dangerous. A heavy indoor spider
population usually signals an underlying insect problem feeding it, so effective
spider control means addressing the spiders and their food supply together.

Spiders are one of the most consistent pest complaints in Bellingham, not
because they are especially dangerous here, but because they are highly visible,
they leave webs everywhere, and some are genuinely large and startling. If you
have found a spider the size of a half-dollar in your bathroom at midnight, you
already know why people call us. This guide covers the species you are most
likely to see, which ones actually warrant concern, why spiders move indoors,
and what real spider control looks like.

## What spider species live in Bellingham and Whatcom County?

Most homeowners here encounter only a handful of species regularly.

### Giant house spider (*Eratigena duellica*) — the big one

This is the spider most Bellingham residents call about. Females can reach a leg
span of up to 4 inches and they move extremely fast in short bursts. They are
brown to dark gray with a patterned abdomen, most visible in late summer and
fall when males roam for mates, but active year-round indoors. Older references
call this species *Eratigena atrica*, a name now treated as a doubtful catch-all
rather than the correct one for our local spider.

- **Habitat:** Dark corners, basements, garages, closets, under furniture
- **Behavior:** Build funnel-shaped webs and wait at the narrow end for prey
- **Danger level:** None to minimal — venom is not medically significant in
  healthy adults
- **Good news:** They are effective predators of silverfish, earwigs, and other
  household pests

### Hobo spider (*Eratigena agrestis*)

Closely related to the giant house spider and frequently confused with it.
Medium-sized, brown, and also a funnel-web builder. Hobo spiders were once
blamed for necrotic wounds, and that claim has not survived scrutiny — the
studies behind it were later questioned and never replicated, the CDC removed
the hobo spider from its list of venomous spiders, and the Washington State
Department of Health states that hobo spiders are not considered dangerous as
previously thought and that no evidence links their venom to skin necrosis. They
tend to stay at ground level in garages, crawl spaces, and along basement walls.

They are also less common than the name suggests. WSU Extension reports that
west of the Cascades the giant house spider outnumbers the hobo spider roughly
three to one, and UC IPM records that the larger species has competitively
displaced it across parts of the Pacific Northwest. Separating the two reliably
means examining the reproductive structures under magnification — the chevron
markings people go looking for are not used as a character by either WSU's
identification key or UC IPM's pest note on the species.

### Yellow sac spider (*Cheiracanthium* spp.)

Small, pale yellow to cream-colored, and they do not build traditional webs.
Found on walls and ceilings, often in silk retreat tubes in corners. They are
legitimate biters — one of the more common causes of spider bites in Washington —
though their venom causes mild, localized pain and redness rather than serious
symptoms.

### Cellar spider / "daddy long legs" (*Pholcus phalangioides*)

The spindly-legged spiders that build messy, irregular webs in basement corners,
garages, and crawl spaces. Completely harmless and actually beneficial — they
prey on other spiders, including more problematic species. The myth that their
venom is extremely potent is false.

### Orb weaver spiders (family Araneidae)

The classic circular-web spiders, including the striking cross spider (*Araneus
diadematus*) with its white cross marking. Primarily outdoor spiders that build
large webs in gardens, on fences, and around exterior lights. They come inside
accidentally or set up at entry points where flying insects gather.

### Western black widow (*Latrodectus hesperus*)

The Washington State Department of Health describes the western black widow as
common to eastern Washington, and that is the accurate shape of it: the range
follows the drier, warmer country east and south of the Cascades, and
Bellingham's cool wet climate is close to the opposite of what the species
wants. It is uncommon here rather than routine. Where one does turn up it is in
a dry, sheltered spot — wood piles, under outdoor furniture, meter boxes, the
undisturbed corners of garages and sheds. Females are glossy black with a red
hourglass on the **underside** of the abdomen.

- **Behavior:** Slow-moving and not aggressive; bites usually happen when the
  spider is pressed against skin
- **Danger level:** This is the one local spider that is genuinely medically
  significant. A bite can cause severe pain, muscle cramping and other
  whole-body symptoms, and warrants prompt medical evaluation, particularly in
  children, older adults, and anyone with existing health conditions. Serious
  outcomes are rare and deaths are very rare, but a suspected bite should be
  looked at by a doctor rather than waited out.

The red hourglass sits on the underside, so you will not see it while the spider
rests on its web above you. The spider is shiny black, globular, and about 1/2
inch in body length. If you find one you suspect is a black widow, do not handle
it — call us and we will identify it.

## Why do spiders come inside Bellingham homes?

Spiders do not seek out houses for their own sake — they follow their food.
Understanding why they move indoors is the key to long-term control.

### They're following insect prey

Spiders are predators; they go where the food is. A home with active silverfish,
earwigs, or flies attracts spiders to exploit that supply. A lot of spiders
usually means a meaningful underlying insect population. Treating spiders without
addressing their food source produces temporary results at best.

### Exterior lighting concentrates flying insects

Standard white or warm-spectrum exterior lights draw flying insects in large
numbers, and those insects draw spiders. That is why entry-adjacent lights are
often the most heavily webbed areas of a home. Switching to yellow LED or sodium
vapor bulbs reduces insect attraction considerably and, with it, web building.

### Harborage and shelter

Spiders need shelter for retreats and egg sacs. Cluttered basements, stored
cardboard, wood piles in garages, and dense landscaping against the exterior all
provide harborage. Reducing clutter and vegetation contact reduces available
habitat.

### Mating season, not the cold

Sightings peak from late August through October, and the reason is not
temperature. Spiders here overwinter outdoors perfectly well and are not moving
in to get warm. What happens in those weeks is that mature males stop
maintaining a web and start walking to find females, so animals that were
sedentary and hidden become mobile and obvious. The Burke Museum, which holds
the state reference collection for regional arachnology, adds a second reading
worth knowing: house spiders are largely an indoor-adapted lineage, and
generally fewer than five percent of the spiders seen indoors have ever been
outdoors at all. Some of the September traffic walked under a door and some of
it hatched in the building — either way the population was there beforehand, and
the season ends on its own.

Moisture still matters, indirectly and year-round. Damp crawl spaces, basements
and plumbing leaks drive the insect prey that spiders eat, and the spider count
follows the insects.

## What actually works for spider control in Bellingham?

1. **Reduce the food supply.** A perimeter insect-barrier treatment that
   suppresses earwigs, silverfish, and flies cuts the spider food supply and
   reduces spider numbers, even without targeting spiders directly.
2. **Exterior web removal and perimeter treatment.** Physically removing exterior
   webs plus a residual treatment at the foundation, entry points, and soffits
   disrupts harborage and creates a treated barrier. This is the most effective
   exterior approach.
3. **Interior treatment of problem areas.** Applications in crawl spaces,
   basements, garage corners, and behind appliances target established
   populations, while targeted web removal pulls out egg sacs.
4. **Exclusion and harborage reduction.** Sealing entry points, fixing screens,
   replacing worn door sweeps, and clearing exterior clutter limits access and
   keeps spiders from re-establishing.
5. **Lighting adjustment.** Switching exterior entry lights to yellow LED
   reduces the insect concentration that draws spiders — an easy, chemical-free
   win.

The most common mistake is spraying surfaces where spiders walk with consumer
insecticides. Spiders' long legs hold their bodies off the surface, so they do
not pick up contact residuals the way crawling insects do. Professional
treatments use products chosen for effectiveness against spiders specifically.

## DIY spider prevention steps for Bellingham homeowners

- Sweep down exterior webs regularly — repeated removal discourages permanent
  establishment
- Switch exterior lights to yellow LED or sodium vapor
- Seal entry points: fix torn screens, replace worn door sweeps, caulk utility
  gaps
- Reduce clutter in basements, garages, and crawl spaces
- Move firewood storage away from the home — wood stacks are ideal black widow
  and giant house spider habitat
- Clear landscaping from direct foundation contact
- Vacuum egg sacs from corners, under furniture, and in closets
- Keep garage doors closed when not in use — garages are the primary interior
  harborage zone

## Common questions about spiders in Bellingham, WA

### Are there dangerous spiders in Bellingham, WA?

The one medically significant spider in the state is the western black widow,
and the Department of Health describes it as common to eastern Washington rather
than here — the range follows the drier country east and south of the Cascades,
which makes it uncommon in Whatcom County and confined to dry sheltered spots
like wood piles and undisturbed garage corners when it does occur. Giant house
and hobo spiders, whatever their size suggests, are not medically significant.
Yellow sac spiders can cause mild local reactions. If you find a small, shiny
black spider with a globular abdomen in a dry sheltered location, treat it as a
possible black widow and do not handle it.

### Why do I see more spiders in fall in Bellingham?

Because it is mating season. Mature males leave their retreats and walk to find
females, which turns hidden animals into visible ones over a few weeks. It is
not the cold — spiders here winter outdoors without difficulty and are not
seeking warmth. The ones crossing your floor in September and October have been
in or around the building all summer, and by the Burke Museum's account a large
share of the house spiders you see indoors have never been outdoors at all.
Treatment done in late summer, before the peak, is better timed than treatment
done during it.

### Are the giant spiders I see in my house really harmless?

Yes — the large brown spiders most homeowners encounter are giant house spiders,
and they are not medically significant in healthy adults. They can bite if
forcibly handled, but the venom does not cause serious symptoms. That said,
"harmless" and "welcome" are different things; if large spiders in your living
space are an ongoing quality-of-life issue, professional control is a legitimate
and effective solution.

### I found a black spider with a red marking. Is it a black widow?

Possibly, though the odds are against it here — the western black widow is a
Washington species that the Department of Health calls common to eastern
Washington, and it is uncommon west of the Cascades. The defining features are a
small shiny black body, a globular abdomen, and a red hourglass on the
underside. The false black widow, which is genuinely common in local garages and
basements, is purple-brown rather than jet black and has no hourglass at all. Do
not handle either; call us and we will identify it at no charge.
If someone has been bitten by a suspected black widow, seek medical evaluation
promptly, as symptoms can be delayed.

### Why do I have so many spiders in my basement?

Basements offer stable temperature and humidity, low light, abundant harborage,
and proximity to the insects spiders eat — earwigs, silverfish, isopods, and
fungus gnats are all common basement prey. Addressing moisture, reducing clutter,
and applying a professional perimeter treatment to the basement interior
typically reduces spider numbers significantly.

### Do ultrasonic spider repellers work?

No — there is no credible evidence that ultrasonic repellers meaningfully affect
spider populations. Independent studies have found no significant reduction in
pest activity. Physical exclusion, prey reduction, targeted treatment, and web
removal are what actually work.

### How long does professional spider treatment take to work?

Initial results appear within days as spiders cross treated surfaces. Reduced
interior web-building and sightings are usually apparent within one to two weeks.
Professional residual treatments typically hold up for 30 to 90 days depending on
conditions, and if activity returns before then, call us and we will come back
out.
