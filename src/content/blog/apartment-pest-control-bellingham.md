When pests show up in a Bellingham apartment or rental this spring, the fastest fix is to report it in writing immediately, treat the whole building instead of a single unit, and coordinate between tenant and landlord rather than pointing fingers. Spring is when ants, spiders, and rodents wake up across Whatcom County, and multi-unit buildings are especially vulnerable because a gap in one apartment becomes a highway to every other. Here's exactly how renters, landlords, and property managers in Bellingham, Ferndale, and Lynden should handle a spring outbreak the right way.

## Who is responsible for pest control in a Bellingham rental — the landlord or the tenant?

In many cases, ongoing pest control in a rental is the **landlord's responsibility**, but there are real exceptions. Washington's Residential Landlord-Tenant Act (RCW 59.18) generally requires landlords to keep rental housing fit to live in, which includes dealing with infestations that weren't caused by the tenant — think rodents entering through gaps in the building envelope or ants trailing in from a shared wall. Single-family rentals are treated differently from multi-unit buildings, which is one of the details people most often get wrong.

Responsibility can shift to the tenant when the tenant's own actions clearly caused the problem — leaving food out, letting garbage pile up, or bringing in a used couch that turned out to be infested. Because the line depends on your lease and the specifics, both sides should document everything in writing and check the lease terms and current Washington law rather than assume. This is general information, not legal advice.

Many well-run buildings in Bellingham sidestep the whole argument by including routine, professional pest service in the lease so small problems get caught before they become disputes.

## What does Washington law generally say about pests in rental housing?

Washington's Residential Landlord-Tenant Act sets baseline duties for both sides. At a high level, responsibilities usually break down like this:

- **Landlords:** keep the unit habitable, respond to repair requests within a reasonable time, and address infestations that aren't the tenant's fault.
- **Tenants:** keep the unit reasonably clean, dispose of garbage properly, avoid attracting pests, report problems promptly, and allow reasonable access for treatment.

Washington law also generally protects tenants from retaliation for making a good-faith complaint. If you're a Whatcom County renter unsure of your rights, keep written records and consider contacting a local tenant resource or reviewing RCW 59.18 directly.

## Why do apartments get worse spring pest outbreaks than single-family homes?

Multi-unit buildings share far more than a roof. The features that make apartment living convenient are the same ones pests use to spread:

- **Shared walls, ceilings, and floors** give ants, cockroaches, and mice a hidden network of voids to travel through.
- **Plumbing chases and utility lines** run between units, creating ready-made highways behind the drywall.
- **Shared trash and recycling areas** concentrate food sources and draw rodents and flies.
- **High turnover** means furniture and boxes move in and out constantly — a common way bed bugs and cockroaches arrive.
- **One neglected unit** can reinfest a whole floor, which is why treating a single apartment rarely holds.

## Which pests invade Bellingham apartments in spring?

As the Pacific Northwest warms up in April and May, a predictable lineup shows up in Whatcom County rentals.

### Ants

Odorous house ants, pavement ants, and moisture-loving carpenter ants start foraging indoors, trailing along baseboards toward kitchens and bathrooms. In apartments, an ant trail often crosses between units, which is why store-bought sprays usually just split the colony and make it worse.

### Spiders

Warmer weather brings more insects, and more insects bring the spiders that eat them. Giant house spiders and common web-builders turn up in corners, closets, and around windows.

### Rodents

Mice slip through gaps about a quarter of an inch across — the width of an ordinary pencil, which is the comparison worth carrying because you can push a pencil into a suspect gap and settle the question on the spot. Washington's Department of Health and Whatcom County's own rodent guidance both use that quarter-inch figure, and Bellingham's rental inspection checklist flags foundation openings larger than a quarter inch as an infestation risk, so in a rented building it is a compliance number as well as a biological one.

Ignore the comparison you will see repeated everywhere else, which is that a mouse needs a hole "the size of a dime." A dime is 0.705 inches across by the United States Mint's own specification — very nearly three times a quarter inch, and wider than the half-inch opening the extension literature gives for rats. Seal an apartment building to the dime standard and you have closed almost nothing while believing the work is finished. Where a larger coin genuinely is useful, it is the penny at 0.750 inches, which is about what an adult Norway rat needs.

Rats matter less than the arithmetic suggests, because the exclusion threshold does not actually move between the two species. Washington's Department of Fish and Wildlife puts it in the form that resolves the confusion: a rat needs a quarter-inch hole to get its incisors around an edge and gnaw the opening up to a size it fits through. The threshold that protects a building is the size at which a rodent can start work, not the size at which it can walk in — which is why a quarter inch is the standard for both.

Mice favor the wall voids, chases and shared service runs of apartment buildings, and in spring they become bolder as they forage and nest. In a multi-unit building that mobility is the whole problem: a gap in one unit's baseboard is an opening into the structure, not into an apartment.

### Occasional invaders

Silverfish, sowbugs, and overwintering pests like boxelder bugs and cluster flies also become active, often appearing near windows as they try to move back outdoors.

## What should a tenant do the moment they spot pests?

Speed and documentation are everything. If you rent in Bellingham and see pests, work through this checklist:

- Take clear, dated photos or video of what you found and where.
- Report it to your landlord or property manager **in writing** (email or text) — not just a phone call — and keep a copy.
- Note whether neighbors have seen the same thing; apartment problems are almost never limited to one unit.
- Avoid over-the-counter sprays and foggers, which can scatter colonies deeper into wall voids and complicate professional treatment.
- Reduce food and water access: seal food, wipe counters, fix or report drips, and empty trash regularly.
- Keep a simple log of every sighting and every message you send.

## What should landlords and property managers do?

A fast, building-wide response protects your tenants, your property, and your reputation. The most effective owners and managers in Whatcom County:

- Respond to reports quickly and in writing to keep a clear timeline.
- Hire a licensed professional rather than relying on maintenance staff and retail products.
- Have adjacent units treated, not just the unit that reported the problem.
- Address the building envelope — gaps, vents, door sweeps, and utility penetrations — so pests can't keep re-entering.
- Schedule follow-up visits to confirm the problem is actually gone.
- Communicate clearly with tenants about timing, preparation, and re-entry.

## Common questions

### Can my landlord make me pay for pest control in Washington?

It depends on the cause. If an infestation results from the tenant's own negligence — poor sanitation or bringing in infested items, for example — the cost may fall to the tenant. If the problem is pre-existing or building-wide, it is typically the landlord's responsibility under Washington's Residential Landlord-Tenant Act, though single-family rentals are handled differently from multi-unit buildings. Check your lease and keep written records either way.

### Can I be evicted for reporting a pest problem?

Washington law generally protects tenants from retaliation for making a good-faith complaint about habitability, including pests. Report problems in writing, keep copies of everything, and consider contacting a local tenant resource if you have concerns.
