If you're finding shield-shaped brown bugs on your windows, walls, and ceilings as the weather cools, you're seeing the **brown marmorated stink bug** doing exactly what it does every fall in Bellingham: looking for a warm, dry place to spend the winter. As nighttime temperatures drop, these bugs move off plants and gravitate to the sunny sides of houses, then slip inside through gaps around windows, siding, vents, and the attic. The single most important thing to know: **don't squish them**, because crushing a stink bug releases the exact odor you're trying to avoid and the fluid stains what it lands on.

The fall stink bug call is one of our most predictable of the year across Bellingham, Ferndale, Lynden, and greater Whatcom County. Here's why they show up, how they get in, and what actually keeps them out.

## What exactly is a brown marmorated stink bug?

The brown marmorated stink bug (*Halyomorpha halys*), or BMSB, is an invasive insect that has spread across much of the country and become well established in the Pacific Northwest. It's about the size of a fingernail, shaped like a shield or heraldic crest, and mottled brown with faint lighter bands on the antennae and a distinctive marbled pattern. "Marmorated" literally means marbled.

During spring and summer they live outdoors, feeding on fruit trees, ornamental plants, and garden crops. They're a serious agricultural nuisance, but for most Bellingham homeowners the real problem is what they do in autumn: they gather in large numbers and try to get indoors. The smell is a defense mechanism, a pungent cilantro-meets-old-gym-sock odor released from glands on their abdomen. Crushing, aggressive vacuuming, or even startling them can trigger it, which is why gentle removal matters.

## Why do stink bugs come indoors every fall?

It comes down to survival. Brown marmorated stink bugs overwinter as adults in a dormant state called diapause. In their native range they'd tuck into rock crevices and dead trees, but in our neighborhoods the warm, sheltered interior of a house (attics, wall voids, window frames, and behind siding) is an even better option.

The trigger is temperature and daylight. As days shorten and nights cool through September and October, stink bugs stop feeding and begin searching for overwintering sites. They're drawn to the warmth radiating off buildings, which is why you'll often see them clustered on the south- and west-facing walls of your home on sunny fall afternoons, since those walls hold the most heat. From there, it's a short trip to any gap that leads inside.

## How are they getting into my house?

Stink bugs are surprisingly good at finding small openings. Because they're flat and shield-shaped, they can squeeze through gaps you'd never think twice about. The most common entry points on Bellingham homes include:

- Gaps around window and door frames, especially where old caulk has cracked or shrunk
- Torn or ill-fitting window screens
- Utility penetrations where pipes, cables, and dryer vents pass through exterior walls
- Gaps under and behind siding, and around trim boards
- Unscreened attic and gable vents, and gaps around soffits
- Weep holes, foundation cracks, and spaces where the roofline meets the walls
- Chimneys and gaps around recessed lighting or exhaust fans

Once inside a wall void or attic, they go dormant. The frustrating part comes later: on warm winter days, or when your heating warms the walls, some of them "wake up," get confused about the season, and wander into your living space, appearing on windows and lampshades in the middle of January.

## Are stink bugs dangerous to people, pets, or my home?

The reassuring news is that brown marmorated stink bugs are essentially harmless to you and your house. They don't bite, they don't sting, they don't carry disease, and they don't reproduce indoors. They're just overwintering, not breeding, inside your walls. They also don't chew wood or damage the structure the way termites or carpenter ants do.

Their offenses are purely nuisance ones: the numbers can be unnerving, and the smell is genuinely unpleasant if they're disturbed or crushed. For people with sensitivities, the odor and shed material from a large indoor cluster can be an irritant. But there's no health or structural threat, which is why the goal is exclusion and removal, not aggressive chemical treatment inside the home.

## What's the right way to remove stink bugs I find inside?

The golden rule is to remove them gently and avoid triggering the smell:

1. **Hand-remove the calm ones.** Scoop a slow-moving stink bug into a container or a piece of paper and release it well away from the house, or drop it into a container of soapy water.
2. **Use a vacuum thoughtfully.** A vacuum is efficient for larger numbers, but a standard bag or canister will smell afterward. Many people keep a dedicated shop vac with a little soapy water in the canister for this job, or empty and clean the vacuum promptly.
3. **Don't squish them on walls or fabric.** Crushing releases the odor and can leave a stain on painted walls, curtains, and upholstery.
4. **Skip the indoor bug bombs.** Foggers and sprays don't reach the bugs tucked inside wall voids, and a dead cluster inside a wall can create odor problems of its own.

## How do I actually keep stink bugs out?

Because stink bugs enter from the outside, **exclusion is the strategy that works**. The goal is to seal the building envelope before the fall migration and reduce the conditions that draw them to the house:

- Seal gaps and cracks around windows, doors, siding, trim, and utility penetrations with quality exterior caulk or sealant.
- Repair or replace damaged screens on windows, doors, and vents, and add screening to attic, gable, and soffit vents.
- Install door sweeps and weatherstripping on exterior doors, including the garage.
- Address roofline and eave gaps, where warm attic air and sun-warmed surfaces are especially attractive.
- Time it right: the best window for exclusion work is late summer through early fall, before the bugs begin clustering on the walls.
- Reduce exterior attractants by managing outdoor lighting at night, which can draw stink bugs toward the house.

The single highest-value fix is sealing the sunny, south- and west-facing walls and their windows first. That's where stink bugs concentrate on warm fall afternoons, so closing those gaps stops the largest share of them before they ever find a way in.

## Common questions

### Why shouldn't I just squish stink bugs?

Crushing a stink bug releases the pungent defensive odor from glands on its abdomen, the very smell you're trying to avoid, and it lingers. It does not call in more of them, though: the National Pesticide Information Center states that killing a stink bug does not attract more stink bugs, and that the odor released will not attract other bugs. Squishing them on walls, curtains, or upholstery can also leave a stain. The better approach is to gently scoop them into a container or use a vacuum you can clean out afterward, then release or dispose of them away from the house.

### Are stink bugs harmful to my family or my house?

No. Brown marmorated stink bugs don't bite, sting, spread disease, or damage the structure of your home, and they don't reproduce indoors. They're only overwintering in your walls, not breeding there. Their offenses are purely nuisance: the numbers can be alarming and the smell is unpleasant when they're disturbed. That's why exclusion and gentle removal, not heavy chemical treatment, is the right response.

### Why do stink bugs keep appearing inside during winter?

Stink bugs that entered in fall settle into wall voids and attics to go dormant for the winter. On warm winter days, or when your heating warms the walls, some of them wake up, get confused about the season, and wander into your living space instead of back outside. That's why you'll see them on windows in January even though you never saw them come in. They've been inside your walls since October.

### When is the best time to stink-bug-proof my home?

Late summer through early fall, before the bugs begin clustering on your exterior walls to look for a way in. Sealing gaps around windows, doors, siding, and vents ahead of the migration stops most of them before they ever get inside. If you wait until they're already appearing indoors in large numbers, exclusion still helps, but you'll be dealing with the ones that already made it into the walls.

### Do stink bug foggers or bug bombs work?

Not well. Indoor foggers and sprays don't reach the bugs tucked deep inside wall voids and attics, so they leave most of the population untouched. Worse, killing a cluster inside a wall can create an odor problem as the dead bugs decompose. The reliable approach is sealing entry points on the outside of the home and removing the bugs you find indoors by hand or vacuum.

### Why do stink bugs seem worse some years than others?

Stink bug numbers swing quite a bit from year to year. A warm, dry summer tends to boost their population, so you'll see heavier fall pressure the following autumn. A sudden early cold snap can compress the whole migration into a short, intense window where it feels like they all show up at once. Local factors matter too: homes near wooded edges, orchards, gardens, or agricultural land in areas like Lynden and Everson often see more of them simply because there's a larger source population nearby.
