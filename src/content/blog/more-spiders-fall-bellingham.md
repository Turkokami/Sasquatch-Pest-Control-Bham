If spiders suddenly seem to be everywhere in your Bellingham home this fall —
bigger ones in the corners, webs across the porch overnight, the occasional
fast-mover darting across the floor — you are not imagining it, and you are not
alone. Fall is peak spider season in the Pacific Northwest for one main reason:
it is **mating season**, so mature male spiders leave their webs and roam in
search of females. What you are seeing is a change in behavior rather than an
arrival. The good news is that the vast majority of spiders you will meet in
Whatcom County are harmless, and a few simple steps go a long way toward keeping
their numbers down.

The fall spider surge is one of the most common seasonal questions we get across
Bellingham, Ferndale, Lynden, and greater Whatcom County. Here is what is
actually going on and what to do about it.

## Why do I see so many more spiders in the fall?

It feels like a sudden invasion, but it is really a seasonal shift in behavior,
and mostly it is about spiders that were already around becoming far more
visible.

### It's mating season

Fall is when many spider species reach maturity and begin mating. Male spiders,
which spend most of their lives hidden, leave their webs and wander in search of
a mate. That is why the spiders you notice in fall are often larger and more
mobile — they are mature males on the move, crossing floors, climbing walls, and
turning up in places you do not usually see spiders. They were there all along;
now they are out in the open.

### It is not the cold — and this is where most fall spider articles go wrong

The intuitive explanation, that spiders are coming inside to escape the weather,
is the part nearly everybody gets wrong, this post included before it was
corrected. Spiders here overwinter perfectly well outdoors and they are not
seeking central heating. What concentrates spiders anywhere is food. They settle
where there are insects to catch, which is why garages, basements, crawl spaces,
eaves and lit entryways carry the most of them, and carry them all year rather
than seasonally.

Worth knowing alongside that: the Burke Museum, which holds the state reference
collection for arachnology in this region, describes house spiders as a largely
indoor-adapted lineage rather than garden spiders that wandered in, and puts the
share of indoor spiders that have ever been outdoors at generally under five
percent. So some of what you meet in September walked under a door, and some of
it hatched in the building or the crawlspace beneath it. Both are happening. In
either version the spiders were present before the month began, and what changed
is how visible they are.

## Which spiders am I likely seeing in Bellingham?

Whatcom County is home to a range of common, harmless spiders. The ones you are
most likely to encounter in fall include:

- **Giant house spider** — a large, fast-moving brown spider that is a classic
  fall sighting in the Pacific Northwest. Its size startles people, but it is
  harmless and actually helps control other pests.
- **Common house spider** — a smaller spider that builds tangled cobwebs in
  corners, garages, and basements.
- **Cross orbweaver (garden spider)** — the large spider that spins circular
  webs across porches, eaves, and gardens in fall. Harmless and beneficial.
- **Cellar spiders (daddy long-legs)** — thin-legged spiders that hang in loose
  webs in basements and crawl spaces.
- **Hobo spider** — a brown funnel-web spider found in the region, and less
  common here than its name recognition suggests. Its old reputation for causing
  necrotic wounds has not held up: the claim rests on work that later research
  did not support, the CDC removed the hobo spider from its list of venomous
  spiders, and the Washington State Department of Health now states that hobo
  spiders are not considered dangerous as previously thought and that no
  evidence links their venom to skin necrosis. Telling one from a giant house
  spider by eye is not possible — that separation needs the reproductive
  structures under magnification — and for safety purposes it does not matter,
  because neither is dangerous.

## Are any of these spiders dangerous?

For the overwhelming majority, no. The spiders you will typically see around a
Bellingham home are harmless to people and pets — they would rather flee than
bite, and most are not capable of causing meaningful harm even if they did. In
fact, they are doing you a favor by eating other insects.

Western Washington is fortunate on this front: we do not have established
populations of the medically significant spiders that worry people in other
parts of the country. The western black widow is a Washington species that the
state Department of Health describes as common to eastern Washington; its range
follows the drier country east and south of the Cascades, so it is uncommon on
this side of them and generally confined to dry, sheltered, undisturbed spots
like woodpiles and utility boxes. The brown recluse is not established in
Washington at all —
verified records in the state are essentially absent, and the occasional report
almost always turns out to be a misidentified local spider or, very rarely, a
single hitchhiker that arrived in freight from elsewhere. It does not live and
breed here.

That does not mean you should handle spiders — any spider can bite if trapped
against skin, and reactions vary — but it does mean the fear of a serious bite
is largely misplaced in our area.

## How do I keep spiders out of my house this fall?

Spider control is really about two things: making it harder for spiders to get
in, and removing what draws them, namely other insects. Handle those, and the
spider population drops on its own.

### Seal entry points

- Seal gaps around windows, doors, siding, and utility penetrations
- Repair torn window and door screens, and add door sweeps
- Screen vents and close gaps along the roofline and foundation

### Reduce what attracts them

- Reduce indoor and outdoor clutter where spiders hide and web up — garages,
  basements, storage areas, and woodpiles
- Sweep away webs and egg sacs regularly, indoors and out, so populations do not
  build
- Manage exterior lighting, which attracts the insects spiders feed on. Consider
  warmer-toned bulbs or moving lights away from doors
- Keep other insects in check, since fewer bugs means less food and fewer
  spiders
- Vacuum regularly, especially in corners, along baseboards, and in undisturbed
  areas

## What's the best way to deal with a spider I find indoors?

For a single spider, the simplest humane approach is the cup-and-paper method:
place a cup over it, slide a piece of paper underneath, and release it outside.
A vacuum is efficient for spiders, webs, and egg sacs alike — just empty it
promptly. There is rarely a need to reach for spray on an individual spider, and
squishing is not necessary if you would rather relocate them, given how many are
actively beneficial.

If you are consistently seeing large numbers, finding webs faster than you can
clear them, or simply want to enjoy your home in fall without the constant
surprises, that is where a professional approach helps, because it targets the
whole picture rather than one spider at a time.

## Common questions

### Why do I suddenly see so many spiders in the fall?

Because it is mating season. Mature male spiders, which normally stay hidden,
leave their webs and wander in search of females, which makes them far more
visible as they cross floors and walls. It is not the cold: spiders here
overwinter outdoors without difficulty and are not looking for heat. Most of
what you see was already living in and around your house, and some of it —
the Burke Museum would say most of it — has never been outdoors at all. What
changed in September is activity, not address.

### Are the spiders in my Bellingham home dangerous?

Almost never. The spiders you will typically see around Whatcom County homes —
giant house spiders, common house spiders, garden orbweavers, and cellar spiders
— are harmless to people and pets and would rather flee than bite. Western
Washington does not have established populations of the medically significant
spiders that worry people elsewhere: the western black widow belongs to the
drier country east and south of the Cascades and is uncommon on this side, and
the brown recluse is not established in this state at all. Handling
spiders still is not advisable, but a serious bite is very unlikely in our area.

### Do house spiders come inside from the yard each fall?

Partly, and less than you would think. Some mature males living along a
foundation, in a window well or under a deck do cross a threshold in these
weeks, which is why door sweeps make a visible difference. But the Burke
Museum's arachnology material describes house spiders as a largely
indoor-adapted lineage rather than outdoor spiders that came in, and puts the
share of indoor spiders that have ever been outdoors at generally under five
percent. Both accounts point the same way for a household: the population was
present before September, and what changes seasonally is activity and
visibility, especially among the roaming males.

### How do I keep spiders out of my house?

Focus on access and food. Seal gaps around windows, doors, siding, and utility
lines; repair screens and add door sweeps; and screen vents. Then reduce what
attracts them: clear clutter and webs, manage exterior lighting that draws their
insect prey, keep other bugs in check, and vacuum regularly in corners and along
baseboards. Since spiders follow their food, keeping other insects down is one
of the most effective long-term spider controls.

### What should I do with a spider I find inside?

For a single spider, the cup-and-paper method works well: place a cup over it,
slide paper underneath, and release it outside. A vacuum handles spiders, webs,
and egg sacs efficiently — just empty it promptly. Because so many local spiders
are beneficial insect-eaters, there is usually no need to spray or squish an
individual spider unless you prefer to. For persistent heavy activity, a broader
approach works better than dealing with them one at a time.

### Should I kill spiders or leave them alone?

That is up to you, but it is worth knowing that most spiders around your home
are genuinely beneficial — they eat flies, mosquitoes, and other insects, and a
healthy spider presence often means fewer other bugs. If a spider is not
bothering you, relocating it outside is a fine option. If you are seeing large
numbers or they are affecting your comfort, addressing the underlying insect
population and entry points is more effective than picking off individuals.
