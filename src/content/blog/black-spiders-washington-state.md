Most black spiders encountered in Washington State and Bellingham homes are
completely harmless. The giant house spider — one of the most commonly seen
large black-to-dark-brown spiders in the Pacific Northwest — is not medically
significant. The western black widow is a Washington species, however, and a
small black spider with a globular abdomen in a dry, sheltered spot deserves
careful attention. The state Department of Health describes that spider as
common to eastern Washington, and its range follows the drier country east and
south of the Cascades, so it is uncommon on this side of them. Identification is
the critical first step.

If you have found a large black spider in your Bellingham home and wondered
whether it is dangerous, that is a reasonable question. Washington has dozens of
spider species and several are black or very dark brown, so getting the
identification right matters. The answer ranges from "completely harmless,
actually beneficial" to "potentially medically significant, do not handle it."
This guide walks through the dark spiders most common in Whatcom County homes,
how to tell them apart, and what to do if you have been bitten by one you cannot
identify.

## The black spiders you'll actually encounter in Bellingham homes

### Giant house spider (*Eratigena duellica*) — the most common black spider here

When homeowners call about a "huge black spider," they are almost always
describing the giant house spider. It is one of the most common spiders in the
Pacific Northwest and responsible for the majority of alarming encounters in
Whatcom County homes. Older references list it under the name *Eratigena
atrica*, which is now treated as a doubtful catch-all name rather than the
correct one for our local species.

- **Size:** Females can reach a leg span of 3–4 inches — genuinely large
- **Color:** Dark brown to black with a mottled abdomen; legs are dark and
  banded
- **Web:** Flat funnel-shaped web with a retreat at the narrow end, in corners,
  basements, and garages
- **Behavior:** Extremely fast in short bursts; retreats when disturbed; males
  wander in late summer and fall
- **Medical significance:** None to minimal — not considered medically
  significant

It is related to the hobo spider and cannot be separated from it by eye. WSU
Extension states that without examining the very small reproductive structures
you cannot definitively identify a hobo spider, which makes reliable separation a
microscope job. Two things are worth knowing anyway. Banded legs work in one
direction only: visible dark rings rule the hobo spider out, because hobo legs
are uniformly colored — but plain legs prove nothing in return, since plenty of
harmless funnel weavers have them. And the underside settles more than the top
does: three or four pairs of pale spots on the sternum means it is not a hobo,
while a pale central stripe there means it might be. For practical purposes both
species are treated the same way: non-dangerous, though alarming.

### Western black widow (*Latrodectus hesperus*) — Washington's medically significant black spider

The western black widow is the spider that gives "black spider in Washington"
its legitimate concern. The Washington State Department of Health describes it
as common to eastern Washington, and that is the honest shape of its
distribution: the range follows the drier, warmer country east and south of the
Cascades, and Bellingham's wet, cool climate is close to the opposite of what it
wants. It is uncommon on this side of the mountains rather than routine. Where
one does appear here, it is in a dry, sheltered, undisturbed location.

How to identify one:

- **Body:** Shiny, jet black — unlike the matte look of giant house spiders
- **Abdomen:** Distinctly globular and ball-shaped
- **Red hourglass:** A red to orange-red hourglass on the **underside** of the
  abdomen — not visible from above
- **Size:** About 1/2 inch body length — much smaller than a giant house spider
- **Web:** Irregular, messy, sticky cobweb — not a funnel web
- **Habitat:** Wood piles, under outdoor furniture, utility boxes, rarely
  disturbed garage corners

The key distinction: if the spider is very large (leg span over 2 inches), it is
almost certainly NOT a black widow, whose body is only about 1/2 inch. If it is
small, shiny black, and globular in a dry sheltered location, treat it as a
potential black widow. Do not handle it — call for identification.

### Hobo spider (*Eratigena agrestis*)

Brown and drab — not truly black, but often called black because of how it looks
in dim light. Builds funnel webs at or near ground level. You will read that the
chevron markings on the abdomen identify one; they do not, and it is worth being
clear about why. Neither WSU Extension's identification factsheet nor UC IPM's
pest note on the species uses the chevrons at all. Both go straight to the
reproductive structures, which means a mature specimen and a microscope.

Hobo spiders spent years being blamed for necrotic skin wounds, and that claim
has not held up. The studies behind it were later called into question, the CDC
removed the hobo spider from its list of venomous spiders, and the Washington
State Department of Health states that hobo spiders are not considered dangerous
as previously thought and that no evidence links their venom to skin necrosis.
They are also less common here than their name recognition suggests: WSU reports
that west of the Cascades the giant house spider outnumbers them about three to
one.

### False black widow (*Steatoda grossa*)

Small, dark purple to black with a rounded abdomen and no hourglass, frequently
mistaken for a true black widow. Non-dangerous, though a bite can cause mild
localized symptoms (uncommon). It builds messy irregular webs in garages and
basements, adding to the confusion. The tells: no hourglass marking, and a
purple-brown rather than jet black color.

### Black ground beetles and harvestmen — not spiders

Many "black spiders" reported by homeowners turn out to be harvestmen (daddy
long legs, a different order altogether), black ground beetles, or other dark
arthropods. Harvestmen have oval bodies, extremely long thin legs, and no waist —
spiders have a distinct waist. They are completely harmless.

## Where do black spiders live in Bellingham homes?

### Giant house spider habitat

They prefer dark, undisturbed spaces for their funnel webs: basement corners,
garages, under stairs, in closets, and behind rarely moved furniture. They are
most visible in September and October when males roam for females, crossing open
floors and ending up in bathtubs and sinks they cannot climb out of.

### Black widow habitat in Whatcom County

Western black widows here are most likely in dry wood piles near structures,
under patio and deck furniture, in meter and utility boxes, in rarely disturbed
garage and shed corners, in dense shrubbery against warm south-facing walls, and
in drier crawl spaces. They are almost never in the main living areas — they seek
undisturbed, dry conditions that active household spaces do not provide. The
garage and outdoor structures are the primary concern.

## What should I do if I find a black spider I can't identify?

- Do not handle it — trap it under a glass or container without touching it
- Observe from a safe distance: shiny or matte? Globular or flat and elongated?
  Smaller than a quarter or larger?
- If small (body under 1/2 inch), shiny black, and globular with a web in a dry
  sheltered location, treat it as a potential black widow and call for
  identification
- If large (leg span over 2 inches), matte brown to black with a funnel web, it
  is almost certainly a harmless giant house spider
- You can bring the spider to us in a sealed container and we will identify it
  at no charge
- If you can safely photograph it, clear shots of the top and underside of the
  abdomen help a lot

## What if I've been bitten by a black spider in Washington?

If you have been bitten and are not certain of the identification:

- Note the time of the bite and photograph or preserve the spider if possible
- Clean the bite site with soap and water
- Apply ice wrapped in cloth to reduce swelling and pain
- Watch how it develops over the next few hours
- Seek immediate medical attention if you develop spreading redness, muscle
  cramping or spasms, nausea, sweating, difficulty breathing, or a rapid heart
  rate — these can indicate black widow envenomation
- Go to an emergency room if any of those whole-body symptoms develop; black
  widow venom acts on the nervous system and can progress

None of this is medical advice, and if you are worried about a bite the right
move is to speak to a health professional or the Washington Poison Center rather
than working from a web page.

Giant house spider bites, if they happen at all, cause mild local pain and
redness that resolves within hours. Hobo spider bites are similar. Black widow
bites warrant medical evaluation regardless of how they look at first.

## Common questions about black spiders in Washington State

### I found a huge black spider in my Bellingham bathroom. Is it a black widow?

Almost certainly not. The giant house spider — by far the most common large dark
spider in Bellingham — regularly ends up in bathtubs and sinks because it falls
in crossing the rim and cannot climb the smooth surface. They look alarming but
are harmless. Black widows are small, about 1/2 inch, and almost never in active
bathroom or kitchen areas. If yours was quarter-sized or larger, it is a giant
house spider. If it is small, shiny black, and sphere-shaped, call for
identification.

### Are black widows common in Bellingham, WA?

No. The Washington State Department of Health describes the western black widow
as common to eastern Washington, and the range follows the drier country east
and south of the Cascades — the wetter, cooler coastal climate here is not the
environment it wants. Uncommon does not mean absent, though, and the places to
be careful are the dry undisturbed ones: wood piles, outdoor furniture, utility
and meter boxes, and dry garage corners. Treat any small, shiny black spider
with a globular abdomen in a spot like that with appropriate caution.

### Are hobo spiders dangerous?

The old assessment has been substantially revised. For years hobo spiders were
described as dangerously venomous and blamed for necrotic wounds, based on
studies that have since been questioned and never replicated. The Washington
State Department of Health now states that they are not considered dangerous as
previously thought and that there is no evidence their venom has caused skin
necrosis in people, and the CDC removed them from its venomous spider list —
the current federal guidance for outdoor workers names only the black widow and
the brown recluse. Bites, where they happen at all, cause mild local symptoms.
If you are bitten by any spider and develop significant symptoms, seek medical
evaluation.

### I see black spiders in my garage every fall. Where are they coming from?

Fall is when giant house spider males leave their retreats to find females,
making them highly visible and mobile. Your garage likely provides year-round
harborage — stable temperature, abundant prey like silverfish and earwigs, and
undisturbed corners for web building. The fall surge is those year-round
residents becoming active. Professional treatment in late summer, plus reducing
clutter and sealing the door-to-house gap, substantially lowers garage spider
activity.

### What do black widow webs look like?

Western black widow webs are irregular, messy, three-dimensional cobwebs — not
organized orb or funnel webs. They are built close to the ground or in sheltered
corners and are notably strong and sticky, with a coarse, irregular look. The
spider retreats to a small tubular shelter at the web's edge. Finding a messy
cobweb like this in a dry sheltered location — under outdoor furniture, in a low
garage corner, in a utility box — is a reason to look carefully before reaching
in.

### My child found a small shiny black spider outside. Should I be concerned?

A small, shiny black spider with a globular abdomen in a dry, sheltered location
warrants cautious attention. Tell your child not to touch it and call for
identification. From a safe distance, look for the red hourglass on the
underside, the jet black shine, and the round abdomen. If you cannot confirm it
is harmless from observation alone, err on the side of caution. A suspected black
widow bite in a child should be assessed by a doctor without delay.
