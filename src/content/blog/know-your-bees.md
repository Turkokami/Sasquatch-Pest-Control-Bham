## Key Identifiers of Carpenter Bees and Bumblebees

**Visual Differences:**

- **Carpenter Bees:** Roughly the same size as a bumblebee, sometimes a little larger, but with a shiny, black or blue-black body and a smooth, hairless abdomen. That bare, glossy rear end is the reliable tell. Their wings can appear iridescent in sunlight.
- **Bumblebees:** Fuzzy from head to tail, with black and yellow stripes. The hair is what makes them such good pollinators, since pollen sticks to it.

Worth knowing before you start looking: carpenter bees are far less common in northwest Washington than they are in warmer parts of the country. A big bee cruising around your yard here is much more often a bumblebee.

**Behavioral Traits:**

- **Carpenter Bees:** Solitary by nature, these bees are not aggressive unless provoked. Female carpenter bees can sting but rarely do so. Males may hover and dart at you and cannot sting at all. They are known for boring into wood to create nests.
- **Bumblebees:** These bees are social creatures living in colonies and can be defensive when their nests are threatened. They are capable of stinging repeatedly, which can be more problematic for those with allergies.

## Understanding Their Impact and How to Manage Them

**Carpenter Bee Challenges:**

Their wood-boring habits can damage wooden structures over time, particularly where the same timber is tunneled year after year. It's important to address potential nesting sites by sealing exposed wood and consulting pest control professionals if you notice recurring activity.

**Bumblebee Encounters:**

Typically nesting underground, bumblebees might be disturbed accidentally. While they are beneficial for pollination, their stings can be painful. Maintaining a tidy yard and managing overgrowth can help minimize unwanted encounters. A bumblebee colony well away from foot traffic is usually best left alone — it dies out on its own by late fall.

## Prevention Tips for Homeowners

- **Seal and Paint Wood:** This discourages carpenter bees from nesting. Bare, weathered softwood is what they look for.
- **Physical Barriers:** Installing nets or screens can prevent access to potential nesting areas.
