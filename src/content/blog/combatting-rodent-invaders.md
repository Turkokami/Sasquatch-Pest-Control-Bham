## Early Signs of a Mouse Infestation

**Detecting the invisible:** Before you ever see a mouse, there are indicators that can alert you to their presence. Look for grease marks or discolored trails on walls and baseboards, which reveal their habitual paths. Listen for the telltale rustling or scratching sounds in the walls, especially at night.

**Unmistakable evidence:** Mouse droppings, which resemble dark grains of rice with pointed ends, are a clear sign of mouse activity. Finding these in your kitchen, basement, or attic is a definite call to action. If your pets are suddenly alert and tracking unseen movements, it's likely they're sensing rodents.

## Entry Points and Prevention

**How mice gain access:** A house mouse can squeeze through a gap about a quarter of an inch across — roughly the width of a pencil. Common entry points include gaps around pipes, cracks in the foundation, and openings near windows or doors. Regularly checking and sealing these vulnerabilities is the single most useful thing you can do to prevent an infestation.

**Securing your home:** Improve your home's defenses by packing gaps with copper mesh or hardware cloth and sealing over them with caulk, and by removing debris or clutter near the building's exterior. Rodents chew straight through caulk, foam, and wood on their own, so the barrier itself needs to be metal. Make sure food sources are sealed and inaccessible to discourage mice from settling in.

## What About Natural Repellents?

**DIY solutions:** Peppermint oil, cayenne pepper, and similar home remedies come up constantly in advice about mice, and they are safe enough to try around a house. What they are not is a control method. There is no good evidence that a scent deterrent moves an established population out of a structure, and relying on one usually means the infestation grows while you wait. Treat them as a minor supplement, never as the plan.

**What actually works:** Sealing the gaps mice use, removing the food and harborage that make your property attractive, and trapping the animals already inside. Those three together resolve mouse problems; scent alone does not.
